\section{Week 17}
\sol{17.1}{Take any point $c$ of the space and the constant sequence $x_n=c$. Every ball $B(c,r)$ contains $x_n$ for every index $n$, so it contains terms at infinitely many indices, yet the sequence takes only one value, $c$. The ball itself may contain many other points of the space, as $B(c,r)$ does on the real line, but only one of them is a value of the sequence.

The proof does not need different values. Lemma~\ref{ch17:lem:pigeonhole} counts indices, not values, and Step~2 needs an infinite set of indices only so that it can always choose a larger index. Repeated values do no harm. For a constant sequence, every subsequence is constant, and a constant sequence converges at once to its value, since every term is at distance $0$ from it.}

\sol{17.2}{Without the requirement $n_k>n_{k-1}$, the chosen indices need not increase, so the terms $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\ldots$ need not form a subsequence. This really can happen. For the constant sequence $x_n=c$, every ball that contains $c$ contains every term, and every other ball contains none. The chosen ball must give an infinite set $S_k$, so it contains $c$, and $S_k=\NN$ for every $k$. The rule ``take the smallest member of $S_k$'' then gives $n_k=0$ for every $k$, and the list $x_0,x_0,x_0,\ldots$ uses one index again and again. It is not a subsequence. The distance estimates of Steps~3 and~4 still hold for the chosen terms, because they use only $n_k\in S_k$; what fails is the claim that these terms form a subsequence, which is what the theorem promises.

The repair: after choosing $n_{k-1}$, choose $n_k$ from the set $S_k\setminus\set{0,1,\ldots,n_{k-1}}$. Any member of this set lies in $S_k$ and is larger than $n_{k-1}$, as required. The set is never empty. If it were, every member of $S_k$ would lie in the finite set $\set{0,1,\ldots,n_{k-1}}$, so $S_k$ would be finite; but $S_k$ is infinite by Step~1.}

\sol{17.3}{(a) Let $x_0,x_1,\ldots$ be a Cauchy sequence in $X$. The Cauchy condition with tolerance $1/2$ supplies a threshold $N$ such that $d(x_m,x_n)<1/2$ for all $m,n\ge N$. Every distance in $X$ is $0$ or $1$, so these distances are all $0$, and $x_n=x_N$ for every $n\ge N$. The sequence converges to the point $x_N$ of $X$: for every $\eps>0$ and every $n\ge N$, $d(x_n,x_N)=0<\eps$. So every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$, and $X$ is complete.

(b) Suppose $X$ is finite, with members $c_1,\ldots,c_k$ (if $X$ is empty, $k=0$ and the empty list covers it). Let $r>0$. Every point $x$ of $X$ is one of the $c_i$, and $d(x,x)=0<r$, so the $k$ balls $B(c_1,r),\ldots,B(c_k,r)$ cover $X$. Hence $X$ is totally bounded. Conversely, suppose $X$ is totally bounded, and take $r=1/2$. Finitely many balls $B(c_1,1/2),\ldots,B(c_k,1/2)$ cover $X$. Each of them contains only its center, because every other point is at distance $1$ from it. So every point of $X$ is one of $c_1,\ldots,c_k$, and $X$ is finite.

(c) Suppose first that there is a point $c$ with $x_n=c$ for infinitely many indices $n$, and let $S$ be the set of these indices. As in Step~2 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:subsequence}, choose any $n_1\in S$ and then, for each $k\ge2$, an index $n_k\in S$ with $n_k>n_{k-1}$. This is always possible, because $S$ is infinite and an infinite set of natural numbers contains a number larger than any given one. The subsequence $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\ldots$ is the constant sequence $c,c,c,\ldots$, which converges to $c$, since every term is at distance $0<\eps$ from $c$ for every $\eps>0$.

Conversely, suppose that a subsequence $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\ldots$ converges to a point $c$ of $X$. For the tolerance $1/2$ there is a threshold $K$ such that $d(x_{n_k},c)<1/2$ for every $k\ge K$. Every distance in $X$ is $0$ or $1$, so $x_{n_k}=c$ for every $k\ge K$. The indices $n_K<n_{K+1}<n_{K+2}<\cdots$ are infinitely many different natural numbers, and the sequence takes the value $c$ at each of them.

Now take $X=\NN$. By (a) and (b), it is complete but not totally bounded. The sequence $x_n=n$ takes each value at only one index, so by what we just proved, no subsequence of it converges, although $X$ is complete. Completeness promises a limit only for a sequence that is already a Cauchy sequence; it says nothing about whether an arbitrary sequence has a subsequence of that kind. Producing such a subsequence is the job that total boundedness does in Theorem~\ref{thm:subsequence}.}

\sol{17.4}{Fix $r>0$. Since $b-a>0$, the Archimedean property gives a positive integer $N$ with $N>(b-a)/r$. Multiplying by $r/N$ shows that the spacing $h=(b-a)/N$ satisfies $0<h<r$. Use the $N+1$ centers
\[
 c_k=a+kh,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,N.
\]
The first center is $c_0=a$, the last is $c_N=a+(b-a)=b$, and the others lie between them, so all of them are points of $[a,b]$, as the definition requires.

Let $x\in[a,b]$. If $x=b$, then $x$ is the center $c_N$, and $d(x,c_N)=0<r$. Otherwise $a\le x<b$. Let $k$ be the largest index with $c_k\le x$; there is one, because $c_0=a\le x$. Then $k<N$, because $c_N=b>x$, and $x<c_{k+1}$. Hence
\[
 d(x,c_k)=x-c_k<c_{k+1}-c_k=h<r,
\]
so $x$ lies in the ball of radius $r$ about $c_k$. Thus the $N+1$ balls of radius $r$ cover $[a,b]$.

The number $N+1$ of balls, and the positions of the centers, depend on the radius $r$ and on the interval, through its endpoints $a$ and $b$. They do not depend on the point $x$: the list of centers was chosen as soon as $r$ was given, before any point $x$ was considered, and that one list covers every $x\in[a,b]$.}

\sol{17.5}{The out-neighbors of $v$ are $u$ and $w$, so $N_1^+(v)=\set{u,w}$. The only route of length two from $v$ is $v\to u\to w$, because $w$ has no out-neighbors. So $R_2(v)=\set w$. But $w$ is already a first out-neighbor of $v$, because of the edge $v\to w$, so it is excluded from the second out-neighborhood, and $N_2^+(v)=\varnothing$. The three sets have $2$, $0$ and $1$ members.

In every directed graph, $N_2^+(v)$ is obtained from $R_2(v)$ by removing $v$ and the first out-neighbors of $v$. So $N_2^+(v)\subseteq R_2(v)$, and $|R_2(v)|\ge|N_2^+(v)|$. A version of the conjecture that asked for a vertex with $|R_2(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$ would therefore be easier to satisfy: every vertex that satisfies Seymour's inequality also satisfies the new one, but not conversely. To see that the truth of the inequality can really change, add a fourth vertex $z$ and the edge $w\to z$. The graph is still oriented. Now $N_1^+(v)=\set{u,w}$, $R_2(v)=\set{w,z}$ and $N_2^+(v)=\set z$. At $v$ the inequality $|R_2(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$ holds, since $2\ge2$, while Seymour's inequality $|N_2^+(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$ fails, since $1<2$.

So a formal statement that used $R_2(v)$ would state a weaker claim than Seymour's conjecture, and a proof of it would not prove the conjecture. In fact the weaker claim has a short proof, by an extremal choice in the style of Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. In a nonempty finite directed graph, choose a vertex $v$ whose outdegree $\delta=|N_1^+(v)|$, the number of its out-neighbors, is as small as possible; such a vertex exists because the outdegrees form a nonempty finite set of natural numbers. If $\delta=0$, then $v$ has no out-neighbors and no routes of length two, so $|R_2(v)|=0\ge0=|N_1^+(v)|$. If $\delta\ge1$, pick an out-neighbor $u$ of $v$. Every out-neighbor $z$ of $u$ is reached by the route $v\to u\to z$, so $N_1^+(u)\subseteq R_2(v)$. The outdegree of $u$ is at least the smallest outdegree $\delta$, and therefore
\[
 |R_2(v)|\ge|N_1^+(u)|\ge\delta=|N_1^+(v)|.
\]
A few lines settle the altered statement, while Seymour's conjecture stayed open for more than thirty years. In an oriented graph $v$ itself never lies in $R_2(v)$, so all of the difficulty lies in excluding the first out-neighbors, which is what turns $R_2(v)$ into $N_2^+(v)$. The replacement changes what the statement means; it is not a harmless simplification of the notation.}

\sol{17.6}{Each request should say what the reader knows, name one particular proof, and ask only for what that proof needs. For the contraction theorem, written by a reader who has met metric spaces but not yet the rest of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}, a suitable request is: ``I know metric spaces, the triangle inequality and proof by induction. I want to read the proof of the contraction theorem that starts from any point, applies the map again and again, shows that the iterates form a Cauchy sequence, and takes their limit. Teach me convergence of a sequence, Cauchy sequences, completeness and the bound for a sum of geometrically shrinking steps, only as far as this proof needs them. After each concept, show me the step of the proof that uses it.'' For Theorem~\ref{thm:subsequence}, written by a reader who knows all of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}, a suitable request is: ``I know metric spaces, Cauchy sequences and completeness. I want to read the proof that every sequence in a complete, totally bounded metric space has a convergent subsequence by keeping infinitely many terms inside smaller and smaller balls. Teach me finite covers by small balls, the infinite pigeonhole principle and subsequences, only as far as this proof needs them. After each concept, show me the step of the proof that uses it.''

Both proofs use convergence, Cauchy sequences and completeness. Only the contraction proof needs fixed points, the contraction factor $q$ and the geometric-sum bound of Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:geometric-cauchy}. Only the subsequence proof needs balls and total boundedness, the infinite pigeonhole principle, and subsequences. So each request asks for something that the other proof does not need.
