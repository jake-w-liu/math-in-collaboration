\section{Week 17}
\sol{17.1}{Take any point $c$ of the space and the constant sequence $x_n=c$. Every ball $B(c,r)$ contains $x_n$ for every index $n$, yet the sequence takes only one value, $c$, however many other points the ball contains.

The proof does not need different values. Lemma~\ref{ch17:lem:pigeonhole} counts indices, not values, and Step~2 needs infinitely many indices only to choose ever larger ones. For a constant sequence, every subsequence is constant and converges at once to its value.}

\sol{17.2}{Without the requirement $n_k>n_{k-1}$, the chosen indices need not increase, so the terms $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\ldots$ need not form a subsequence. For example, for a constant sequence $x_n=c$, each chosen ball must contain $c$ to give an infinite set $S_k$ (a ball without $c$ contains no term), and then $S_k=\NN$ for every $k$. The rule ``take the smallest member of $S_k$'' then gives $n_k=0$ for every $k$, and $x_0,x_0,x_0,\ldots$ is not a subsequence. The distance estimates of Steps~3 and~4 still hold, because they use only $n_k\in S_k$; what fails is the subsequence that the theorem promises.

The repair: after choosing $n_{k-1}$, choose $n_k$ from $S_k\setminus\set{0,1,\ldots,n_{k-1}}$, whose members lie in $S_k$ and are larger than $n_{k-1}$. This set is never empty: otherwise $S_k$ would lie inside the finite set $\set{0,1,\ldots,n_{k-1}}$ and be finite, but $S_k$ is infinite by Step~1.}

\sol{17.3}{(a) The argument is the one given for $\NN$ in Section~\ref{ch17:sec:counterexamples}, which uses only that every distance is $0$ or $1$. For a Cauchy sequence in $X$, the tolerance $1/2$ supplies a threshold $N$ with $d(x_m,x_n)<1/2$, and hence $d(x_m,x_n)=0$, for all $m,n\ge N$. So $x_n=x_N$ for every $n\ge N$, and the sequence converges to the point $x_N$ of $X$: for every $\eps>0$ and $n\ge N$, $d(x_n,x_N)=0<\eps$.

(b) Suppose $X$ is finite, with members $c_1,\ldots,c_k$ (if $X$ is empty, $k=0$ and the empty list covers it). For every $r>0$, each point $x$ of $X$ is one of the $c_i$, and $d(x,x)=0<r$, so the balls $B(c_1,r),\ldots,B(c_k,r)$ cover $X$, and $X$ is totally bounded. Conversely, suppose $X$ is totally bounded, and take $r=1/2$. Finitely many balls $B(c_1,1/2),\ldots,B(c_k,1/2)$ cover $X$, and each contains only its center, because every other point is at distance $1$ from it. So $X=\set{c_1,\ldots,c_k}$ is finite.

(c) Suppose first that $x_n=c$ for every $n$ in an infinite set $S$ of indices. As in Step~2 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:subsequence}, choose $n_1<n_2<\cdots$ in $S$, which is possible because $S$ is infinite. The subsequence $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\ldots$ is the constant sequence $c,c,c,\ldots$, which converges to $c$.

Conversely, suppose that a subsequence $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},\ldots$ converges to a point $c$ of $X$. For the tolerance $1/2$ there is a threshold $K$ with $d(x_{n_k},c)<1/2$, and hence $x_{n_k}=c$, for every $k\ge K$. The indices $n_K<n_{K+1}<n_{K+2}<\cdots$ are infinitely many natural numbers, and the sequence takes the value $c$ at each of them.

Now take $X=\NN$. By (a) and (b), it is complete but not totally bounded. The sequence $x_n=n$ takes each value at only one index, so by (c) no subsequence of it converges, although $X$ is complete. Completeness gives a limit only to a sequence that is already a Cauchy sequence; producing a Cauchy subsequence is the job that total boundedness does in Theorem~\ref{thm:subsequence}.}

\sol{17.4}{Fix $r>0$. Since $b-a>0$, the Archimedean property gives a positive integer $N$ with $N>(b-a)/r$, so the spacing $h=(b-a)/N$ satisfies $0<h<r$. Use the $N+1$ centers
\[
 c_k=a+kh,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,N.
\]
They run from $c_0=a$ to $c_N=b$, so all of them are points of $[a,b]$, as the definition requires.

Let $x\in[a,b]$. If $x=b$, then $x$ is the center $c_N$. Otherwise $a\le x<b$. Let $k$ be the largest index with $c_k\le x$; there is one, because $c_0=a\le x$. Then $k<N$, because $c_N=b>x$, and $x<c_{k+1}$. Hence
\[
 d(x,c_k)=x-c_k<c_{k+1}-c_k=h<r,
\]
so $x$ lies in the ball of radius $r$ about $c_k$. Thus the $N+1$ balls of radius $r$ cover $[a,b]$.

The number $N+1$ of balls, and the centers, depend on the radius $r$ and on the endpoints $a$ and $b$, but not on the point $x$: the list was chosen as soon as $r$ was given, before any $x$ was considered, and that one list covers every $x\in[a,b]$.}

\sol{17.5}{The out-neighbors of $v$ are $u$ and $w$, so $N_1^+(v)=\set{u,w}$. The only route of length two from $v$ is $v\to u\to w$, because $w$ has no out-neighbors, so $R_2(v)=\set w$. But $w$ is a first out-neighbor of $v$, because of the edge $v\to w$, so it is excluded from the second out-neighborhood, and $N_2^+(v)=\varnothing$.

In every directed graph, $N_2^+(v)$ is $R_2(v)$ with $v$ and the first out-neighbors of $v$ removed, so $|R_2(v)|\ge|N_2^+(v)|$. A statement asking for a vertex with $|R_2(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$ would therefore be easier to satisfy, and the difference is real. Add a fourth vertex $z$ and the edge $w\to z$; the graph is still oriented. Now $N_1^+(v)=\set{u,w}$, $R_2(v)=\set{w,z}$ and $N_2^+(v)=\set z$, so at $v$ the altered inequality holds ($2\ge2$) while Seymour's fails ($1<2$).

So a formal statement using $R_2(v)$ would state a weaker claim, and a proof of it would not prove Seymour's conjecture. In fact the weaker claim has a short proof by an extremal choice, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. In a nonempty finite directed graph, choose a vertex $v$ of smallest outdegree $\delta=|N_1^+(v)|$. If $\delta=0$, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise pick an out-neighbor $u$ of $v$. Every out-neighbor $z$ of $u$ is reached by the route $v\to u\to z$, so $N_1^+(u)\subseteq R_2(v)$, and $u$ has outdegree at least $\delta$; hence
\[
 |R_2(v)|\ge|N_1^+(u)|\ge\delta=|N_1^+(v)|.
\]
A few lines settle the altered statement, while Seymour's conjecture stayed open for more than thirty years. In an oriented graph $v$ itself never lies in $R_2(v)$, so all of the difficulty lies in excluding the first out-neighbors. The replacement changes what the statement means; it is not a harmless simplification of notation.}
