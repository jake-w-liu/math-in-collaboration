\sol{14.1}{Write $a=(a_0,a_1,a_2)=(1,1,-1)$, so $n=3$. The aperiodic correlations are
\[
 A_1=a_0a_1+a_1a_2=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)=0,\qquad A_2=a_0a_2=1\cdot(-1)=-1.
\]
For the periodic correlations, the positions wrap around modulo $3$. At shift one the last term is $a_2a_{3\bmod3}=a_2a_0$, and at shift two the last two terms are $a_1a_{3\bmod3}=a_1a_0$ and $a_2a_{4\bmod3}=a_2a_1$. So
\begin{align*}
 C_1&=a_0a_1+a_1a_2+a_2a_0=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)+(-1)\cdot1=-1,\\
 C_2&=a_0a_2+a_1a_0+a_2a_1=1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot1=-1.
\end{align*}
Equation~\eqref{eq:periodaperiod} predicts $C_1=A_1+A_2=0+(-1)=-1$ and $C_2=A_2+A_1=-1$, which agrees. Since $|A_1|=0$ and $|A_2|=1$ are both at most one, $(1,1,-1)$ is a Barker sequence. Its periodic correlations at nonzero shifts are not zero, as expected: for an odd length, no periodic correlation can be zero (Section~\ref{ch14:sec:periodic}).}
\sol{14.2}{Every term of a correlation is a product of two entries of the sequence, and $(-x)(-y)=xy$. For $1\le s\le n-1$,
\[
 A_s(-a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}(-a_j)(-a_{j+s})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}a_ja_{j+s}=A_s(a).
\]
For $0\le s\le n-1$, in the same way,
\[
 C_s(-a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-a_j)(-a_{(j+s)\bmod n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_ja_{(j+s)\bmod n}=C_s(a).
\]
Consequently, $a$ is a Barker sequence exactly when $-a$ is, and $a$ has $C_s(a)=0$ for all $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$ exactly when $-a$ has this property. This is why every count in the table of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:experiment} is even.}
\sol{14.3}{The rows of $H$ are
\[
 \text{row }0=(1,1,1,-1),\quad \text{row }1=(-1,1,1,1),\quad \text{row }2=(1,-1,1,1),\quad \text{row }3=(1,1,-1,1).
\]
The entry in position $(i,\ell)$ of $HH^{\mathsf T}$ is the dot product of row $i$ with row $\ell$. So
\begin{align*}
 (HH^{\mathsf T})_{02}&=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot1=1-1+1-1=0,\\
 (HH^{\mathsf T})_{13}&=(-1)\cdot1+1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1=-1+1-1+1=0,\\
 (HH^{\mathsf T})_{22}&=1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1+1\cdot1=4.
\end{align*}
These agree with $HH^{\mathsf T}=4I$, which has $0$ off the diagonal and $4$ on it. By Proposition~\ref{ch14:prop:rows}(a), the entry in position $(0,2)$ equals $C_{(0-2)\bmod4}=C_2$, and the entry in position $(1,3)$ equals $C_{(1-3)\bmod4}=C_2$ as well. Both agree with the value $C_2=0$ computed in Section~\ref{ch14:sec:periodic}.}
\sol{14.4}{The definition of $C_s$ requires $0\le s\le n-1$, and the definition of $C_{n-s}$ requires $0\le n-s\le n-1$, that is, $1\le s\le n$. Both sides are therefore defined exactly when $1\le s\le n-1$. When $n=1$ there is no such $s$, and there is nothing to prove.

Let $1\le s\le n-1$. By definition,
\[
 C_{n-s}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j+n-s)\bmod n}.
\]
The integers $j+n-s$ and $j-s$ differ by $n$, so they have the same remainder on division by $n$, and $(j+n-s)\bmod n=(j-s)\bmod n$. Hence
\[
 C_{n-s}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j-s)\bmod n}.
\]
Now set $k=(j-s)\bmod n$. As $j$ runs through $0,1,\ldots,n-1$, so does $k$, each value exactly once, because $j\mapsto(j-s)\bmod n$ is a bijection of $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$ (Section~\ref{ch14:sec:circulant}). Moreover $j\equiv k+s\pmod n$, and $j$ lies between $0$ and $n-1$, so $j=(k+s)\bmod n$. Replacing both occurrences of the old index,
\begin{align*}
 C_{n-s}
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{(k+s)\bmod n}\,a_k\\
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k\, a_{(k+s)\bmod n}\\
 &=C_s.
\end{align*}
The first equality lists the same terms in a different order, the second swaps the two factors in each product, and the last is the definition of $C_s$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:periodaperiod} gives a second proof. It says $C_s=A_s+A_{n-s}$, and applied to the shift $n-s$ it says $C_{n-s}=A_{n-s}+A_{n-(n-s)}=A_{n-s}+A_s$. The two right sides are equal. For example, the sequence $(1,1,-1)$ of Exercise~\ref{ex:14.1} has $C_1=C_2=-1$.}
\sol{14.5}{By Section~\ref{ch14:sec:square}, the order must be a perfect square. By Lemma~\ref{thm:hadamardorder}, an order greater than $2$ must be divisible by four.
\begin{itemize}
\item $8$ is not a perfect square, since $2^2=4<8<9=3^2$. It is ruled out.
\item $9=3^2$ and $25=5^2$ are perfect squares, but neither is divisible by four. Both are ruled out. (Both are also odd, so the parity argument of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:periodic} rules them out as well.)
\item $16=4^2=4\cdot2^2$ and $36=6^2=4\cdot3^2$ satisfy both conditions. They remain possible orders, of the form $4u^2$ with $u=2$ and $u=3$.
\end{itemize}
The answer does not show that a circulant Hadamard matrix of order $16$ or $36$ exists. Necessary conditions can only rule orders out. Passing both tests means only that these two tests cannot exclude $16$ and $36$; neither test builds a matrix. In fact, the research claim described in Section~\ref{ch14:sec:research} asserts that there is no circulant Hadamard matrix of either order. Notice also that only the divisibility condition applies to all Hadamard matrices; the square condition needs the circulant pattern. There are Hadamard matrices of order $8$, for example, but none of them is circulant.}
\sol{14.6}{\emph{Existence.} Suppose the search finds a sequence $a$ of length $n$ with $C_s(a)=0$ for every $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$. By Proposition~\ref{ch14:prop:rows}(b), the circulant matrix of $a$ is a circulant Hadamard matrix of order $n$. This conclusion does not depend on trusting the search: anyone can recompute the $n-1$ correlations of this one sequence, by hand or with an independent program. The sequence is a \emph{certificate} of existence that can be checked on its own.

\emph{Nonexistence at one length.} Suppose instead that the search examines every one of the $2^n$ sequences correctly and finds none that qualifies. The first row of a circulant Hadamard matrix of order $n$ would be one of the sequences examined, so no such matrix exists. This conclusion has no short certificate. To accept it, we must trust that the program examined every case and computed every correlation correctly, or run an independent search ourselves.

\emph{Only that length.} The search examined sequences of length $n$ and no others, so it says nothing about any other length. The table of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:experiment}, for instance, says nothing about length $16$.

\emph{All orders greater than $4$.} There are infinitely many such orders, so no finite search can cover them. What is needed is a proof that works for an arbitrary order $n$. The proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} is an argument of this kind, since it applies to every $n$ at once, but it is not strong enough: it still leaves the orders $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$ open. Alternatively, one could prove a reduction showing that any example, if one existed, would have to appear among finitely many cases, and then search all of those cases.}
