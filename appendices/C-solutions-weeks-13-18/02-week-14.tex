\sol{14.1}{Write $a=(a_0,a_1,a_2)=(1,1,-1)$, so $n=3$. The aperiodic correlations are
\[
 A_1=a_0a_1+a_1a_2=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)=0,\qquad A_2=a_0a_2=1\cdot(-1)=-1.
\]
For the periodic correlations, the positions wrap around modulo $3$ (for instance $3\bmod3=0$ and $4\bmod3=1$):
\begin{align*}
 C_1&=a_0a_1+a_1a_2+a_2a_0=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)+(-1)\cdot1=-1,\\
 C_2&=a_0a_2+a_1a_0+a_2a_1=1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot1=-1.
\end{align*}
Equation~\eqref{eq:periodaperiod} predicts $C_1=A_1+A_2=-1$ and $C_2=A_2+A_1=-1$, which agrees. Since $|A_1|=0$ and $|A_2|=1$ are at most one, $(1,1,-1)$ is a Barker sequence.}
\sol{14.2}{Every term of a correlation is a product of two entries, and $(-x)(-y)=xy$. For $1\le s\le n-1$,
\[
 A_s(-a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}(-a_j)(-a_{j+s})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}a_ja_{j+s}=A_s(a),
\]
and for $0\le s\le n-1$, in the same way,
\[
 C_s(-a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-a_j)(-a_{(j+s)\bmod n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_ja_{(j+s)\bmod n}=C_s(a).
\]}
\sol{14.3}{The rows of $H$ are
\[
 \text{row }0=(1,1,1,-1),\quad \text{row }1=(-1,1,1,1),\quad \text{row }2=(1,-1,1,1),\quad \text{row }3=(1,1,-1,1).
\]
The entry in position $(i,\ell)$ of $HH^{\mathsf T}$ is the dot product of row $i$ with row $\ell$:
\begin{align*}
 (HH^{\mathsf T})_{02}&=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot1=1-1+1-1=0,\\
 (HH^{\mathsf T})_{13}&=(-1)\cdot1+1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1=-1+1-1+1=0,\\
 (HH^{\mathsf T})_{22}&=1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1+1\cdot1=4.
\end{align*}
These agree with $HH^{\mathsf T}=4I$, which has $4$ on the diagonal and $0$ elsewhere. By Proposition~\ref{ch14:prop:rows}(a), the entries in positions $(0,2)$ and $(1,3)$ equal $C_{(0-2)\bmod4}=C_2$ and $C_{(1-3)\bmod4}=C_2$, in agreement with the value $C_2=0$ found in Section~\ref{ch14:sec:periodic}.}
\sol{14.4}{The definition of $C_s$ requires $0\le s\le n-1$, and that of $C_{n-s}$ requires $0\le n-s\le n-1$, that is, $1\le s\le n$. So both sides are defined exactly when $1\le s\le n-1$ (for $n=1$, never).

Let $1\le s\le n-1$. By definition,
\[
 C_{n-s}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j+n-s)\bmod n}.
\]
The integers $j+n-s$ and $j-s$ differ by $n$, so they have the same remainder on division by $n$. Hence
\[
 C_{n-s}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j-s)\bmod n}.
\]
Now set $k=(j-s)\bmod n$. As $j$ runs through $0,1,\ldots,n-1$, so does $k$, each value exactly once, because $j\mapsto(j-s)\bmod n$ is a bijection of $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$ (Section~\ref{ch14:sec:circulant}). Moreover $j\equiv k+s\pmod n$, and $j$ lies between $0$ and $n-1$, so $j=(k+s)\bmod n$. Replacing both occurrences of the old index,
\begin{align*}
 C_{n-s}
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{(k+s)\bmod n}\,a_k\\
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k\, a_{(k+s)\bmod n}\\
 &=C_s.
\end{align*}
The first equality lists the same terms in a different order, the second swaps the two factors in each product, and the last is the definition of $C_s$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:periodaperiod} gives a second proof: it says $C_s=A_s+A_{n-s}$, and applied to the shift $n-s$ it says $C_{n-s}=A_{n-s}+A_{n-(n-s)}=A_{n-s}+A_s$.}
\sol{14.5}{By Section~\ref{ch14:sec:square}, the order must be a perfect square, and by Lemma~\ref{thm:hadamardorder}, an order greater than $2$ must be divisible by four.
\begin{itemize}
\item $8$ is not a perfect square: for an integer $r$, if $|r|\le2$ then $r^2\le4$, and if $|r|\ge3$ then $r^2\ge9$. So $8$ is ruled out.
\item $9=3^2$ and $25=5^2$ are perfect squares, but neither is divisible by four, so both are ruled out.
\item $16=4\cdot2^2$ and $36=4\cdot3^2$ satisfy both conditions. They remain possible orders, of the form $4u^2$ with $u=2$ and $u=3$.
\end{itemize}
The answer does not show that a circulant Hadamard matrix of order $16$ or $36$ exists: necessary conditions can only rule orders out. In fact, both orders were ruled out long ago by the stronger restrictions mentioned in Section~\ref{ch14:sec:research}. You can even settle order $16$ yourself: extending the search of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:experiment} to the $2^{16}=65536$ sequences of length $16$ finds none with $C_s=0$ for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le15$.

Only the divisibility condition applies to all Hadamard matrices; the square condition needs the circulant pattern. For example, Hadamard matrices of order $8$ exist, although none is circulant, since $8$ is not a perfect square. Write $h_0,h_1,h_2,h_3$ for the rows of the matrix $H$ of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:circulant}, and form the $8$-by-$8$ matrix with the rows
\[
(h_0,h_0),\ (h_1,h_1),\ (h_2,h_2),\ (h_3,h_3),\ (h_0,-h_0),\ (h_1,-h_1),\ (h_2,-h_2),\ (h_3,-h_3),
\]
where $(h_i,\pm h_i)$ means row $h_i$ followed by $\pm h_i$. The dot product of two of these rows is the dot product of their first halves plus that of their second halves. Since different rows of $H$ are orthogonal, for $i\ne\ell$
\[
(h_i,h_i)\cdot(h_\ell,h_\ell)=(h_i,-h_i)\cdot(h_\ell,-h_\ell)=2\,h_i\cdot h_\ell=0,
\]
and for all $i$ and $\ell$, including $i=\ell$,
\[
(h_i,h_i)\cdot(h_\ell,-h_\ell)=h_i\cdot h_\ell-h_i\cdot h_\ell=0.
\]
So different rows are orthogonal, and by Section~\ref{ch14:sec:circulant} this matrix with entries $\pm1$ is a Hadamard matrix of order $8$.}
\sol{14.6}{\emph{Existence.} Suppose the search finds a sequence $a$ of length $n$ with $C_s(a)=0$ for every $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$. By Proposition~\ref{ch14:prop:rows}(b), the circulant matrix of $a$ is a circulant Hadamard matrix of order $n$. This does not depend on trusting the search: the sequence is a \emph{certificate} of existence, whose $n-1$ correlations anyone can recompute, by hand or with an independent program.

\emph{Nonexistence at one length.} Suppose instead that the search correctly examines all $2^n$ sequences and finds none that qualifies. The first row of a circulant Hadamard matrix of order $n$ would be one of them, so no such matrix exists. This conclusion has no short certificate: to accept it, we must trust that the program examined every case and computed every correlation correctly, or run an independent search ourselves. And since only sequences of length $n$ were examined, it says nothing about any other length.

\emph{All orders greater than $4$.} There are infinitely many such orders, so no finite search can cover them; what is needed is a proof that works for an arbitrary order $n$. The proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} is of this kind, but not strong enough: it leaves the orders $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$ open. Alternatively, one could prove that any example, if one existed, would have to appear among finitely many cases, and then search all of those cases.}
