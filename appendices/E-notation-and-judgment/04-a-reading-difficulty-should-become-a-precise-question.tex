\section{Turning a reading difficulty into a precise question}\label{appE:sec:question}
Suppose you understand what a theorem says but cannot follow one line of its proof. Asking an assistant, or a classmate, to explain the whole proof again rarely helps: the new explanation may skip the same step, or replace it by a different argument that you then have to check from the beginning. It works better to turn the difficulty into a precise question, as Habit~4 (Section~\ref{ch04:sec:promises}) suggests. Write down three things:
\begin{itemize}
\item the exact line that you do not follow;
\item what is known at that point of the proof: the hypotheses, and the facts already established;
\item the last step that you do understand.
\end{itemize}
Then ask which definition or earlier fact leads from what is known to the line. A good answer is usually short: one calculation written out in more detail, or one small example. It is rarely a new technique.

Here are two questions of this kind, one from each half of the course.
\begin{itemize}
\item From Case~1 of the inductive proof of Hall's theorem (Section~\ref{ch09:sec:inductive}): ``I know that the task sets $N(S)$ and $N(T)$ can overlap. Why is $|N(S)\cup N(T)|=|N(S)|+|N(T)\setminus N(S)|$?'' The answer is a splitting into two sets with no common member. Every task in the union lies either in $N(S)$ or in $N(T)\setminus N(S)$, never in both, and the sizes of two disjoint finite sets add (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets}). Nothing more about graphs is needed. Exercise~\ref{ex:9.3} asks you to write this out in full.
\item From the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:inexact} (Section~\ref{ch18:sec:onestep}): ``I see that each update error is later multiplied by $q$. Why is the weight of $\eps_j$ exactly $q^{n-1-j}$?'' The answer is a count. The error $\eps_j$ is made in the update that produces $y_{j+1}$. After it come the updates that produce $y_{j+2},\ldots,y_n$, and there are $n-(j+1)=n-1-j$ of them, each contributing one factor $q$.
\end{itemize}

Once you have the answer, go back to the proof and write the step in your own words. Then change one small feature and work out what changes. In the Hall example, what if the group $T$ were allowed to share students with $S$, or even to equal $S$? The identity for $|N(S)\cup N(T)|$ still holds; when $T=S$ it simply reads $|N(S)|=|N(S)|+0$. What fails is a different step of the proof, the equation $|S\cup T|=|S|+|T|$, which needs $S$ and $T$ to be disjoint. Without it, Hall's condition for $S\cup T$ gives a weaker inequality, and the conclusion $|N(T)\setminus N(S)|\ge|T|$ no longer follows.

In the second example, what if the update errors were numbered from $1$ instead of $0$, so that $\eps_j$ bounds the error in the update that produces $y_j$? Then $n-j$ updates follow it, and its weight becomes $q^{n-j}$. And what if $q$ were equal to $1$? Then every weight is $1$, nothing shrinks, and the bound $e_0+\eps_0+\cdots+\eps_{n-1}$ can grow without limit. If you can answer variations like these, the explanation has become your own: you can reason with it, and not only repeat the words in which it was given.

The same approach works for a definition that you do not understand. Ask, in this order: What kind of object does the definition apply to? What exactly must such an object satisfy? What is one example, and what is a \emph{near miss}, an object that fails only one of the requirements? For the definition of an odd integer (Definition~\ref{def:parity}), the objects are integers, and an integer $a$ is odd when $a=2k+1$ for some integer $k$. The number $7=2\cdot3+1$ is an example. The number $8$ is a near miss. The equation $8=2\cdot3.5+1$ has exactly the right shape, but $3.5$ is not an integer, and no integer $k$ satisfies $2k+1=8$; so the requirement that $k$ be an integer is the one that fails. Finally, when the definition appears in a proof, decide in which direction it is being used. Either the proof is \emph{given} an object that satisfies the definition and unpacks the information that this provides, or it must \emph{show} that a new object satisfies the definition. The first proof of the book, Proposition~\ref{thm:odd-sum}, does both: it unpacks the fact that $a$ and $b$ are odd, and it shows that $a+b$ meets the definition of even (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:definition}).
