\section{Week 11}
\sol{11.1}{Regroup the difference as $(2x-y)-(2a-b)=2(x-a)-(y-b)$. The triangle inequality and the rule $|cx|=|c|\,|x|$ from Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} give
\begin{align*}
 |(2x-y)-(2a-b)|
 &\le|2(x-a)|+|{-(y-b)}|\\
 &=2|x-a|+|y-b|\\
 &\le2(0.02)+0.03=0.07.
\end{align*}
The bound $0.07$ is attained: $a=b=0$, $x=0.02$ and $y=-0.03$ satisfy both hypotheses and give $(2x-y)-(2a-b)=0.04+0.03=0.07$. Because the second error is subtracted, the worst case comes from errors of opposite signs. Since $0.07$ is attained, no smaller bound holds in every case.}
\sol{11.2}{Let $\eps>0$. By the Archimedean property, choose an integer $N>3/\eps$; it is positive because $3/\eps>0$. Let $n\ge N$. Then $n+1>N>0$, so $1/(n+1)<1/N$, and multiplying by $3$ gives $3/(n+1)<3/N$. Multiplying $N>3/\eps$ by the positive number $\eps/N$ gives $\eps>3/N$. Together,
\[
 |a_n-0|=\frac3{n+1}<\frac3N<\eps\qquad\text{for every }n\ge N.
\]
So the threshold is any integer $N>3/\eps$, and $a_n\to0$. For example, $\eps=0.01$ calls for $N>300$, so $N=301$ works; indeed $a_{301}=3/302\approx0.00993$.}
\sol{11.3}{Recall that $d_\infty(x,y)$ is the largest of the $n$ numbers $|x_1-y_1|,\ldots,|x_n-y_n|$.

\emph{Nonnegativity.} Each of these numbers is an absolute value and so is at least $0$; their largest is one of them, so $d_\infty(x,y)\ge0$.

\emph{Zero exactly for equal vectors.} If $x=y$, all $n$ numbers are $0$, and so is their largest. Conversely, suppose $d_\infty(x,y)=0$. Every number $|x_j-y_j|$ is at most the largest one, which is $0$, and at least $0$, so $|x_j-y_j|=0$, that is, $x_j=y_j$, for every $j$. Hence $x=y$.

\emph{Symmetry.} For each $j$, $|x_j-y_j|=|y_j-x_j|$, because $y_j-x_j=-(x_j-y_j)$. So $d_\infty(x,y)$ and $d_\infty(y,x)$ are the largest of the same list of numbers, and they are equal.}
\sol{11.4}{The hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:geometric-cauchy} holds with $D=2$ and $q=1/3$, so for all $m>n$
\[
 d(x_m,x_n)\le\frac{2(1/3)^n}{1-1/3}=3\Bigl(\frac13\Bigr)^n=3^{1-n},
\]
a bound that does not depend on $m$.

For an explicit threshold, we need to know when $3^{1-n}$ becomes small. With $q=1/3$ we have $c=1/q-1=2$, and Bernoulli's inequality gives $3^n\ge1+2n$, so $3^{1-n}\le3/(1+2n)$. Let $\eps>0$, and choose an integer $N>3/(2\eps)$. For $n\ge N$ we get $1+2n>2n\ge2N>3/\eps$, hence $3^{1-n}\le3/(1+2n)<\eps$. Now take any $m,n\ge N$. If $m=n$, their distance is $0<\eps$. Otherwise, since $d$ is symmetric, we may name them so that $m>n$; then $d(x_m,x_n)\le3^{1-n}<\eps$. So the sequence is Cauchy, with threshold any integer $N>3/(2\eps)$. (To conclude that it converges, we would also need the space to be complete.)}
\sol{11.5}{Take $x_n=2+2^{-(n+1)}$ for $n\in\NN$, that is, $2.5,\ 2.25,\ 2.125,\ldots$. Since $0<2^{-(n+1)}\le1/2$, every term satisfies $2<x_n\le2.5$, so the sequence lies in $(2,3)$.

\emph{The sequence is Cauchy.} On the real line it converges to $2$. Indeed, $|x_n-2|=2^{-(n+1)}$, and $2^{n+1}\ge n+2$ (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence}), so $|x_n-2|\le1/(n+2)<1/(n+1)$. Given $\eps>0$, choose an integer $N>1/\eps$; then every $n\ge N$ satisfies $|x_n-2|<1/(n+1)<1/N<\eps$. By Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:convergent-cauchy}, the sequence is Cauchy on the real line. Distances between its terms are the same in $(2,3)$ as in $\RR$, so it is Cauchy in $(2,3)$.

\emph{The sequence has no limit in $(2,3)$.} A limit $L\in(2,3)$ would also be a limit on the real line, since distances in $(2,3)$ are distances in $\RR$. As the sequence converges to $2$ there, Proposition~\ref{thm:limitunique} would force $L=2$, which is not in $(2,3)$. In particular, $(2,3)$ is not complete.}
\sol{11.6}{\emph{1. The supremum.} Every $x\in(0,1)$ satisfies $x<1$, so $1$ is an upper bound for $(0,1)$. Now let $b$ be any upper bound, and suppose $b<1$. Since $1/2\in(0,1)$, we have $1/2\le b$, so $b>0$. The midpoint $t=(b+1)/2$ of $b$ and $1$ satisfies $0<b<t<1$, so $t\in(0,1)$ and $t>b$. Then $b$ is not an upper bound, a contradiction. Hence every upper bound $b$ satisfies $b\ge1$, and $1$ is a supremum of $(0,1)$.

\emph{2. No maximum.} A maximum would be a member $x$ of $(0,1)$ that is also an upper bound. For any $x\in(0,1)$, the number $t=(x+1)/2$ satisfies $x<t<1$, so $t$ is a member of $(0,1)$ larger than $x$. Thus $x$ is not an upper bound, and no member of $(0,1)$ is a maximum.

\emph{3. Supremum versus maximum.} The interval $(0,1)$ is nonempty and has the upper bound $1$, but by part~2 it has no maximum. So it is a counterexample to ``every nonempty set of real numbers that has an upper bound has a maximum.'' A maximum must belong to the set, while a supremum need not: the supremum $1$ of $(0,1)$ is exactly the point missing from the interval, just as the limit $0$ was missing from $(0,1)$ in Section~\ref{ch11:sec:complete}.}
