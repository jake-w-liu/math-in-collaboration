\sol{10.1}{The row rule gives
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot1+1\cdot(-2)\\0\cdot1+3\cdot(-2)\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}0\\-6\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The columns of $A$ are $Ae_1=(2,0)$ and $Ae_2=(1,3)$, and $x=1e_1-2e_2$. By linearity,
\[
Ax=Ae_1-2Ae_2=(2,0)+(-2,-6)=(0,-6),
\]
the same answer: the coordinates of $x$ say how much of each column to take.}
\sol{10.2}{The kernel consists of the pairs with $x+y=0$, that is, with $y=-x$, so
\[
\ker T=\{(t,-t):t\in\RR\}.
\]
Similarly, $x+y=5$ exactly when $y=5-x$, so the solutions of $T(x,y)=5$ are the pairs $(t,5-t)$ with $t\in\RR$. As Section~\ref{ch10:sec:kernel} predicts, this is one particular solution plus a vector in the kernel:
\[
(t,5-t)=(0,5)+(t,-t).
\]}
\sol{10.3}{Write $U(s,t)=\bigl((s+t)/2,(s-t)/2\bigr)$. By the definition of an inverse in Section~\ref{ch03:sec:functions}, we must check that $U(T(x,y))=(x,y)$ and $T(U(s,t))=(s,t)$ for all real $x,y,s,t$. For the first, apply $U$ with $s=x+y$ and $t=x-y$:
\[
 U(T(x,y))=\left(\frac{(x+y)+(x-y)}{2},\ \frac{(x+y)-(x-y)}{2}\right)=\left(\frac{2x}{2},\ \frac{2y}{2}\right)=(x,y).
\]
For the second,
\[
 T(U(s,t))=\left(\frac{s+t}{2}+\frac{s-t}{2},\ \frac{s+t}{2}-\frac{s-t}{2}\right)=\left(\frac{2s}{2},\ \frac{2t}{2}\right)=(s,t).
\]
Both compositions are identity maps, so $U$ is the inverse of $T$.}
\sol{10.4}{The dot product is $u\cdot v=1\cdot2+2\cdot(-1)=0$. The squared lengths are $1^2+2^2=5$ and $2^2+(-1)^2=5$, so both lengths equal $\sqrt5$. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality reads $|0|\le\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5=5$, which is true and strict. This agrees with the equality case of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:cs}: since $v\ne0$, equality would require $u$ to be a multiple of $v$, but $(1,2)=c(2,-1)$ would need both $2c=1$ and $-c=2$, which is impossible.}
\sol{10.5}{Both sides of $\norm{u+v}_2=\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2$ are nonnegative, and nonnegative numbers are equal exactly when their squares are, so we must decide when $\norm{u+v}_2^2=(\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2)^2$. The proof in Section~\ref{ch10:sec:cs} gives the chain
\begin{align*}
 \norm{u+v}_2^2&=\norm{u}_2^2+2(u\cdot v)+\norm{v}_2^2\\
 &\le\norm{u}_2^2+2|u\cdot v|+\norm{v}_2^2\\
 &\le\norm{u}_2^2+2\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2+\norm{v}_2^2=(\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2)^2.
\end{align*}
If either inequality were strict, the first number would be strictly smaller than the last, so the two ends are equal exactly when both inequalities are equalities.

The first inequality is an equality exactly when $u\cdot v=|u\cdot v|$, that is, when $u\cdot v\ge0$. The second is an equality exactly when $|u\cdot v|=\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$, which by the equality case of the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality happens exactly when $v=0$ or $u$ is a multiple of $v$.

Now combine the two conditions. If $v=0$, both hold, since then $u\cdot v=0$. If $v\ne0$ and $u=sv$, then $u\cdot v=s(v\cdot v)$, and since $v\cdot v>0$ this is nonnegative exactly when $s\ge0$. So equality holds exactly when $v=0$ or $u=sv$ with $s\ge0$.

For a strict example, take $v=(1,1)$ and $u=-v$. Then $u+v=0$, so $\norm{u+v}_2=0$, while $\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2=2\sqrt2$; here $u$ is a multiple of $v$, but with the negative factor $s=-1$.}
\sol{10.6}{Assume that $a_1u_1+\cdots+a_ku_k=0$, pick any index $j$, and take the dot product of both sides with $u_j$. By linearity in the first argument, applied one term at a time as in Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps}, the left side becomes
\[
 a_1(u_1\cdot u_j)+\cdots+a_k(u_k\cdot u_j),
\]
and the right side becomes $0\cdot u_j=0$. For every $i\ne j$, orthogonality gives $u_i\cdot u_j=0$, so only the term with $i=j$ remains:
\[
 a_j(u_j\cdot u_j)=a_j\norm{u_j}_2^2=0.
\]
The vector $u_j$ is nonzero, so its squared length is positive, and dividing by it gives $a_j=0$. Because $j$ was arbitrary, every coefficient is zero, which is the definition of linear independence in Section~\ref{ch10:sec:vectors}.

The word ``nonzero'' cannot be left out: $u_1=(1,0)$ and $u_2=(0,0)$ are orthogonal, yet $0u_1+1u_2=(0,0)$ is a combination equal to zero with a nonzero coefficient, so the list is not linearly independent. In the proof, this is where dividing by $\norm{u_2}_2^2=0$ would be impossible.}
