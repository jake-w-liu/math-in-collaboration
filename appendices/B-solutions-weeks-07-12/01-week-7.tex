\sol{7.1}{(a)~The new graph has the five edges $ab$, $bc$, $ca$, $cd$, and $bd$. Since $a$ is joined to $b,c$; $b$ to $a,c,d$; $c$ to $a,b,d$; and $d$ to $b,c$, the degrees are
\[
\deg(a)=2,\qquad\deg(b)=3,\qquad\deg(c)=3,\qquad\deg(d)=2.
\]
The degree sum is $2+3+3+2=10=2\cdot5$, twice the number of edges, as Theorem~\ref{thm:handshake} requires. The vertices of odd degree are $b$ and $c$: two of them, an even number, as Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:odd} requires.

(b)~Deleting $cd$ from $G$ leaves the three edges $ab$, $bc$, and $ca$, and the degrees are
\[
\deg(a)=2,\qquad\deg(b)=2,\qquad\deg(c)=2,\qquad\deg(d)=0.
\]
The vertex $d$ is still in the graph, with no neighbors. The degree sum is $2+2+2+0=6=2\cdot3$, again twice the number of edges. No vertex has odd degree, and their number, $0$, is even.}
\sol{7.2}{Recall from Section~\ref{ch05:sec:euclid} that the remainder of an integer on division by four is one of $0,1,2,3$; this holds for negative integers too, for example $-7=(-2)\cdot4+1$ has remainder $1$. Use these four remainders as boxes, and place each index $i\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ in the box of the remainder of $x_i$. Five indices are placed in four boxes, and $5>4\cdot1$, so by the pigeonhole principle (Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:pigeonhole} with $k=1$) some box contains two different indices $i\ne j$. Then $x_i$ and $x_j$ leave the same remainder.

We placed the indices, not the values, in the boxes. This matters when two of the integers are equal: if $x_1=x_2=7$, the indices $1$ and $2$ land in the same box, and the conclusion holds with $i=1$ and $j=2$.

Four integers are not enough: $0,1,2,3$ leave four different remainders. Here the pigeonhole principle does not apply, because $4$ is not larger than $4\cdot1$.}
\sol{7.3}{Take the directed cycle $a\to b\to c\to a$. It is an oriented graph: there are no loops, and each pair of vertices is joined in only one direction. It is nonempty and finite, and every vertex has an out-neighbor, so there is no sink. The only hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:sink} that fails is acyclicity.

Every nonempty oriented graph with fewer vertices has a sink. (The graph with no vertices has none, which is why Theorem~\ref{thm:sink} assumes nonemptiness.) With one vertex there can be no edges, because a directed edge needs two different vertices, so the vertex is a sink. With two vertices $u$ and $v$, there is at most one edge. If there is none, both vertices are sinks. If the edge is $u\to v$, then $v\to u$ is forbidden, and $u$ is the only other vertex, so $v$ is a sink; the case $v\to u$ is the same with the names exchanged. Hence three is the smallest possible number of vertices.

In the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sink}, a longest directed path is, for example, $a\to b\to c$, since no directed path has more than three vertices. Its last vertex $c$ has the out-neighbor $a$ on the path: this is the second case of the proof, where $c\to a$ closes a directed cycle. There the proof reached a contradiction only by using acyclicity, so for this graph it cannot conclude that $c$ is a sink.}
\sol{7.4}{At $a$, the first out-neighborhood is $N_1^+(a)=\{b,c\}$. The two-step routes from $a$ are $a\to b\to d$ and $a\to c\to d$. Both end at $d$, which is neither $a$ nor a first out-neighbor of $a$, so $N_2^+(a)=\{d\}$ and $|N_2^+(a)|=1$: the vertex $d$ is counted once, even though two routes reach it.

At $b$, we have $N_1^+(b)=\{d\}$. Since $d$ has no out-neighbors, there is no two-step route from $b$, and $N_2^+(b)=\varnothing$. In the same way, $N_1^+(c)=\{d\}$ and $N_2^+(c)=\varnothing$. Finally, $d$ is a sink, so $N_1^+(d)=N_2^+(d)=\varnothing$.

Comparing sizes: at $a$ we get $1<2$, at $b$ and $c$ we get $0<1$, and at $d$ we get $0\ge0$. So only $d$ satisfies $|N_2^+(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$.

This agrees with Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:acyclic-seymour}: $d$ is a sink, and the graph is acyclic. Every vertex on a directed cycle has an arrow entering it and one leaving it; no arrow enters $a$ and none leaves $d$, so a directed cycle could use only $b$ and $c$, and its arrows would join $b$ and $c$. But no arrow joins them.}
\sol{7.5}{By the handshake theorem, the $n$ degrees add up to $2m$, so their average is $2m/n$. By the second half of the averaging principle (Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:average}), some vertex has degree at most this average.

This says nothing about how far the other degrees may be from the average. For example, let $n\ge3$ and take a \emph{star}: one vertex, the center, is joined to each of the other $n-1$ vertices, called leaves, and there are no other edges. The star has $m=n-1$ edges, so its average degree is
\[
\frac{2(n-1)}{n}=2-\frac2n,
\]
which is less than $2$. Every leaf has degree $1$, below the average, since $2-2/n\ge2-2/3=4/3>1$ for $n\ge3$. The center, however, has degree $n-1$, which grows without bound as $n$ grows, while the average stays below $2$.}

Its first out-neighborhood is $N_1^+(a)=\{b,c\}$. The only two-step route from $a$ is $a\to b\to c$, since $c$ has no out-neighbors, and it ends at $c$, which is already a first out-neighbor of $a$. So $N_2^+(a)=\varnothing$, and $|N_2^+(a)|=0<2=|N_1^+(a)|$: the king $a$ fails the inequality.

The two properties measure different things: being a king means that every other vertex is within directed distance two, while the inequality compares the numbers of vertices at directed distance exactly one and exactly two. A king that beats almost everyone directly leaves few vertices for its second out-neighborhood.}
