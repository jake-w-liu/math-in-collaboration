\sol{8.1}{The graph has three edges: Ada--$1$, Ada--$2$, and Ben--$1$. Take the matching $M=\{\text{Ada--}1\}$.

\emph{$M$ is maximal.} There are two edges outside $M$, and each shares an endpoint with Ada--$1$. The edge Ada--$2$ shares the endpoint Ada, and the edge Ben--$1$ shares the endpoint~$1$. So adding either edge to $M$ gives a set of edges that is not a matching.

\emph{$M$ is not maximum.} The set $\{\text{Ada--}2,\ \text{Ben--}1\}$ is a matching: its two edges have different students and different tasks, so they share no endpoint. It has two edges, while $M$ has one.

In fact $M$ is the only correct answer. The graph has exactly five matchings: the empty matching, $\{\text{Ada--}1\}$, $\{\text{Ada--}2\}$, $\{\text{Ben--}1\}$, and $\{\text{Ada--}2,\ \text{Ben--}1\}$. The empty matching is not maximal, since any edge can be added to it. Ben--$1$ can be added to $\{\text{Ada--}2\}$, and Ada--$2$ can be added to $\{\text{Ben--}1\}$. The matching $\{\text{Ada--}2,\ \text{Ben--}1\}$ is maximum, because no matching can have more edges than the $2$ students. The example shows the difference between the two words: a maximal matching cannot be enlarged while keeping all of its current edges, whereas a maximum matching is compared with every matching in the graph, including those that undo current choices.}
\sol{8.2}{In the matching $\{a\text{--}2,\ b\text{--}1\}$, the student $c$ is unmatched, tasks $1$ and $2$ are used, and task~$3$ is unused. Search from $c$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Inspect $c$. Its only edge, $c$--$2$, is unmatched, so task~$2$ is reached with predecessor $c$. Task~$2$ is held by $a$, so $a$ is reached with predecessor~$2$.
\item Inspect $a$. The edge $a$--$2$ is matched; the edge $a$--$1$ is unmatched, so task~$1$ is reached with predecessor $a$. Task~$1$ is held by $b$, so $b$ is reached with predecessor~$1$.
\item Inspect $b$. The edge $b$--$1$ is matched; the edge $b$--$3$ is unmatched, so task~$3$ is reached with predecessor $b$. Task~$3$ is unused, and the search succeeds.
\end{itemize}
Following the predecessors back from task~$3$ gives the augmenting path
\[
c\;\text{--}\;2\;\text{--}\;a\;\text{--}\;1\;\text{--}\;b\;\text{--}\;3.
\]
Its edges $c$--$2$, $a$--$1$, and $b$--$3$ are unmatched, its edges $a$--$2$ and $b$--$1$ are matched, and its ends $c$ and $3$ are unmatched. Flipping removes $a$--$2$ and $b$--$1$ and adds the other three edges, which gives the matching
\[
\{a\text{--}1,\ b\text{--}3,\ c\text{--}2\}.
\]
It has three edges and saturates the set of students: each student receives an acceptable task, and the tasks $1$, $3$, $2$ are different. Both $a$ and $b$ changed tasks, $a$ from $2$ to $1$ and $b$ from $1$ to $3$, so that $c$ could receive the only task it accepts.}
\sol{8.3}{Let $S$ be the set of all three students. Then $N(S)=\{1,2\}$, so $|N(S)|=2<3=|S|$, and $S$ is a shortage set. It certifies that no full assignment exists: a full assignment would give the three students three different tasks, all of them in $N(S)$, but $N(S)$ has only two members. Anyone can check this certificate by listing the tasks the three students accept and counting them.

Each student has two options, but adding up the options, $2+2+2=6$, counts the same two tasks again and again. What matters is the number of \emph{different} tasks available to the group, and that number is $2$. No smaller group is a shortage set: one student accepts $2\ge1$ tasks, and any two students together accept $2\ge2$ tasks. Indeed, any two of the students can be given different tasks; the shortage appears only when all three compete.}
\sol{8.4}{(a)~Write the augmenting path as $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$. The proof uses the fact that its vertices are all different in two places. First, it concludes that an inner vertex $v_i$ lies on exactly two edges of the path, one matched and one unmatched, and that an end lies on exactly one; so after the flip each of these vertices lies on exactly one edge of the new matching. Second, it counts the edges of the path as $r+1$ unmatched and $r$ matched ones, all different. If a vertex appeared twice, it could lie on two unmatched edges of the walk, both of which the flip adds, and it would then have two partners.

Here is an example. Take students $x$, $s$, $u$ and tasks $1$, $2$, $3$, with edges $x$--$1$, $s$--$1$, $s$--$2$, $s$--$3$, $u$--$1$, $u$--$2$, and the matching $M=\{s\text{--}1,\ u\text{--}2\}$. Student $x$ is unmatched and task~$3$ is unused. The walk
\[
x,\ 1,\ s,\ 2,\ u,\ 1,\ s,\ 3
\]
uses the edges $x$--$1$, $s$--$1$, $s$--$2$, $u$--$2$, $u$--$1$, $s$--$1$, $s$--$3$, which are unmatched, matched, unmatched, matched, unmatched, matched, unmatched in turn. So it is an alternating walk from an unmatched student to an unused task, but it visits task~$1$ and student $s$ twice. Flipping along it removes the matched edges $s$--$1$ and $u$--$2$ and adds the unmatched edges $x$--$1$, $s$--$2$, $u$--$1$, $s$--$3$. The result
\[
\{x\text{--}1,\ s\text{--}2,\ u\text{--}1,\ s\text{--}3\}
\]
is not a matching: task~$1$ lies on two of its edges, and so does student~$s$. Cutting out the part of the walk between the two visits to task~$1$ leaves $x,1,s,3$, which is an augmenting path; flipping along it gives the matching $\{x\text{--}1,\ s\text{--}3,\ u\text{--}2\}$.

(b)~The lengths of the alternating walks from an unmatched student $x$ to an unused task form a nonempty set of natural numbers, so by the well-ordering property there is a shortest such walk, say $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$ with $v_0=x$. Since $x$ is a student and $v_k$ is a task, $k\ge1$. The graph is bipartite, so the vertices of the walk alternate between students and tasks: $v_i$ is a student when $i$ is even and a task when $i$ is odd. The first edge is unmatched, because $x$ is unmatched, so the $m$th edge of the walk is unmatched when $m$ is odd and matched when $m$ is even.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $v_i=v_j$ for some $i<j$. Since $v_i$ and $v_j$ are the same vertex, they lie on the same side, so $i$ and $j$ have the same parity, and $j-i$ is even. Cut out the entries $v_{i+1},\ldots,v_j$, as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{ch07:lem:shortest-walk}. What remains, $v_0,\ldots,v_i,v_{j+1},\ldots,v_k$, is a walk from $x$ to the same unused task, for the reason given in that proof. Its first $i$ edges are unchanged. Each later edge is an edge of the old walk moved forward by $j-i$ positions, which is an even number, so it is unmatched exactly when its new position is odd. Therefore the shorter walk is still alternating. It has length $k-(j-i)<k$, which contradicts the choice of a shortest walk. Hence a shortest walk repeats no vertex. It is an alternating path with at least one step whose ends are both unmatched, that is, an augmenting path.

The search of Section~\ref{ch08:sec:search} produces such a path directly: its predecessor records never repeat a vertex, because each vertex is reached only once.}
\sol{8.5}{The fact that a quantity increases by one at every step does not by itself rule out infinitely many steps. The counter $0,1,2,3,\ldots$ increases by one at every step and never stops. To conclude that a procedure stops, we also need a ceiling that the quantity can never pass.

Finiteness of $L$ provides this ceiling. Each edge of a matching contains exactly one student, and no two edges of a matching contain the same student. So sending each edge to its student is an injective map from the matching to $L$, and a matching has at most $|L|$ edges. Suppose the procedure starts from a matching with $k$ edges. After $m$ steps the matching has $k+m$ edges, so $k+m\le|L|$, that is, $m\le|L|-k$. Hence at most $|L|-k$ augmentations can occur, and at most $|L|$ when the procedure starts from the empty matching. In the language of Habit~5, the nonnegative integer $|L|-|M|$ decreases by one at every step, so it cannot keep decreasing forever.}
\sol{8.6}{The search reaches students and tasks by two moves: from a reached student $s$ along an unmatched edge $s$--$t$ to the task $t$, and from a reached used task to its owner. A task is reached only by the first move, and a student other than $x$ only by the second.

\emph{$B\subseteq N(A)$.} Every task $t$ in $B$ was reached by the first move, from some student $s$ in $A$ along an edge $s$--$t$. So $s$ accepts $t$, and $t\in N(A)$.

\emph{$N(A)\subseteq B$.} Let $t\in N(A)$, and choose a student $s\in A$ that accepts $t$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The edge $s$--$t$ is unmatched.} Because the search is complete, every reached student has been inspected, and inspecting $s$ follows every unmatched edge at $s$. So $t$ has been reached, and $t\in B$.
\item \emph{The edge $s$--$t$ is matched.} Then $s$ is matched, so $s\ne x$, since $x$ is unmatched. A student other than $x$ is reached only by a move from the task it holds, and the task $s$ holds is $t$, its unique partner. So $t$ was reached before $s$, and $t\in B$.
\end{itemize}
In both cases $t\in B$. The two containments together give $N(A)=B$.

Completeness is used only in the unmatched case, and there it is essential. In the three-student example of Section~\ref{ch08:sec:shortage}, a search that stopped right after reaching task~$1$ and its owner $a$ would have $A=\{c,a\}$ and $B=\{1\}$, while $N(A)=\{1,2\}$, because the unmatched edge $c$--$2$ was never followed.}
