\sol{5.1}{We use induction on $n$.

\emph{Base case.} For $n=0$ the sum $\sum_{j=1}^{0}j$ is the empty sum, which is $0$, and the right side is $0\cdot(0+1)/2=0$.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $k$ be a natural number, and assume the induction hypothesis $\sum_{j=1}^{k}j=k(k+1)/2$. Split off the last term of the sum for $k+1$:
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=1}^{k+1}j
 &=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j+(k+1)\\
 &=\frac{k(k+1)}2+(k+1) &&\text{by the induction hypothesis}\\
 &=\frac{k(k+1)}2+\frac{2(k+1)}2\\
 &=\frac{k(k+1)+2(k+1)}2\\
 &=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}2.
\end{align*}
The last line factors out $k+1$. Since $(k+1)\bigl((k+1)+1\bigr)/2=(k+1)(k+2)/2$, this is the formula with $k+1$ in place of $n$. By induction, the formula holds for every $n\in\NN$.}
\sol{5.2}{The first terms are $y_1=2\cdot3=6$, $y_2=2\cdot6=12$ and $y_3=2\cdot12=24$. Each term is twice the one before, so $y_n$ should be $3$ doubled $n$ times: $y_n=3\cdot2^n$.

\emph{Base case.} At $n=0$ the formula gives $3\cdot2^0=3$, which is $y_0$.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $n$ be a natural number, and assume that $y_n=3\cdot2^n$. Then
\[
 y_{n+1}=2y_n=2\cdot(3\cdot2^n)=3\cdot2^{n+1}.
\]
This is the formula at $n+1$. By induction, $y_n=3\cdot2^n$ for every $n\in\NN$.}

\sol{5.5}{As in Section~\ref{ch05:sec:recurrence}, subtracting $1$ from both sides of the rule gives the halving rule $x_{n+1}-1=(x_n-1)/2$ for every $n\in\NN$. We prove $x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)$ by induction on $n$.

\emph{Base case.} At $n=0$ the right side is $2^{0}(x_0-1)=x_0-1$, which is the left side.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $n$ be a natural number, and assume that $x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)$. Then
\[
 x_{n+1}-1=\frac{x_n-1}{2}=\frac{2^{-n}(x_0-1)}{2}=2^{-(n+1)}(x_0-1),
\]
using the halving rule above, then the induction hypothesis, then $2^{-n}/2=2^{-(n+1)}$. This is the formula at $n+1$, which completes the induction.

\emph{The error is never zero.} Suppose that $x_0\ne1$, and let $n$ be any natural number. The number $2^{-n}=1/2^n$ is positive, so it is not zero, and $x_0-1$ is not zero by assumption. A product of two nonzero real numbers is never zero, so $x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)\ne0$: the error halves at every step but never reaches zero.}
\sol{5.6}{The base case gives $P(0)$, the step with $k=0$ gives $P(2)$, the step with $k=2$ gives $P(4)$, and so on. To make ``and so on'' precise, apply ordinary induction on $m$ to the statement $P(2m)$: the base case $m=0$ is $P(0)$, and the step with $k=2m$ turns $P(2m)$ into $P(2m+2)=P\bigl(2(m+1)\bigr)$. So $P(2m)$ holds for every $m\in\NN$. An even natural number $n$ is $2m$ with $m=n/2\ge0$, so $P(n)$ holds for every even natural number $n$.

Nothing follows about the odd numbers, because each use of the step moves from an even number to the next even number. Indeed, take $P(n)$ to be ``$n$ is even.'' Then $P(0)$ is true, and the step holds, because if $k$ is even, so is $k+2$; yet $P(n)$ is false for every odd $n$. So the answer to the first question is the even natural numbers, and only those.

The additional base case $P(1)$ is enough. The same induction on $m$, applied to $P(2m+1)$ with base case $P(1)$ and the step with $k=2m+1$, which turns $P(2m+1)$ into $P(2m+3)=P\bigl(2(m+1)+1\bigr)$, gives $P(2m+1)$ for every $m\in\NN$. Every natural number $n$ is even or odd (Lemma~\ref{ch04:lem:even-or-odd}). If $n=2m+1$ is odd, then $2m=n-1\ge-1$, so the integer $m$ is at least $-\tfrac12$ and hence at least $0$. So $m\in\NN$, and $P(n)$ holds. Together with the even case, $P(n)$ is true for every natural number $n$.}
