\sol{5.1}{We use induction on $n$.

\emph{Base case.} For $n=0$ the sum $\sum_{j=1}^{0}j$ has no terms, so it is the empty sum, which is $0$. The right side is $0\cdot(0+1)/2=0$ as well.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $k$ be a natural number, and assume the induction hypothesis $\sum_{j=1}^{k}j=k(k+1)/2$. Split off the last term of the sum for $k+1$:
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=1}^{k+1}j
 &=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j+(k+1)\\
 &=\frac{k(k+1)}2+(k+1) &&\text{by the induction hypothesis}\\
 &=\frac{k(k+1)}2+\frac{2(k+1)}2\\
 &=\frac{k(k+1)+2(k+1)}2\\
 &=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}2.
\end{align*}
In the third line we wrote $k+1$ as $2(k+1)/2$, so that both terms have denominator $2$. In the last line we factored out the common factor $k+1$. The result is the formula with $k+1$ in place of $n$, because $(k+1)\bigl((k+1)+1\bigr)/2=(k+1)(k+2)/2$. By induction, the formula holds for every $n\in\NN$.}
\sol{5.2}{The first terms are $y_1=2\cdot3=6$, $y_2=2\cdot6=12$ and $y_3=2\cdot12=24$. Each term is twice the one before, so $y_n$ should be the starting value $3$ doubled $n$ times. This suggests the formula $y_n=3\cdot2^n$, in which the exponent $n$ counts the doublings.

\emph{Base case.} At $n=0$ the formula gives $3\cdot2^0=3\cdot1=3$, which is $y_0$.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $n$ be a natural number, and assume that $y_n=3\cdot2^n$. Then
\[
 y_{n+1}=2y_n=2\cdot(3\cdot2^n)=3\cdot2^{n+1}.
\]
This is the formula at $n+1$. By induction, $y_n=3\cdot2^n$ for every $n\in\NN$.}

\sol{5.5}{First rewrite the rule in terms of the error. Subtracting $1$ from both sides of $x_{n+1}=(x_n+1)/2$ gives, for every $n\in\NN$,
\[
 x_{n+1}-1=\frac{x_n+1}{2}-1=\frac{x_n+1-2}{2}=\frac{x_n-1}{2}.
\]
Now we prove $x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)$ by induction on $n$.

\emph{Base case.} At $n=0$ the right side is $2^{0}(x_0-1)=1\cdot(x_0-1)=x_0-1$, which is the left side.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $n$ be a natural number, and assume that $x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)$. Then
\[
 x_{n+1}-1=\frac{x_n-1}{2}=\frac{2^{-n}(x_0-1)}{2}=2^{-(n+1)}(x_0-1),
\]
using the halving rule above, then the induction hypothesis, then $2^{-n}/2=2^{-(n+1)}$. This is the formula at $n+1$, which completes the induction.

\emph{The error is never zero.} Suppose that $x_0\ne1$, and let $n$ be any natural number. The number $2^{-n}=1/2^n$ is positive, so it is not zero, and $x_0-1$ is not zero by assumption. A product of two nonzero real numbers is never zero, so $x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)\ne0$. The size of the error, $2^{-n}\abs{x_0-1}$, halves at every step, so the terms come as close to $1$ as we like; but unless the sequence starts at $1$, no term is ever exactly equal to $1$.}
\sol{5.6}{The base case gives $P(0)$. The step with $k=0$ then gives $P(2)$, the step with $k=2$ gives $P(4)$, and so on. So the proof covers exactly the even numbers $0,2,4,\ldots$. It says nothing about the odd numbers, because each use of the step moves from an even number to the next even number. Indeed, the statement $P(n)$: ``$n$ is even'' satisfies $P(0)$ and the step from $P(k)$ to $P(k+2)$, yet $P(1)$ is false. So no conclusion about odd numbers can follow from these two facts alone.

Add the base case $P(1)$. The same step then gives $P(3)$ from $k=1$, $P(5)$ from $k=3$, and so on, so every odd number is covered as well. Since every natural number is even or odd, the two chains together cover every natural number.

To turn the picture of two chains into a proof by ordinary induction, let $Q(m)$ be the statement ``$P(2m)$ and $P(2m+1)$ are both true.'' The base case $Q(0)$ says that $P(0)$ and $P(1)$ hold, and these are exactly the two base cases. If $Q(m)$ holds, the step with $k=2m$ gives $P(2m+2)$ and the step with $k=2m+1$ gives $P(2m+3)$; together these say $Q(m+1)$. By induction, $Q(m)$ holds for every $m\in\NN$. Every natural number $n$ equals $2m$ or $2m+1$ for some $m\in\NN$, so $P(n)$ is true for every natural number $n$.}
