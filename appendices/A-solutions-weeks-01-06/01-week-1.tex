\sol{1.1}{At $x=4$,
\[
2(x+3)=2(4+3)=2\cdot7=14,\qquad\text{whereas}\qquad 2x+3=2\cdot4+3=8+3=11.
\]
In $2(x+3)$ we first add three to the input and then double the result, so the added three is doubled as well. In $2x+3$ we double only the input and add three afterwards, so the three is not doubled. Multiplying out confirms this: $2(x+3)=2x+6$, which is always exactly $3$ more than $2x+3$, whatever the value of $x$. At $x=4$ the difference is $14-11=3$.}
\sol{1.2}{We must show that the sum has the form required by the definition of odd: twice an integer, plus one.

Let $a$ be an even integer and $b$ an odd integer. By the definitions of even and odd, there are integers $k$ and $\ell$ with $a=2k$ and $b=2\ell+1$. We use two different letters because nothing says the two witnesses are equal. Substitute and regroup:
\begin{align*}
 a+b&=2k+(2\ell+1)\\
 &=2k+2\ell+1\\
 &=2(k+\ell)+1.
\end{align*}
The number $k+\ell$ is an integer, because a sum of integers is an integer. So $a+b$ is twice an integer plus one, which means that $a+b$ is odd. The witness for the oddness of the sum is $k+\ell$.

For example, $a=4$ and $b=-3$ have witnesses $k=2$ and $\ell=-2$, since $4=2\cdot2$ and $-3=2\cdot(-2)+1$. Their sum is $1$, and the proof's witness is $k+\ell=0$; indeed $1=2\cdot0+1$. The example illustrates the proof, but only the general argument covers every even integer and every odd integer.}
\sol{1.3}{The assistant's calculation multiplies an odd number by \emph{itself}: writing both factors as $2k+1$, with the same $k$, forces the two factors to be equal. So the argument covers only products such as $3\cdot3$ or $(-5)\cdot(-5)$. It does not cover the product of two different odd integers, for example $3\cdot5$. The algebra in the argument may be correct, yet it proves a narrower statement than the one claimed.

For the general claim, let $a$ and $b$ be odd integers. By the definition of odd, there are integers $k$ and $\ell$ with $a=2k+1$ and $b=2\ell+1$, where $k$ and $\ell$ may be different. Multiply out one bracket at a time, as in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:algebra}:
\begin{align*}
 ab&=(2k+1)(2\ell+1)\\
 &=2k(2\ell+1)+(2\ell+1)\\
 &=4k\ell+2k+2\ell+1\\
 &=2(2k\ell+k+\ell)+1.
\end{align*}
The number $2k\ell+k+\ell$ is an integer, because products and sums of integers are integers. So $ab$ is twice an integer plus one, and therefore $ab$ is odd. For instance, $3=2\cdot1+1$ and $5=2\cdot2+1$ give $k=1$ and $\ell=2$, so the witness is $2\cdot1\cdot2+1+2=7$, and indeed $3\cdot5=15=2\cdot7+1$. Setting $\ell=k$ in the general proof recovers the assistant's special case $(2k+1)^2$.}
\sol{1.4}{The index $j$ takes the three values $1$, $2$, and $3$, so the sum has three terms:
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=1}^{3}(2a_j+1)&=(2a_1+1)+(2a_2+1)+(2a_3+1)\\
 &=(2\cdot2+1)+(2\cdot(-1)+1)+(2\cdot4+1)\\
 &=5+(-1)+9=13.
\end{align*}
As a check, the same sum can be regrouped as $2(a_1+a_2+a_3)+3=2\cdot5+3=13$.}
\sol{1.5}{By the fact quoted in the exercise, $x(x-2)=0$ holds only if $x=0$ or $x-2=0$, that is, only if $x=0$ or $x=2$. Substituting shows that both values work: $0\cdot(0-2)=0$ and $2\cdot(2-2)=2\cdot0=0$. So the real solutions are exactly $0$ and $2$.

Dividing both sides of $x(x-2)=0$ by $x$ gives $x-2=0$, and hence $x=2$. But division by $x$ is allowed only when $x\ne0$, so this step says nothing about the value $x=0$, and that value is in fact a solution. Division by $x$ therefore finds only the solution $2$. It gives a complete answer only if the case $x=0$ is checked separately.}
\sol{1.6}{Use
\[
 f(n)=(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-10),
\]
the product of the ten factors $n-1$, $n-2$, \ldots, $n-10$. If $n$ is one of the integers $1,2,\ldots,10$, then one of the factors is $n-n=0$, so the whole product is $0$. At $n=11$ the factors are $10,9,\ldots,1$, all positive, so
\[
f(11)=10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1=3\,628\,800\ne0.
\]
This formula agrees with the claim ``$f(n)=0$'' at all ten checked values but fails at $n=11$. Checking finitely many values cannot, on its own, establish a statement about every positive integer.}
