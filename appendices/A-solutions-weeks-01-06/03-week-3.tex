\sol{3.1}{$A\cup B=\{1,2,3,4\}$, with $2$ and $4$ listed once although they lie in both sets; $A\cap B=\{2,4\}$; and $A\setminus B=\{1\}$, because the members $2$ and $4$ of $A$ also lie in $B$.}
\sol{3.2}{To prove that $g\circ f$ is surjective, we start from the \emph{output} end. Take an arbitrary target $c\in C$; we must find a witness $a\in A$ with $g(f(a))=c$.

Since $g$ is surjective and $c$ belongs to its codomain $C$, there is some $b\in B$ with $g(b)=c$. Since $f$ is surjective and this $b$ belongs to its codomain $B$, there is some $a\in A$ with $f(a)=b$. Then
\[
 (g\circ f)(a)=g(f(a))=g(b)=c.
\]
Since $c$ was an arbitrary target, the composition is surjective.

Notice the order. The functions are applied with $f$ first and $g$ second, but the witnesses are found the other way round: first $b$ for $g$, then $a$ for $f$. We could not start by choosing $a$, because until we know $b$ we do not know which target of $f$ to aim for.}
\sol{3.3}{\emph{Injective.} Let $m,n\in\ZZ$ with $f(m)=f(n)$, that is, $2m=2n$. Dividing both sides by $2$ gives $m=n$. So $f$ is injective.

\emph{Not surjective.} If $2n=1$, then $n=1/2$, which is not an integer. So the target $1$ is never reached, and $f$ is not surjective.

\emph{A bijection.} Let $2\ZZ=\{2k:k\in\ZZ\}$ be the set of even integers, and use the rule $n\mapsto2n$ as a function from $\ZZ$ to $2\ZZ$; this is allowed, because every output $2n$ is even. It is still injective by the same argument, since changing the codomain does not change any output. It is now surjective: each member of $2\ZZ$ has the form $2k$ for some $k\in\ZZ$, and the input $k$ has output $2k$. Hence it is a bijection. The new codomain is exactly the image of the original function.}
\sol{3.4}{Let $x\in A$. Then
\begin{align*}
 x\in f^{-1}(S\cup T)
 &\Longleftrightarrow f(x)\in S\cup T &&\text{definition of preimage}\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow f(x)\in S\ \text{or}\ f(x)\in T &&\text{definition of union}\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow x\in f^{-1}(S)\ \text{or}\ x\in f^{-1}(T) &&\text{definition of preimage}\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow x\in f^{-1}(S)\cup f^{-1}(T) &&\text{definition of union}.
\end{align*}
As for intersections in Section~\ref{ch03:sec:images}, reading this chain of equivalences downward and then upward gives the two containments, so the sets are equal. No injectivity, surjectivity, or inverse function is needed.}
\sol{3.5}{\emph{Left inverse.} Here $h(f(a))=a$ for every $a\in A$. To prove that $f$ is injective, take $x,y\in A$ with $f(x)=f(y)$. Since $f(x)$ and $f(y)$ are equal elements of $B$, the domain of $h$, applying $h$ gives
\[
 h(f(x))=h(f(y)).
\]
By the left-inverse identity, the left side equals $x$ and the right side equals $y$. So $x=y$.

\emph{Right inverse.} Here $f(h(b))=b$ for every $b\in B$. To prove that $f$ is surjective, take any target $b\in B$. The element $h(b)\in A$ is a witness, because $f(h(b))=b$.

An inverse satisfies both identities, so by the two parts, every function that has an inverse is bijective. A function can, however, have one kind of one-sided inverse without the other. For example, let $A=\{0\}$, $B=\{0,1\}$, $f(0)=0$, and $h(0)=h(1)=0$. Then $h\circ f=\id_A$, so $h$ is a left inverse of $f$. But no input reaches $1$, so $f$ is not surjective and, by the second part, has no right inverse at all.}
\sol{3.6}{No, equality can fail, but one containment always holds.

\emph{The containment $f(S\cap T)\subseteq f(S)\cap f(T)$.} Let $z\in f(S\cap T)$. Then $z=f(x)$ for some $x\in S\cap T$. That $x$ belongs to $S$, so $z\in f(S)$, and it belongs to $T$, so $z\in f(T)$. Hence $z\in f(S)\cap f(T)$.

\emph{A counterexample to equality.} Let $f:\RR\to\RR$ be $f(x)=x^2$, with $S=\{1\}$ and $T=\{-1\}$. Then $S\cap T=\varnothing$, and the image of the empty set is empty, since there are no inputs to produce outputs; so $f(S\cap T)=\varnothing$. But $f(S)=f(T)=\{1\}$, so $f(S)\cap f(T)=\{1\}$.

The containment proof cannot be reversed: a member $z$ of $f(S)\cap f(T)$ has preimages $s\in S$ and $t\in T$, but they may be different inputs, like $1$ and $-1$ here, and then neither needs to lie in $S\cap T$. If $f$ is injective, however, $f(s)=z=f(t)$ forces $s=t\in S\cap T$, so the equality holds.}
