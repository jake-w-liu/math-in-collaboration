\section{Week 2}
\sol{2.1}{The original implication is true: if $x=3$, then $x^2=3\cdot3=9$.

The converse is ``If $x^2=9$, then $x=3$.'' It is false, and $x=-3$ is a counterexample. It satisfies the hypothesis, since $(-3)^2=9$, but not the conclusion, since $-3\ne3$.

The contrapositive is ``If $x^2\ne9$, then $x\ne3$.'' It is true, because it is equivalent to the original implication, which is true. We can also see this directly: if $x$ were equal to $3$, then $x^2$ would equal $9$, which contradicts the hypothesis $x^2\ne9$.

So the original statement and its contrapositive are true, and the converse is false. This is the same pattern as for ``divisible by four implies even'' in Sections~\ref{ch02:sec:implication} and~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive}: a true implication always has a true contrapositive, but its converse may fail.}
\sol{2.2}{Work from the outside in. The original statement says that \emph{every} integer $n$ has at least one successful response $m$. It fails if \emph{some} integer $n$ has no successful response at all. So the negation begins ``There is an integer $n$ such that, for every integer $m$, \ldots.'' The domains stay the same: both $n$ and $m$ are still integers.

A proposed response $m$ succeeds when $m>n$ \emph{and} $m$ is even. By De Morgan's law, it fails when at least one of these requirements fails: $m\le n$, or $m$ is not even. The comparison $m>n$ is strict, so its failure is $m\le n$, which includes the case $m=n$. In symbols, the original statement is
\[
 \forall n\in\ZZ\ \exists m\in\ZZ:\quad (m>n)\land(m\text{ is even}),
\]
and its negation is
\[
 \exists n\in\ZZ\ \forall m\in\ZZ:\quad
 (m\le n)\lor\neg(m\text{ is even}).
\]
In words: there is an integer $n$ such that every integer $m$ fails at least one requirement, that is, $m$ is at most $n$ or $m$ is not even. The ``or'' is inclusive. An $m$ that fails both requirements, such as an odd integer smaller than $n$, is still an unsuccessful response and must not be left out.

Writing down the negation does not claim that it is true. In fact the original statement is true, so its negation is false. For any integer $n$, take $m=2|n|+2$. It is an integer, because $|n|$ is either $n$ or $-n$, and it is even, because $m=2(|n|+1)$. It is also larger than $n$:
\[
m=2|n|+2\ge|n|+2>|n|\ge n.
\]
The first inequality holds because $|n|\ge0$, and the last because $|n|$ equals $n$ when $n\ge0$ and is positive, hence larger than $n$, when $n<0$. For instance, $n=-5$ gives $m=12$, and $n=7$ gives $m=16$.}
\sol{2.3}{The first statement is true. The skeptic chooses $x$ first, and the prover then chooses $y$ already knowing $x$. The choice $y=-x$ works, because $x+(-x)=0$. For example, $x=5$ is answered by $y=-5$.

The second statement is false. It asks for a single $y$, chosen before $x$, with $x+y=0$ for every real number $x$. Suppose such a $y$ existed. Taking $x=0$ would give $y=0$, and taking $x=1$ would give $1+y=0$, that is, $y=-1$. One number cannot equal both $0$ and $-1$, so no such $y$ exists. Put differently, whatever $y$ is proposed, the skeptic can choose $x=1-y$, and then $x+y=1\ne0$.

The two statements differ only in the order of their quantifiers, exactly as statements (A) and (B) do in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers}.}
\sol{2.4}{The number $x=-3$ is a counterexample. It satisfies the hypothesis, since $(-3)^2=9>4$, but not the conclusion, since $-3$ is not greater than $2$.

The repaired claim is ``If a real number $x$ satisfies $x^2>4$, then $x<-2$ or $x>2$,'' or equivalently ``then $|x|>2$.'' Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} proves that $x^2>4$ holds exactly when $|x|>2$. In brief: if $|x|>2$, squaring the inequality $2<|x|$ between nonnegative numbers gives $4<|x|^2=x^2$; and if $|x|\le2$, squaring this inequality in the same way gives $x^2=|x|^2\le4$. The second case is the contrapositive of ``if $x^2>4$, then $|x|>2$,'' so the repaired claim is true for every real $x$. The first case shows the reverse: every real number that satisfies the new conclusion also satisfies $x^2>4$. So the new conclusion describes exactly the real numbers with $x^2>4$.

Other repairs give true statements but lose information. For instance, ``if $x^2>4$, then $x\ne0$'' is true but much weaker: $x=1$ satisfies $x\ne0$ although $1^2>4$ fails. Restricting the claim to positive $x$ would also make it true, but that changes the hypothesis, which the exercise does not allow, and the restricted claim would say nothing about negative numbers.}
\sol{2.5}{Let Ada and Ben be the two students, and let tasks~$1$ and~$2$ be available. Suppose both students accept task~$1$ and neither accepts task~$2$. Each student accepts at least one task, namely task~$1$. But giving Ada and Ben different acceptable tasks would require two different tasks, each acceptable to the student who receives it, while task~$1$ is the only task either of them accepts.

Following the hint, the condition looks at groups of students:
\begin{center}
\emph{for every group of students, the number of tasks acceptable to at least one member of the group is at least the number of students in the group.}
\end{center}
In the example, the group made up of Ada and Ben has two students but only one acceptable task between them, and $1\ge2$ is false. So the condition fails, and it detects the problem.

The condition must hold whenever all the students can be given different acceptable tasks. Suppose such an assignment exists, and take any group of $k$ students. The assignment gives these $k$ students $k$ different tasks, and each of those tasks is acceptable to the student who receives it, so it is acceptable to at least one member of the group. Hence at least $k$ tasks are acceptable to members of the group.

The group condition is stronger than ``each student accepts at least one task.'' Applied to a group consisting of a single student, it says exactly that this student accepts at least one task, and it also checks every larger group, which is where the example fails. Chapter~\ref{ch:hall} proves the converse: when there are finitely many students and tasks, the group condition guarantees that all the students can be given different acceptable tasks. This is Hall's theorem, Theorem~\ref{thm:hall}.}
\sol{2.6}{Let $\eps$ be a positive real number. By the Archimedean property, choose an integer $N$ with $N>1/\eps$. It is positive, because $1/\eps>0$. Now let $n$ be any integer with $n\ge N$. Then $0<N\le n$, so the rule from Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers} that taking reciprocals of positive numbers reverses $\le$ gives $1/n\le1/N$. As in Example~\ref{ch02:ex:archimedes}, the inequality $N>1/\eps$ gives $1/N<\eps$. Together,
\[
\frac1n\le\frac1N<\eps.
\]
For instance, for $\eps=1/100$ the choice $N=101$ works, and for $\eps=0.003$ the choice $N=334$ works.

The statement involves three choices, made in this order: first the tolerance $\eps$ is given, then we choose $N$, and then any integer $n\ge N$ may be tested. Since $\eps$ comes first, $N$ may depend on it. In fact it must, because a smaller $\eps$ may need a larger $N$: the choice $N=101$ works for $\eps=1/100$ but not for $\eps=1/1000$, and no single $N$ works for every $\eps$. Indeed, testing $n=N$ shows that such an $N$ would satisfy $1/N<\eps$ for every positive $\eps$, which the Pause and decide question at the end of Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers} rules out.

The integer $n$ comes after $N$. The phrase ``every integer $n\ge N$'' only makes sense once $N$ has been fixed, because $N$ decides which integers are tested. To see why this order matters, imagine that $N$ could be chosen after seeing $n$. Then for any $n$ we could choose $N=n+1$. The tested integer $n$ would not satisfy $n\ge N$, so the requirement ``if $n\ge N$, then $1/n<\eps$'' would hold vacuously, as in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive}. Since this trick works whatever condition follows ``if $n\ge N$,'' a statement with the choices in that order promises nothing. With $N$ fixed before $n$, the statement makes a real promise: from $N$ onward, every number $1/n$ is less than $\eps$. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} defines limits by statements of exactly this shape.}
