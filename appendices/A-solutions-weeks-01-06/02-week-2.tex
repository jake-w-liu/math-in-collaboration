\section{Week 2}
\sol{2.1}{The original implication is true: if $x=3$, then $x^2=3\cdot3=9$.

The converse is ``If $x^2=9$, then $x=3$.'' It is false, and $x=-3$ is a counterexample: $(-3)^2=9$, but $-3\ne3$.

The contrapositive is ``If $x^2\ne9$, then $x\ne3$.'' It is true, because it is equivalent to the original implication, which is true.}
\sol{2.2}{Work from the outside in. The statement says that \emph{every} integer $n$ has a successful response $m$, so it fails if \emph{some} integer $n$ has none. The negation therefore begins ``There is an integer $n$ such that, for every integer $m$, \ldots,'' with both domains unchanged.

A response $m$ succeeds when $m>n$ \emph{and} $m$ is even. By De Morgan's law, it fails when $m\le n$ (which includes $m=n$) or $m$ is not even. In symbols, the original statement is
\[
 \forall n\in\ZZ\ \exists m\in\ZZ:\quad (m>n)\land(m\text{ is even}),
\]
and its negation is
\[
 \exists n\in\ZZ\ \forall m\in\ZZ:\quad
 (m\le n)\lor\neg(m\text{ is even}).
\]
In words: there is an integer $n$ such that every integer $m$ is at most $n$ or is not even. The ``or'' is inclusive: an $m$ that fails both requirements, such as an odd integer smaller than $n$, is still an unsuccessful response.

Writing down the negation does not claim that it is true. In fact it is false, because the original statement is true. For any integer $n$, take $m=2|n|+2=2(|n|+1)$, which is an even integer because $|n|$ is $n$ or $-n$. Then $m\ge|n|+2>|n|\ge n$: the first step holds because $|n|\ge0$, and the last because $|n|=n$ when $n\ge0$, while $|n|>0>n$ when $n<0$.}
\sol{2.3}{The first statement is true. The skeptic chooses $x$ first, and the prover, already knowing $x$, chooses $y=-x$, since $x+(-x)=0$.

The second statement is false. It asks for a single $y$, chosen before $x$, with $x+y=0$ for every real number $x$. Whatever $y$ is proposed, the skeptic can choose $x=1-y$, and then $x+y=1\ne0$. So no choice of $y$ works. The two statements differ only in the order of their quantifiers, like statements (A) and (B) in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers}.}
\sol{2.4}{The number $x=-3$ is a counterexample: it satisfies the hypothesis, since $(-3)^2=9>4$, but $-3$ is not greater than $2$.

The repaired claim is ``If a real number $x$ satisfies $x^2>4$, then $x<-2$ or $x>2$,'' or equivalently ``then $|x|>2$.'' Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} proves that $x^2>4$ holds exactly when $|x|>2$, that is, when $x<-2$ or $x>2$. One direction of this equivalence says that the repaired claim is true for every real $x$. The other says that every real number satisfying the new conclusion satisfies $x^2>4$, so the new conclusion describes exactly the real numbers with $x^2>4$.

Other repairs can be true but lose information: ``if $x^2>4$, then $x\ne0$'' is true, but $x=1$ satisfies $x\ne0$ although $1^2>4$ fails.}
\sol{2.5}{Let Ada and Ben be the two students, with tasks~$1$ and~$2$ available. Suppose both students accept task~$1$ and neither accepts task~$2$. Each student accepts at least one task, but task~$1$ is the only task either of them accepts, so they cannot be given two different acceptable tasks.

Following the hint, consider the condition
\begin{center}
\emph{for every group of students, the number of tasks acceptable to at least one member of the group is at least the number of students in the group.}
\end{center}
In the example, the group made up of Ada and Ben has two students but only one acceptable task between them, so the condition fails.

The condition must hold whenever all the students can be given different acceptable tasks. Suppose such an assignment exists, and take any group of $k$ students. The assignment gives them $k$ different tasks, each acceptable to the member of the group who receives it. Hence at least $k$ tasks are acceptable to members of the group.

The condition is stronger than ``each student accepts at least one task'': for a group of one student, it says exactly that, and it also checks every larger group, which is where the example fails. Hall's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:hall}) proves the converse: with finitely many students and tasks, the group condition guarantees that all the students can be given different acceptable tasks.}
\sol{2.6}{Let $\eps$ be a positive real number. By the Archimedean property, choose an integer $N>1/\eps$; as in Example~\ref{ch02:ex:archimedes}, $N$ is positive and $1/N<\eps$. Now let $n$ be any integer with $n\ge N$. Since $0<N\le n$, taking reciprocals gives $1/n\le1/N$. Together,
\[
\frac1n\le\frac1N<\eps.
\]

The choices are made in this order: first $\eps$ is given, then we choose $N$, and then any integer $n\ge N$ may be tested. Since $\eps$ comes first, $N$ may depend on it, and it must: $N=101$ works for $\eps=1/100$ but not for $\eps=1/1000$, and no single $N$ works for every $\eps$, since testing $n=N$ would give $1/N<\eps$ for every positive $\eps$, which the Pause and decide question at the end of Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers} rules out.

The integer $n$ comes after $N$: the phrase ``every integer $n\ge N$'' only makes sense once $N$ is fixed. If $N$ could be chosen after seeing $n$, we could take $N=n+1$; then $n\ge N$ would be false, so ``if $n\ge N$, then $1/n<\eps$'' would hold vacuously (Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive}), whatever condition followed. With the choices in that order the statement would promise nothing. With $N$ fixed before $n$, it makes a real promise: from $N$ onward, every number $1/n$ is less than $\eps$. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} defines limits by statements of exactly this shape.}
