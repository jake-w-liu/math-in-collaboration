\chapter*{To the reader}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{To the reader}
This book starts from ordinary arithmetic: if you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide fractions and whole numbers, including negative numbers and zero, you can begin. Mathematicians call the whole numbers $\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots$ the \emph{integers}. You do not need calculus, linear algebra, experience with proofs, or programming. Chapter~\ref{ch:start} reviews the algebra and notation we need and explains what definitions, theorems, and proofs are.

We use the real numbers---all the points of the number line, including numbers such as $\sqrt2$ that are not fractions---as a familiar tool. Besides the ordinary rules of arithmetic, such as $a+b=b+a$, we accept four \emph{starting facts} without proof; each is stated again where it is first used:
\begin{itemize}
\item every nonnegative real number has exactly one nonnegative square root (Chapter~\ref{ch:start});
\item for every real number, however large, there is a larger integer (the \emph{Archimedean property}, Chapter~\ref{ch:logic});
\item every nonempty set of positive integers has a smallest member (the \emph{well-ordering property}, Chapter~\ref{ch:proof});
\item the real numbers are \emph{complete}: roughly, the number line has no holes. If, for every distance however small, all the terms of a sequence from some point on lie within that distance of one another, then the terms approach some point of the line. Cutting off $\sqrt2=1.41421\ldots$ after more and more digits gives such a sequence, $1$, $1.4$, $1.41$, $1.414$, \ldots, which approaches $\sqrt2$; a line containing only fractions would have a hole there (Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}).
\end{itemize}

Many students begin a mathematics course by asking how much they must memorize before they can start. This book is organized around a different question: \emph{How can I enter a mathematical conversation, notice what is missing from my understanding, and learn enough to take part responsibly?} An AI assistant can be a useful partner in that conversation, suggesting examples, explanations, arguments, and code. It can also make convincing-sounding mistakes, and it cannot understand the mathematics for you. So throughout the book you will practice three skills: asking for exactly the step you are missing, noticing when an answer has quietly changed an assumption, and explaining what an argument really establishes.

Three problems run through the semester. Can each person in a group be given a different task that they find acceptable? Can a process that repeatedly improves an approximate answer settle down to an exact solution? Can a sequence of plus and minus signs be arranged so that it has almost no resemblance to shifted copies of itself? Each starts with small examples you can work out by hand. Followed far enough, they lead to Hall's matching theorem (Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall}), the contraction theorem (Chapters~\ref{ch:limits} and~\ref{ch:contraction}), and a question about special matrices that has been open since the 1960s and for which a proof was claimed in 2026 (Chapter~\ref{ch:signs}). A fourth strand, finite probability (Chapter~\ref{ch:probability}), shows how computing an average can prove that an object with a desired property exists, even when the argument does not say how to build it.

Most definitions are introduced as answers to problems, so that you can see why they are worded as they are. Major proofs appear first as plans, then as complete arguments, and finally as arguments you can modify and test, for instance by removing a hypothesis (one of the assumptions a theorem makes) and finding the step that breaks. Along the way the book collects eight \emph{habits}, short pieces of advice about making progress, each in a shaded box headed by its number and name. Habit~1, for example, is \emph{choose a representation that exposes the goal}: write your objects in a form in which what you want to prove can be seen, as when you write a number as $2k$, with $k$ an integer, to show that it is even.

\section*{How to use a chapter}
Each chapter opens with a shaded box headed ``This week'' that says what the chapter is for. When the text raises a question or points out a pattern, try a small example of your own before looking for the answer. Many sections stop at a question marked \textbf{Pause and decide}, followed by a \textit{Check}: cover the check, commit to an answer, and only then read on. When you read a proof, ask two questions about every line: \emph{Why is this line true?} and \emph{Why is this line here?}---that is, which later step needs it? You understand the proof when you can answer both.

Near the end of each chapter there are six exercises, numbered by chapter: Exercise~\ref{ex:7.3} is the third exercise of Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. The first four are \emph{core} exercises, which practice the main ideas directly. The last two are \emph{transfer} exercises, which use the same ideas in a new setting or with a changed hypothesis. The one exception is Chapter~\ref{ch:hall}, whose sixth exercise is the midterm portfolio described in \emph{Planning the semester}. Complete solutions are in Appendices~\ref{app:sola}--\ref{app:solc}, grouped by week: the section headed ``Week~7'' holds the solutions for Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. Look at a solution only after a genuine attempt; the introduction to Appendix~\ref{app:sola} suggests how to use the solutions.

Two further appendices are for reference. Appendix~\ref{app:practical} explains just enough of the programming language Python to run the book's computer experiments. It also says where to find the official installation instructions for Lean, the \emph{proof assistant} (a program that checks every step of a proof) used in Chapters~\ref{ch:lean1} and~\ref{ch:lean2}, and how to have Lean check the book's Lean examples on your own computer. Appendix~\ref{app:guide} begins with a table of the book's main symbols, each with the place where it is explained, and returns to several of the habits.

\section*{Keeping track of evidence}
For each important statement you meet, keep two separate records in your notebook. The first says how well you understand it: \emph{understood} (you can explain it in your own words and use it in a new situation), \emph{can follow} (you can check each step but could not yet explain it yourself), or \emph{open} (you cannot yet follow it). Chapter~\ref{ch:bridge}, which studies a long proof, adds \emph{borrowed}, for a result that you use without having studied its proof. The second record says what supports the statement: \emph{proved} (in this book or by you), \emph{cited from another source}, \emph{checked by computer for finitely many cases}, or \emph{not established}. For example, if a computer confirms that $1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2$ for every $n$ from $1$ to $1000$, the second record says ``checked by computer for finitely many cases''; only after the induction proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:odds} in Chapter~\ref{ch:induction} does it say ``proved.'' Record the four starting facts as \emph{assumptions}: we use them without proof, although more advanced books prove them from a careful construction of the numbers.

A proof that a computer has checked step by step can still be poorly understood, and a pattern you understand well from experiments can still be unproved. Keeping both records stops these two kinds of confidence from blurring into one.

\section*{Where the course ends}
The course ends with an independent investigation of your own, the \emph{capstone}. It is open-ended, so it has no answer key; instead, Chapter~\ref{ch:capstone} works through a complete model investigation, and Section~\ref{appE:sec:rubric} gives a \emph{rubric}: a table of the qualities on which your investigation can be assessed.

By the end of the semester you should be able to rebuild several complete proofs from their main ideas, without having memorized their wording; run and check small computer experiments; carry out a small, carefully limited investigation with an AI assistant; and read short proofs written in Lean. Most research papers will still be out of reach, but you will have a method for an unfamiliar proof: find out exactly which background it needs, learn that background, and connect it back to the proof.

Whether an argument is correct and whether it is new are separate questions. The theorems in this book are well-established results. A result you discover for yourself may be correct and still be well known; it becomes a contribution to research only after it has been compared carefully with what is already known and its evidence has been checked.
