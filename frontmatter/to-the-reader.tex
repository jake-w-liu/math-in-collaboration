\chapter*{To the reader}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{To the reader}
This book starts from ordinary arithmetic. If you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide whole numbers and fractions, you have enough background to begin. No university mathematics is assumed: you do not need calculus, linear algebra, or any experience with proofs, and you do not need to know how to program. Chapter~\ref{ch:start} rebuilds the algebraic notation we need, and the language of definitions, theorems, and proofs is explained before it is used.

We use the real numbers---all the points of the number line, including numbers such as $\sqrt2$ that are not fractions---as a familiar tool, without constructing them from anything simpler. Along with the ordinary rules of arithmetic, such as $a+b=b+a$, we take four properties of numbers as starting facts and do not prove them. Each one is stated openly where it is first used:
\begin{itemize}
\item every nonnegative real number has exactly one nonnegative square root (Chapter~\ref{ch:start});
\item beyond any real number, however large, there is an integer (Chapter~\ref{ch:logic});
\item every nonempty set of positive integers has a smallest member (Chapter~\ref{ch:proof});
\item the real numbers are \emph{complete}, which roughly says that the number line has no gaps; this is what guarantees that the limits we need actually exist (Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}).
\end{itemize}

Many students approach a mathematics course with the question ``How much do I have to memorize before I can start?'' This book is organized around a different question: \emph{How can I enter a mathematical conversation, notice what is missing from my understanding, and learn enough to take part responsibly?} An AI assistant can be a useful partner in that conversation. It can suggest examples, explanations, arguments, and code. It can also make mistakes that sound convincing, and it cannot understand the mathematics for you. Throughout the book you will practice three skills that make such a partner useful: asking for exactly the step you are missing, noticing when an answer has quietly changed an assumption, and explaining what an argument really establishes.

Three problems run through the semester. Can a group of people be given different tasks so that each person receives a task they find acceptable? Can a process that repeatedly improves an approximate answer settle down to an exact solution? Can a sequence of plus and minus signs be arranged so that it has almost no resemblance to shifted copies of itself? Each problem starts with small examples that you can work out by hand. Followed far enough, they lead to Hall's matching theorem (Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall}), the contraction theorem (Chapters~\ref{ch:limits} and~\ref{ch:contraction}), and a long-standing question about special matrices on which there is current research (Chapter~\ref{ch:signs}). A fourth strand, finite probability (Chapter~\ref{ch:probability}), shows how computing an average can prove that an object with a desired property exists without showing directly how to build it, and when such a proof can be turned into a construction.

Most definitions are introduced as answers to problems, so that you can see why they are worded as they are. Major proofs appear first as plans, then as complete arguments, and finally as things you can modify and test. Along the way the book collects eight numbered habits, each in a shaded box headed ``Habit.'' Three of them are: choose a representation that exposes what you want to prove; write down exactly what a statement promises; and when an attempt fails, try to turn the failure into a concrete obstruction. The habits come back in chapter after chapter, so that they become familiar through use.

\section*{How to use a chapter}
Each chapter opens with a shaded box headed ``This week'' that says what the chapter is for. Read the opening problem and try a small example of your own before you look for a solution, either in the text or from an assistant. Then read the explanation slowly. Throughout the text you will meet questions marked \textbf{Pause and decide}, each followed by a \textit{Check}. Cover the check, commit to an answer, and only then read on. When you read a proof, ask two questions about every line: \emph{Why is this line true?} and \emph{Why is this line here?} You understand a proof when you can answer both questions for every line.

Near the end of each chapter there are six exercises, numbered by chapter: Exercise~\ref{ex:7.3} is the third exercise of Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. The first four are \emph{core} exercises, which practice the main ideas directly. The last two are \emph{transfer} exercises, which ask you to use the same ideas in a new setting or with a changed hypothesis. Complete solutions to all of them are in Appendices~\ref{app:sola}--\ref{app:solc}, grouped by week: the section headed ``Week~7'' contains the solutions for Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. Look at a solution only after a genuine attempt, and afterwards close the book and write the argument again in your own words. The independent investigation that ends the course is open-ended, so it has no answer key. Instead, Chapter~\ref{ch:capstone} works through a complete model investigation, and Appendix~\ref{app:guide} gives a rubric for assessing your own.

\section*{Where the course ends}
By the end of the semester you should be able to rebuild several complete proofs from their main ideas, without having memorized their wording. You should be able to work out what background you need for an unfamiliar statement, run and check small mathematical experiments on a computer, read short proofs written in Lean, a \emph{proof assistant} (a computer program that checks every step of a proof), and carry out a carefully limited investigation with the help of an AI assistant. You will not yet be able to read every research paper; a specialized proof can require a great deal of further study. What you will have is a method: find out exactly which background a proof needs, learn it, and connect it back to the proof you wanted to understand.

Whether an argument is correct and whether it is new are separate questions. The theorems proved in this book are well-established results, presented so that you can follow and rebuild them. A result you discover for yourself may be correct and still be well known. It becomes a contribution to research only after it has been compared carefully with what is already known and its evidence has been checked.
