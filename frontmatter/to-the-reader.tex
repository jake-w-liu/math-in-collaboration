\chapter*{To the reader}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{To the reader}
This book starts from ordinary arithmetic. If you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide fractions and whole numbers, including negative numbers and zero, you have enough background to begin. Mathematicians call the whole numbers $\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots$ the \emph{integers}. No university mathematics is assumed: you do not need calculus, linear algebra, or any experience with proofs, and you do not need to know how to program. Chapter~\ref{ch:start} reviews the algebra and notation we need and explains what definitions, theorems, and proofs are.

We use the real numbers---all the points of the number line, including numbers such as $\sqrt2$ that are not fractions---as a familiar tool. Along with the ordinary rules of arithmetic, such as $a+b=b+a$, we accept four properties of numbers without proof. These are our \emph{starting facts}, and each is stated again where it is first used:
\begin{itemize}
\item every nonnegative real number has exactly one nonnegative square root (Chapter~\ref{ch:start});
\item for every real number, however large, there is a larger integer (the \emph{Archimedean property}, Chapter~\ref{ch:logic});
\item every nonempty set of positive integers has a smallest member (the \emph{well-ordering property}, Chapter~\ref{ch:proof});
\item the real numbers are \emph{complete}: roughly, the number line has no holes. Suppose that for every distance, however small, all the terms of a sequence from some point on lie within that distance of one another. Then the terms approach some point of the line. For example, cutting off $\sqrt2=1.41421\ldots$ after more and more digits gives the fractions $1$, $1.4$, $1.41$, $1.414$, \ldots, and these approach the point $\sqrt2$. If the line contained only fractions, there would be a hole at $\sqrt2$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}).
\end{itemize}

Many students approach a mathematics course with the question ``How much do I have to memorize before I can start?'' This book is organized around a different question: \emph{How can I enter a mathematical conversation, notice what is missing from my understanding, and learn enough to take part responsibly?} An AI assistant can be a useful partner in that conversation. It can suggest examples, explanations, arguments, and code. It can also make mistakes that sound convincing, and it cannot understand the mathematics for you. Throughout the book you will practice three skills that make such a partner useful: asking for exactly the step you are missing, noticing when an answer has quietly changed an assumption, and explaining what an argument really establishes.

Three problems run through the semester. Can a group of people be given different tasks so that each person receives a task they find acceptable? Can a process that repeatedly improves an approximate answer settle down to an exact solution? Can a sequence of plus and minus signs be arranged so that it has almost no resemblance to shifted copies of itself? We begin each one with small examples that you can work out by hand. Followed far enough, these problems lead to Hall's matching theorem (Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall}), the contraction theorem (Chapters~\ref{ch:limits} and~\ref{ch:contraction}), and a question about special matrices that has been open since the 1960s and for which a proof was claimed in 2026 (Chapter~\ref{ch:signs}). A fourth strand, finite probability (Chapter~\ref{ch:probability}), shows how computing an average can prove that an object with a desired property exists, even when the argument does not say how to build it. That chapter then turns one such proof into a step-by-step construction.

Most definitions are introduced as answers to problems, so that you can see why they are worded as they are. Major proofs appear first as plans, then as complete arguments, and finally as arguments you can modify and test, for instance by removing one hypothesis (one of the assumptions a theorem makes) and finding the step that breaks. Along the way the book collects eight \emph{habits}, short pieces of advice about how to make progress, each in a shaded box headed by its number and name. Habit~1, for example, is \emph{choose a representation that exposes the goal}: write the objects you are working with in a form in which the thing you want to prove can be seen, as when you write a number as $2k$, with $k$ an integer, to show that it is even. Habit~7, \emph{make failure informative}, applies when an attempt to build something fails: look for a concrete object that shows why it cannot be built. For instance, three people who between them find only two of the tasks acceptable cannot all receive different tasks. Later chapters, and the guide in Appendix~\ref{app:guide}, return to several of these habits when they are needed again.

\section*{How to use a chapter}
Each chapter opens with a shaded box headed ``This week'' that says what the chapter is for. Whenever the text raises a question or points out a pattern, try a small example of your own before you look for the answer, either further on in the text or from an assistant. Then read the explanation slowly. Many sections stop at a question marked \textbf{Pause and decide}, followed by a \textit{Check}. Cover the check, commit to an answer, and only then read on. When you read a proof, ask two questions about every line: \emph{Why is this line true?} and \emph{Why is this line here?} The second question asks which later step needs the line. You understand the proof when you can answer both.

Near the end of each chapter there are six exercises, numbered by chapter: Exercise~\ref{ex:7.3} is the third exercise of Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. The first four are \emph{core} exercises, which practice the main ideas directly. The last two are \emph{transfer} exercises, which ask you to use the same ideas in a new setting or with a changed hypothesis. Chapter~\ref{ch:hall} is the one exception: there only the fifth exercise is a transfer exercise, and the sixth is the midterm portfolio, a larger piece of work described in \emph{Planning the semester}. Complete solutions to all the exercises are in Appendices~\ref{app:sola}--\ref{app:solc}. They are grouped by week, since the course covers one chapter per week: the section headed ``Week~7'' contains the solutions for Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}. Look at a solution only after a genuine attempt; the introduction to Appendix~\ref{app:sola} suggests how to use the solutions.

Two further appendices are for reference. Appendix~\ref{app:practical} assumes no programming experience. It explains just enough of the programming language Python to run the book's computer experiments. It also tells you where to find the official installation instructions for Lean, the \emph{proof assistant} used in Chapters~\ref{ch:lean1} and~\ref{ch:lean2}, and how to have Lean check the book's Lean examples on your own computer. A proof assistant is a computer program that checks every step of a proof. Appendix~\ref{app:guide} begins with a table of the book's main symbols, each with the place where it is explained, so that you can find your way back when a symbol looks unfamiliar.

\section*{Keeping track of evidence}
For each important statement you meet, keep two separate records in your notebook. The first says how well you understand it: \emph{understood} (you can explain it in your own words and use it in a new situation), \emph{can follow} (you can check each step but could not yet explain it yourself), or \emph{open} (you cannot yet follow it). Chapter~\ref{ch:bridge}, which studies a long proof, adds one more label, \emph{borrowed}, for a result that you use without having studied its proof. The second record says what supports the statement: \emph{proved} (in this book or by you), \emph{cited from another source}, \emph{checked by computer for finitely many cases}, or \emph{not established}. For example, if a computer confirms that $1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2$ for every $n$ from $1$ to $1000$, the second record says ``checked by computer for finitely many cases.'' Only after the induction proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:odds} in Chapter~\ref{ch:induction} does it say ``proved.'' The four starting facts need a label of their own. We use them without proof, so record each of them as an \emph{assumption}. More advanced books prove them, starting from a careful construction of the numbers.

A proof that a computer program has checked step by step can still be poorly understood, and a pattern you understand well from experiments can still be unproved. Keeping both records stops these two kinds of confidence from blurring into one.

\section*{Where the course ends}
The course ends with an independent investigation of your own, called the \emph{capstone}. It is open-ended, so it has no answer key. Instead, Chapter~\ref{ch:capstone} works through a complete model investigation, and Section~\ref{appE:sec:rubric} gives a \emph{rubric}: a table of the qualities on which your own investigation can be assessed.

By the end of the semester you should be able to rebuild several complete proofs from their main ideas, without having memorized their wording. You should also be able to run and check small mathematical experiments on a computer, carry out a small, carefully limited investigation like the capstone with the help of an AI assistant, and read short proofs written in Lean. Most research papers will still be out of reach, because a specialized proof can require a great deal of further study. What you will have is a method for an unfamiliar proof: find out exactly which background it needs, learn that background, and connect it back to the proof you wanted to understand.

Whether an argument is correct and whether it is new are separate questions. The theorems proved in this book are well-established results, presented so that you can follow and rebuild them. A result you discover for yourself may be correct and still be well known. It becomes a contribution to research only after it has been compared carefully with what is already known and its evidence has been checked.
