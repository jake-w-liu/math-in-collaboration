\chapter*{Planning the semester}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Planning the semester}
The schedule below covers eighteen teaching weeks, one chapter per week. Each week is planned around three hours of class and roughly four to six hours of individual reading and exercises; some students will need more time than this and some less. Chapters~\ref{ch:start}--\ref{ch:structure} deliberately move slowly, because everything later depends on them. Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall} form one two-week unit, and so do Chapters~\ref{ch:limits} and~\ref{ch:contraction}: in each pair, the first chapter develops the ideas that the long proof in the second chapter uses, so plan the two weeks together. Chapters~\ref{ch:bridge} and~\ref{ch:capstone} introduce only the few new ideas that one particular proof or investigation needs; their main work is to bring together what came before.

A chapter is a week's reading, meant to be divided at its section headings rather than read in one sitting. For a long proof, a single sitting might cover only the statement and one of its smaller arguments. When you come back, start by saying what has already been established and what remains to be done. The worked examples are part of the main path through each chapter, so do not skip them. In a taught course, the four core exercises of each chapter form the required weekly work, and the two transfer exercises provide extra practice once the core exercises feel secure.

\section*{A repeatable weekly rhythm}
A week of class can follow a simple pattern. In the first meeting, unpack the opening problem and work out one example together. In the second, reconstruct the main proof and compare two possible next moves at a difficult step. In the third, examine a proposed argument---one written by a classmate, taken from the text, or produced by an AI assistant---and decide exactly what it proves and where it might fail. If you are working on your own, follow a similar sequence: try the opening problem yourself, read the chapter while answering its ``Pause and decide'' questions, rebuild the main proof with the book closed, and finish with a transfer exercise. When an example is already familiar to you, do not skip it; instead, change one of its assumptions and work out what happens.

\section*{Assessment that rewards understanding}
One reasonable weighting gives 40\% to the weekly core exercises, 25\% to the midterm portfolio in Week~9, and 35\% to the final investigation. AI assistance can be allowed, provided that each submission says openly how it was used. Besides its mathematics, every submission should contain three things: a short explanation of its central step, written without assistance; one boundary case that has been tested, such as zero, the empty set, or the smallest allowed input; and a statement of anything that is borrowed from elsewhere or not yet checked. The grade should reflect the mathematics and the understanding the student demonstrates, not the length of a conversation log or the polish of generated prose.

The midterm portfolio is Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6}. It contains a complete proof that Hall's condition guarantees a full assignment; a graph that has a full assignment, together with that assignment; a graph that has none, together with a group of students who accept too few tasks between them; and a paragraph explaining why a greedy assignment can get stuck even when a full assignment exists. The solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6} in Appendix~\ref{app:solb} is a model portfolio.

The final investigation is the independent capstone described at the end of Chapter~\ref{ch:capstone}. It contains a precise statement, a map of the background it needs, a proof or a counterexample, computer checks where they are relevant, a record of the assumptions used and the evidence for each claim, and a short explanation of what is new to the published literature and what is new only to the student. It ends with a short oral discussion in which the student explains one delicate step, changes an assumption, and predicts what fails. Chapter~\ref{ch:capstone} first works through a complete model investigation, and Appendix~\ref{app:guide} gives a suggested grading rubric.

\section*{Keeping track of evidence}
For each important statement you meet, keep two separate records in your notebook. The first says how well you understand it: \emph{can explain}, \emph{can follow}, or \emph{not yet understood}. The second says what supports it: \emph{proved} (in this book or by you), \emph{cited from another source}, \emph{checked by computer for finitely many cases}, or \emph{not established}. For example, if a computer confirms that $1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2$ for every $n$ from $1$ to $1000$, the second record says ``checked by computer for finitely many cases.'' Only after the induction proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:odds} in Chapter~\ref{ch:induction} does it say ``proved.''

The two records measure different things. A proof that a computer has checked can still be poorly understood, and a pattern you understand well from experiments can still be unproved. Keeping both records stops these two kinds of confidence from blurring into one.
