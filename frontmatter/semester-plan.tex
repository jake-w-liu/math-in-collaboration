\chapter*{Planning the semester}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Planning the semester}
The schedule below covers eighteen teaching weeks, one chapter per week, each planned around three hours of class and four to six hours of reading and exercises on your own. Chapters~\ref{ch:start}--\ref{ch:structure} deliberately move slowly, because everything later depends on them. Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall} form a two-week unit, and so do Chapters~\ref{ch:limits} and~\ref{ch:contraction}: the first chapter of each pair develops the ideas that the long proof in the second uses, so plan the two weeks together. The last two chapters, \ref{ch:bridge} and~\ref{ch:capstone}, bring together what came before and introduce new ideas only when a particular proof or investigation needs them.

Terms in the table that you may not know yet, such as residue classes or the kernel of a map, are explained in their chapters.

Read each chapter in several sittings, breaking at its section headings, and when you come back, start by saying what has been established and what remains. Do not skip the worked examples: they are part of the main path. In a taught course, the required work is the four core exercises of each chapter and, in Week~9, the midterm portfolio, Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6}; the transfer exercises give extra practice once the core exercises feel secure.

\section*{A weekly rhythm}
If the class meets three times a week, the first meeting can pose the chapter's question and work one small example together; the second can reconstruct the main proof and compare two possible next moves at a difficult step; the third can examine a proposed argument---by a classmate, from the text, or from an AI assistant---and decide exactly what it proves and where it might fail. On your own, read the chapter as \emph{To the reader} suggests, then rebuild the main proof with the book closed, work the core exercises, and finish with a transfer exercise.

\section*{Assessment that rewards understanding}
A reasonable weighting gives 40\% to the weekly core exercises, 25\% to the midterm portfolio in Week~9, and 35\% to the final investigation. If AI assistance is allowed, each submission should say openly how it was used. Besides its mathematics, every submission should contain a short explanation of its central step, written without help; one boundary case you have tested, such as zero, the empty set, or the smallest allowed input; and a statement of anything borrowed or not yet checked. The grade should reflect the mathematics and understanding shown, not the length of a conversation log or the polish of generated prose.

The midterm portfolio, Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6}, is about the assignment problem of Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall}, in which a \emph{full assignment} gives every student a different acceptable task. It has four parts:
\begin{itemize}
\item a checkable full assignment for the graph of Exercise~\ref{ex:9.2};
\item a graph with no full assignment, and a group of students who together accept fewer tasks than the group has members, which proves that none exists;
\item a complete proof, in your own words, of the harder half of Hall's theorem: if every group of students together accepts at least as many tasks as the group has members, then a full assignment exists;
\item a paragraph on why handing out tasks one at a time, never taking one back, can get stuck even when a full assignment exists.
\end{itemize}
Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6} states each part precisely, and its solution in Appendix~\ref{app:solb} is a model portfolio.

The final investigation is the independent capstone of Section~\ref{ch18:sec:independent}, which lists what the write-up should contain and describes the short oral discussion that ends it: you explain one delicate step, change an assumption, and predict what fails. Section~\ref{appE:sec:rubric} suggests a grading rubric.
