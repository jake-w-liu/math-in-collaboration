\chapter*{Planning the semester}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Planning the semester}
The schedule below covers eighteen teaching weeks, one chapter per week. Each week is planned around three hours of class and about four to six hours of reading and exercises on your own. Chapters~\ref{ch:start}--\ref{ch:structure} deliberately move slowly, because everything later depends on them. Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall} form one two-week unit, and so do Chapters~\ref{ch:limits} and~\ref{ch:contraction}: in each pair, the first chapter develops the ideas that the long proof in the second chapter uses, so plan the two weeks together. The last two chapters, \ref{ch:bridge} and~\ref{ch:capstone}, bring together what came before; they introduce new ideas only when one particular proof or investigation needs them.

The table lists the topic of each week and the main piece of work you produce in it. Terms you may not know yet, such as residue classes or the kernel of a map, are explained in the chapters where they appear.

Read each chapter in several sittings, breaking at its section headings. For a long proof, a single sitting might cover only the statement and one of its smaller arguments. When you come back, start by saying what has already been established and what remains to be done. The worked examples are part of the main path through each chapter, so do not skip them. If an example is already familiar, work through it anyway, then change one of its assumptions and see what happens. In a taught course, the four core exercises of each chapter form the required weekly work, and the transfer exercises provide extra practice once the core exercises feel secure. Week~9 is the exception: its sixth exercise, Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6}, is the midterm portfolio, and it is required.

\section*{A weekly rhythm}
If the class meets three times a week, each week can follow a simple pattern. In the first meeting, set out the question the chapter is about and work out one small example together. In the second, reconstruct the main proof and compare two possible next moves at a difficult step. In the third, examine a proposed argument---one written by a classmate, taken from the text, or produced by an AI assistant---and decide exactly what it proves and where it might fail. If you are working on your own, follow a similar sequence: read the ``This week'' box and try a small example yourself, read the chapter while answering its ``Pause and decide'' questions, rebuild the main proof with the book closed, work the core exercises, and finish with a transfer exercise.

\section*{Assessment that rewards understanding}
One reasonable weighting gives 40\% to the weekly core exercises, 25\% to the midterm portfolio in Week~9, and 35\% to the final investigation. If AI assistance is allowed, each submission should say openly how it was used. Besides its mathematics, every submission should contain three things: a short explanation of its central step, written without help; one boundary case that you have tested, such as zero, the empty set, or the smallest allowed input; and a statement of anything borrowed from elsewhere or not yet checked. The grade should reflect the mathematics and the understanding you show, not the length of a conversation log or the polish of generated prose.

The midterm portfolio, Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6}, is about the assignment problem of Chapters~\ref{ch:matching} and~\ref{ch:hall}, in which a \emph{full assignment} gives every student a different acceptable task. It has four parts:
\begin{itemize}
\item a full assignment for the graph of Exercise~\ref{ex:9.2}, presented so that a reader can check it;
\item a graph with no full assignment, together with a group of students who between them accept fewer tasks than there are students in the group, which shows that no full assignment exists;
\item a complete proof, in your own words, of Hall's theorem, which says that a full assignment exists whenever every group of students, taken together, accepts at least as many tasks as the group has members;
\item a paragraph explaining why handing out tasks one at a time, without ever taking one back, can get stuck even when a full assignment exists.
\end{itemize}
Exercise~\ref{ex:9.6} states each part precisely, and its solution in Appendix~\ref{app:solb} is a model portfolio.

The final investigation is the independent capstone of Section~\ref{ch18:sec:independent}. That section lists what the write-up should contain, from a precise statement of the question to an explanation of what is new. It also describes the short oral discussion that ends the investigation: you explain one delicate step, change an assumption, and predict what fails. Section~\ref{appE:sec:rubric} suggests a grading rubric.
