\section{The sum of all correlations reveals a square}\label{ch14:sec:square}
Which lengths $n$ allow a sign sequence whose periodic correlations at all nonzero shifts are zero? A first restriction comes from adding up all the periodic correlations at once. The total turns out to depend only on the sum of the entries. Let
\[
r=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j=a_0+a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1}
\]
be the sum of the entries of $a$. Each row of the circulant matrix contains every entry of $a$ once, so $r$ is also the sum of the entries in any row, and we call it the \emph{row sum}. For $(1,1,1,-1)$, the row sum is $r=2$.

What do we get if we add up all the periodic correlations, including $C_0$? Each $C_s$ is itself a sum over $j$, so the total is a double sum of $n\cdot n=n^2$ products. A finite sum may be added in any order. So instead of adding, for each $s$, all the terms with that $s$, we may add, for each $j$, all the terms with that $j$:
\begin{align*}
\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}C_s
&=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j+s)\bmod n}
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j+s)\bmod n}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j\left(\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}a_{(j+s)\bmod n}\right)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k\right)
 =\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j\,r=r\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j=r^2.
\end{align*}
Here is what happens at each step.
\begin{itemize}
\item In the second line, $a_j$ does not depend on $s$, so it is a common factor of all the terms in the inner sum and can be pulled out in front of it.
\item The third line is the key step. Fix $j$. As $s$ runs through $0,1,\ldots,n-1$, the position $(j+s)\bmod n$ runs through $j,j+1,\ldots,n-1$ and then wraps around to $0,1,\ldots,j-1$. It visits every position exactly once. For example, with $n=4$ and $j=2$, the values $s=0,1,2,3$ give the positions $2,3,0,1$. So the inner sum is the sum of all the entries of $a$, added in a different order, and that is $r$.
\item Finally, $r$ is the same number for every $j$, so it can be pulled out of the outer sum, which leaves $r\cdot r$.
\end{itemize}
For $(1,1,1,-1)$ the identity says $4+0+0+0=2^2$. It holds for every sequence, not only for special ones. The sequence $(1,1,-1,-1)$ has row sum $r=0$ and periodic correlations $C_0=4$, $C_1=0$, $C_2=-4$, $C_3=0$, which add up to $0=0^2$.

Another way to see the whole count is to use three unknown entries $a_0,a_1,a_2$. The three periodic correlations consist of these products:
\begin{align*}
 C_0&=a_0a_0+a_1a_1+a_2a_2,\\
 C_1&=a_0a_1+a_1a_2+a_2a_0,\\
 C_2&=a_0a_2+a_1a_0+a_2a_1.
\end{align*}
Together they contain each of the nine products $a_ja_k$, with $j$ and $k$ between $0$ and $2$, exactly once. Here $a_0a_1$ and $a_1a_0$ count as two different products, because they come from different pairs $(j,k)$. Multiplying out $(a_0+a_1+a_2)(a_0+a_1+a_2)$ gives the same nine products, so the two totals agree. For general $n$, the same thing happens: each first index $j$ meets every second index exactly once as the shift varies. That is why the total is $\bigl(\sum_j a_j\bigr)^2$, and not, for example, $\sum_j a_j^2$.

The identity does not use the fact that the entries are signs. The same rearrangement works for any real numbers $a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}$. The signs are used only afterward, to know that $C_0=n$ and that the row sum is an integer. Keeping track of which hypothesis is used where tells us which parts of the argument would still work in a more general setting.

Now suppose that $C_s=0$ for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$. Then the left side of the identity is just $C_0=n$, and therefore
\[
n=r^2.
\tag{14.2}\label{eq:squareorder}
\]
Since $r$ is a sum of signs, it is an integer. So the length $n$ must be a perfect square, that is, the square of an integer. By Section~\ref{ch14:sec:circulant}, the same is true of the order of every circulant Hadamard matrix. In the language of Section~\ref{ch02:sec:implication}, being a perfect square is a \emph{necessary} condition for the order of a circulant Hadamard matrix.

Notice what kind of statement this is. The argument never used a particular value of $n$, so it holds for every length at once. No list of computed examples, however long, could give a conclusion of that kind.

\pauseq{Which lengths $n$ with $1\le n\le13$ pass the square test? Has one of them already been ruled out by another argument?}{The perfect squares in this range are $1$, $4$, and $9$.
