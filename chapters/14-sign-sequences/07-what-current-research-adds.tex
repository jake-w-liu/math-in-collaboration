\section{What current research adds}\label{ch14:sec:research}
The question of this chapter is a well-known research problem. The \emph{circulant Hadamard conjecture}, usually attributed to H.~J.~Ryser, states that there is no circulant Hadamard matrix of order greater than $4$. In the language of this chapter: apart from the lengths $1$ and $4$, no sign sequence has all of its periodic correlations at nonzero shifts equal to zero. The conjecture has been open since the 1960s. Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} shows that a counterexample would need an order $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$. Over the decades, researchers have proved much stronger restrictions on the possible orders, but none of these results rules out all of them.

Barker sequences have a related history. Since the early 1960s it has been known that a Barker sequence of odd length has length at most $13$. In our table, the odd lengths that have Barker sequences are exactly $1$, $3$, $5$, $7$, $11$, and $13$.

For even lengths, the Barker question turns into a question about circulant Hadamard matrices. It has long been known that when $n>2$ is even, every Barker sequence of length $n$ also has $C_s=0$ for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$, so its circulant matrix is a Hadamard matrix. We do not prove this here, but you can check one instance of it at length four. The eight Barker sequences of length four are $(1,1,1,-1)$, its three other rotations, and the negatives of these four. They are also the eight sequences counted in the last column. The same result explains the zeros in the Barker column at the even lengths $6$, $8$, $10$, and $12$. A Barker sequence of one of these lengths would be the first row of a circulant Hadamard matrix of that order, and by Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} such an order must be $1$ or of the form $4u^2$, that is, one of $1,4,16,36,\ldots$. None of the four lengths is on this list. In the same way, a Barker sequence of even length greater than $4$ would give a circulant Hadamard matrix of order greater than $4$. So the circulant Hadamard conjecture, if true, also rules out such sequences, and whether they exist has likewise been open for decades.

In September 2026, a preprint claimed a proof of the circulant Hadamard conjecture. (Recall from Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour} that a preprint is a research paper made public before a journal has reviewed it.) It was released in OpenAI's public mathematics repository, together with a formalization in Lean~\cite{oai179}. Lean is a \emph{proof assistant}: a computer program that checks every step of a proof written in its own precise language. A \emph{formalization} of a theorem is a version of its statement and proof written in such a language, so that the program can check it. Chapters~\ref{ch:lean1} and~\ref{ch:lean2} introduce Lean.

According to the repository's description, the formalized statement says that a real circulant Hadamard matrix of positive order $n$ exists exactly when $n=1$ or $n=4$. The word ``real'' separates the matrices of this chapter, whose entries are the real numbers $1$ and $-1$, from a related family with complex entries that researchers also study. A second formalized statement, described there as a consequence, says that a sign sequence of positive even length whose aperiodic correlations at all nonzero shifts have absolute value at most one must have length $2$ or $4$. In our terms, every Barker sequence of even length has length $2$ or $4$. The description also notes that the preprint's classification of Barker sequences of odd length is not part of the formalized statements.

Nothing in this chapter proves these claims. Our experiment stops at length $13$, and Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} still permits the orders $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$, that is, $16,36,64,\ldots$. What the chapter does give you is the vocabulary to read the claims precisely, and the exact list of orders that a complete proof has to rule out. Ruling them out requires far more mathematics than this course covers. When you meet a summary of a result like this, whether in a news report or from an AI assistant, first check which statement was proved: which matrices, which kind of correlation, which lengths. Chapter~\ref{ch:bridge} returns to this example and describes how to begin reading a proof whose background you do not yet have.
