\section{What current research adds}\label{ch14:sec:research}
The question of this chapter is a famous research problem. The \emph{circulant Hadamard conjecture}, usually attributed to H.~J.~Ryser and open since the 1960s, states that there is no circulant Hadamard matrix of order greater than $4$: apart from the lengths $1$ and $4$, no sign sequence has all of its periodic correlations at nonzero shifts equal to zero. Researchers have since proved restrictions on the possible orders much stronger than Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary}, but none rules out all of them.

Barker sequences have a related history. Since the early 1960s it has been known that a Barker sequence of odd length has length at most $13$; in our table, the odd lengths with Barker sequences are exactly $1$, $3$, $5$, $7$, $11$, and $13$.

For even lengths, the Barker question becomes a question about circulant Hadamard matrices. It has long been known that when $n>2$ is even, every Barker sequence of length $n$ also has $C_s=0$ for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$, so its circulant matrix is a Hadamard matrix. We do not prove this, but you can check it at length four: the eight Barker sequences of length four ($(1,1,1,-1)$, its three other rotations, and the negatives of these four) are exactly the eight sequences in the last column. This result explains the zeros in the Barker column at the even lengths $6$, $8$, $10$, and $12$: a Barker sequence of such a length would be the first row of a circulant Hadamard matrix of that order, which Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} rules out because none of these lengths is $1$ or of the form $4u^2$. Likewise, a Barker sequence of even length greater than $4$ would give a circulant Hadamard matrix of order greater than $4$, so the conjecture, if true, rules out such sequences too; whether they exist has also been open for decades.

In September 2026, a preprint (a research paper made public before a journal has reviewed it, Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour}) claimed a proof of the circulant Hadamard conjecture. It was released in OpenAI's public mathematics repository, together with a formalization in Lean~\cite{oai179}. Lean, introduced in Chapters~\ref{ch:lean1} and~\ref{ch:lean2}, is a \emph{proof assistant}: a computer program that checks every step of a proof written in its own precise language. A \emph{formalization} of a theorem is a version of its statement and proof written in such a language.

According to the repository's description, the formalized statement says that a real circulant Hadamard matrix of positive order $n$ exists exactly when $n=1$ or $n=4$. The word ``real'' separates the matrices of this chapter, with entries the real numbers $\pm1$, from a related family with complex entries. A second formalized statement, described there as a consequence, says that a sign sequence of positive even length whose aperiodic correlations at all nonzero shifts have absolute value at most one, that is, a Barker sequence of even length, must have length $2$ or $4$. The description also notes that the preprint's classification of Barker sequences of odd length is not part of the formalized statements.

Nothing in this chapter proves these claims: our experiment stops at length $13$, and Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} still permits the orders $16,36,64,\ldots$, whose exclusion requires far more mathematics than this course covers. What the chapter gives you is the vocabulary to read the claims precisely, and the exact list of orders that a complete proof must rule out. When you meet a summary of a result like this, whether in a news report or from an AI assistant, first check which statement was proved: which matrices, which kind of correlation, which lengths. Chapter~\ref{ch:bridge} returns to this example and describes how to begin reading a proof whose background you do not yet have.
