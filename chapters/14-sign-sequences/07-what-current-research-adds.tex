\section{What current research adds}
The circulant matrices of this chapter lead directly to a well-known problem. The \emph{circulant Hadamard conjecture}, usually attributed to H.~J. Ryser and open since the 1960s, states that a circulant sign Hadamard matrix of order $n>4$ does not exist. In the language of this chapter: apart from the lengths $1$ and $4$, no sign sequence has all of its periodic correlations at nonzero shifts equal to zero. Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary} shows that any further example would need order $4u^2$ with $u>1$, and much stronger restrictions have been proved over the years, but until recently the conjecture remained open.

Barker sequences have a related history. It has been known since the early 1960s that a Barker sequence of odd length has length at most $13$; in our table the odd lengths that admit Barker sequences are exactly $1,3,5,7,11,13$. For even lengths, the question is tied to circulant Hadamard matrices, and it was likewise open whether any even length beyond $4$ is possible.

In September 2026 a preprint claiming a proof of the circulant Hadamard conjecture was released in OpenAI's public mathematics repository, together with a Lean formalization~\cite{oai179}. According to the repository's description, the formal statement says that a real circulant Hadamard matrix of positive order $n$ exists exactly when $n=1$ or $n=4$. A second formal statement, described there as a consequence, says that a sign sequence of positive even length whose nonzero-shift aperiodic correlations all have absolute value at most one must have length $2$ or $4$. The repository notes that the preprint's treatment of odd lengths is not part of the formalized statement.

These are claims reported by an external source. They are not established by our finite experiment, which stops at length $13$, nor by Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary}, which still permits the orders $16,36,64,\ldots$. What this chapter has given you is the ability to read those claims precisely: you know what a circulant matrix is, what row orthogonality means in terms of correlations, and exactly which cases an elementary argument leaves open. Ruling out the orders $4u^2$ with $u>1$ requires substantially more mathematics. Chapter~17 discusses how to approach that additional background without pretending that a short elementary argument has already supplied it.
