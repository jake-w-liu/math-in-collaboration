\section{Periodic correlation is a different quantity}\label{ch14:sec:periodic}
Instead of letting entries fall off the end, we can write the $n$ entries around a circle, like the hours on a clock face, and rotate the copy by $s$ places. Now a position that runs past the end of the list comes back in at the start: the position $j+s$ is replaced by its remainder on division by $n$, written $(j+s)\bmod n$ (Section~\ref{ch05:sec:euclid}). For $a=(1,1,1,-1)$ and $s=1$, the picture of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:aperiodic} becomes
\[
\begin{array}{rrrr}
1&1&1&-1\\
-1&1&1&1
\end{array}
\]
The $-1$ that dropped off the end comes back in at the front, so all four columns overlap.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Periodic autocorrelation]\label{ch14:def:periodic}
Let $a$ be a sign sequence of length $n$. For each shift $s$ with $0\le s\le n-1$, the \emph{periodic autocorrelation} of $a$ at shift $s$ is
\[
C_s(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j+s)\bmod n}.
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

We write $C_s$ when the sequence is clear. Unlike $A_s$, this sum always has $n$ terms. At shift zero every entry meets itself, so $C_0=n$; this says nothing about the sequence, but we keep shift zero because the value is needed in Section~\ref{ch14:sec:square}.

For $a=(1,1,1,-1)$ and $s=1$, the last position $3+1=4$ wraps around to $4\bmod4=0$:
\[
C_1=a_0a_1+a_1a_2+a_2a_3+a_3a_0=1+1-1-1=0.
\]
In the same way,
\begin{align*}
C_2&=a_0a_2+a_1a_3+a_2a_0+a_3a_1=1-1+1-1=0,\\
C_3&=a_0a_3+a_1a_0+a_2a_1+a_3a_2=-1+1+1-1=0,
\end{align*}
and $C_0=4$. Like $A_s$, $C_s$ is the number of agreements minus the number of disagreements, so $C_s=0$ says that the sequence agrees with its rotated copy in exactly half of the $n$ positions. Sequences with this property at every nonzero shift, like this one, are what the rest of the chapter is about.

\subsection*{How the two kinds of correlation are related}
Start with $n=5$ and $s=2$. The periodic sum has five terms, and the last two are the ones that wrap around:
\[
 C_2=\underbrace{a_0a_2+a_1a_3+a_2a_4}_{A_2}
       +\underbrace{a_3a_0+a_4a_1}_{A_3}.
\]
Swapping the factors turns the last two terms into $a_0a_3+a_1a_4$, which is $A_3$. The same split works for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$. If $0\le j\le n-1-s$, then $j+s\le n-1$: nothing wraps, and these terms $a_ja_{j+s}$ are exactly those of $A_s$. If $n-s\le j\le n-1$, then $n\le j+s<2n$, so subtracting $n$ once brings the position back into the list: $(j+s)\bmod n=j+s-n$. Thus
\[
 C_s=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}a_j a_{j+s}
     +\sum_{j=n-s}^{n-1}a_j a_{j+s-n}.
\]
The first sum is $A_s$. In the second, put $k=j+s-n$, so $j=k+n-s$; the first term $j=n-s$ has $k=0$, and the last term $j=n-1$ has $k=s-1$. Substituting,
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=n-s}^{n-1}a_j a_{j+s-n}
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}a_{k+n-s}a_k\\
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}a_k a_{k+(n-s)}\\
 &=A_{n-s}.
\end{align*}
The middle step swaps the factors. The last is the definition of $A_{n-s}$: the shift $n-s$ lies between $1$ and $n-1$, and the index of $A_{n-s}$ runs from $0$ to $n-1-(n-s)=s-1$. So, for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$,
\begin{equation}
C_s=A_s+A_{n-s}.\label{eq:periodaperiod}
\end{equation}
For $(1,1,1,-1)$ this gives $C_1=A_1+A_3=1+(-1)=0$, as computed directly. But small aperiodic correlations do not make the periodic ones zero. The Barker sequence $(1,1,1,-1,1)$ has $A_1=0$, $A_2=1$, $A_3=0$, and $A_4=1$ (check them), so by \eqref{eq:periodaperiod} its periodic correlations $C_1=A_1+A_4=1$ and $C_2=A_2+A_3=1$ are not zero.

\pauseq{Explain why, when the length $n$ is odd, no periodic correlation $C_s$ can be zero.}{The sum $C_s$ has $n$ terms, each $1$ or $-1$. By the parity argument of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:aperiodic}, if $m$ terms equal $-1$, the sum is $n-2m$, which has the same parity as $n$. So when $n$ is odd, $C_s$ is odd, and therefore not zero, as for $(1,1,1,-1,1)$ above.}
