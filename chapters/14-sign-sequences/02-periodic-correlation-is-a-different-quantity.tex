\section{Periodic correlation is a different quantity}\label{ch14:sec:periodic}
There is a second natural way to compare a sequence with a shifted copy of itself. Instead of letting entries fall off the end, write the $n$ entries around a circle, like the hours on a clock face, and rotate the copy by $s$ places. Now every entry has a partner: a position that runs past the end of the list comes back in at the start. In symbols, the position $j+s$ is replaced by its remainder on division by $n$, which Section~\ref{ch05:sec:euclid} writes as $(j+s)\bmod n$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Periodic autocorrelation]\label{ch14:def:periodic}
Let $a$ be a sign sequence of length $n$. For each shift $s$ with $0\le s\le n-1$, the \emph{periodic autocorrelation} of $a$ at shift $s$ is
\[
C_s(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j a_{(j+s)\bmod n}.
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Again we write $C_s$ when the sequence is clear. Unlike $A_s$, this sum always has $n$ terms, one for each position $j$. At shift zero every entry is paired with itself, so $C_0=n$.

For $a=(1,1,1,-1)$ and $s=1$, the second positions $j+1$ are $1,2,3,4$, and the last of these wraps around to $4\bmod4=0$:
\[
C_1=a_0a_1+a_1a_2+a_2a_3+a_3a_0=1+1-1-1=0.
\]
In the same way,
\begin{align*}
C_2&=a_0a_2+a_1a_3+a_2a_0+a_3a_1=1-1+1-1=0,\\
C_3&=a_0a_3+a_1a_0+a_2a_1+a_3a_2=-1+1+1-1=0,
\end{align*}
and $C_0=4$. So every periodic correlation of this sequence at a nonzero shift is zero. Sequences with this property are what the rest of the chapter is about.

\subsection*{How the two kinds of correlation are related}
Fix a shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$. The terms of $C_s$ fall into two groups, according to whether the second position wraps around.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $0\le j\le n-1-s$, then $j+s\le n-1$. Nothing wraps, and the term is $a_ja_{j+s}$. These are exactly the terms of $A_s$.
\item If $n-s\le j\le n-1$, then $n\le j+s\le (n-1)+s<2n$. Subtracting $n$ once brings the position back into the list, so $(j+s)\bmod n=j+s-n$, and the term is $a_ja_{j+s-n}$.
\end{itemize}
Thus
\[
 C_s=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}a_j a_{j+s}
     +\sum_{j=n-s}^{n-1}a_j a_{j+s-n}.
\]
The first sum is $A_s$. To recognize the second, give its terms a new index $k=j+s-n$. The first term, $j=n-s$, has $k=0$, and the last term, $j=n-1$, has $k=s-1$. Solving for the old index gives $j=k+n-s$. Substituting,
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=n-s}^{n-1}a_j a_{j+s-n}
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}a_{k+n-s}a_k\\
 &=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}a_k a_{k+(n-s)}\\
 &=A_{n-s}.
\end{align*}
The middle step only swaps the two factors in each product. The last step is the definition of $A_{n-s}$, the aperiodic correlation at shift $n-s$. This shift lies between $1$ and $n-1$, so $A_{n-s}$ is defined, and its index runs from $0$ to $n-1-(n-s)=s-1$, exactly as in the line above it.

Here is the same split in a concrete case, $n=5$ and $s=2$. The periodic sum has five terms, and the last two are the ones that wrap around:
\[
 C_2=\underbrace{a_0a_2+a_1a_3+a_2a_4}_{A_2}
       +\underbrace{a_3a_0+a_4a_1}_{A_3}.
\]
Swapping the factors turns the last two terms into $a_0a_3+a_1a_4$, which is $A_3$. In general, for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$,
\[
C_s=A_s+A_{n-s}.
\tag{14.1}\label{eq:periodaperiod}
\]
For $(1,1,1,-1)$ this gives $C_1=A_1+A_3=1+(-1)=0$, in agreement with the direct calculation.

The two kinds of correlation are related, but they measure different things. Knowing that the aperiodic correlations are small says little about whether the periodic ones are zero. Take the Barker sequence $(1,1,1,-1,1)$ of length five. You can check that its aperiodic correlations are $A_1=0$, $A_2=1$, $A_3=0$, and $A_4=1$. By \eqref{eq:periodaperiod}, two of its periodic correlations are $C_1=A_1+A_4=1$ and $C_2=A_2+A_3=1$, and neither is zero. Equation~\eqref{eq:periodaperiod} is the bridge between the two notions, and an argument that passes from one kind of correlation to the other has to cross it explicitly.

\pauseq{Explain why, when the length $n$ is odd, no periodic correlation $C_s$ can be zero.}{The sum $C_s$ has $n$ terms, each equal to $1$ or $-1$. The parity argument of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:aperiodic} applies to any such sum: if $m$ terms equal $-1$, the sum is $n-2m$, which has the same parity as $n$. So when $n$ is odd, $C_s$ is odd, and zero is even. In particular, for odd $n\ge3$, no sign sequence of length $n$ has all of its periodic correlations at nonzero shifts equal to zero. The sequence $(1,1,1,-1,1)$ above, with $C_1=C_2=1$, is one instance of this.}
