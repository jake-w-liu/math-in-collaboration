\section{A sequence with small echoes}\label{ch14:sec:aperiodic}
Sign sequences, met in Section~\ref{ch06:sec:wlog}, are the main characters of this chapter. A \emph{sign sequence} of length $n$, where $n\ge1$, is a list $a=(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1})$ in which every entry is $1$ or $-1$. As in Python (Appendix~\ref{app:practical}), we number the positions from $0$, so the last entry is $a_{n-1}$.

Think of the sequence as a pattern of pulses, and of a copy delayed by $s$ steps as an echo. If the pattern matches its echoes only weakly, a receiver that compares what it hears with the pattern gets a strong match only at the moment the pattern arrives, which is why such sequences are used in radar and digital communication. To measure the match, write a copy shifted $s$ places to the right under the sequence and look at the positions where the two rows overlap. For $a=(1,1,1,-1)$ and $s=1$:
\[
\begin{array}{rrrrr}
1&1&1&-1&\\
 &1&1&1&-1
\end{array}
\]
In each overlapping position, the entry $a_j$ of the shifted copy stands below the entry $a_{j+s}$ of the original. Their product $a_ja_{j+s}$ is $1$ when the two entries agree and $-1$ when they differ, so adding these products gives the number of agreements minus the number of disagreements.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Aperiodic autocorrelation]\label{ch14:def:aperiodic}
Let $a$ be a sign sequence of length $n$. For each shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$, the \emph{aperiodic autocorrelation} of $a$ at shift $s$ is
\[
A_s(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-s}a_j a_{j+s}.
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

We write $A_s$ when the sequence is clear. ``Aperiodic'' means that nothing wraps around: entries pushed past the end drop off, as the last $-1$ in the picture does. Shifts of $n$ or more would leave no overlap. Shift zero is left out because there every entry meets itself, giving $a_0^2+\cdots+a_{n-1}^2=n$ for every sequence of length $n$, which tells us nothing.

\subsection*{Decode the summation limits before computing}
The term $a_ja_{j+s}$ makes sense only when both $j$ and $j+s$ lie in the list $0,1,\ldots,n-1$. So the sum starts at $j=0$ and stops when $j+s=n-1$, that is, at $j=n-1-s$, giving $(n-1-s)-0+1=n-s$ terms, one for each overlapping position. For length five and shift two,
\[
 A_2=a_0a_2+a_1a_3+a_2a_4,
\]
with $5-2=3$ terms; there is no term $a_3a_5$, because $a_5$ is not in the list. At the largest shift, $s=n-1$, a single term $a_0a_{n-1}$ remains. Note that the subscript on $A_s$ is a shift, while the subscript on $a_j$ is a position.

\subsection*{A first example, and the sequences we are looking for}
For $a=(1,1,1,-1)$ there are three shifts to check:
\begin{align*}
A_1&=a_0a_1+a_1a_2+a_2a_3=(1)(1)+(1)(1)+(1)(-1)=1,\\
A_2&=a_0a_2+a_1a_3=(1)(1)+(1)(-1)=0,\\
A_3&=a_0a_3=(1)(-1)=-1.
\end{align*}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Barker sequence]\label{ch14:def:barker}
A \emph{Barker sequence} is a sign sequence $a$ of length $n$ with $|A_s(a)|\le1$ for every shift $s$ with $1\le s\le n-1$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

So $(1,1,1,-1)$ is a Barker sequence. These sequences are named after R.~H.~Barker, who studied them in 1953. A sequence of length one has no shifts to check, so the condition holds vacuously (Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive}), and both $(1)$ and $(-1)$ are Barker sequences.

Why ``at most one'' rather than ``zero''? Because $A_s$ often cannot be zero. If $m$ of its $n-s$ terms equal $-1$, the other $n-s-m$ equal $1$, and
\[
A_s=(n-s-m)-m=(n-s)-2m.
\]
Subtracting the even number $2m$ does not change parity, so $A_s$ is even exactly when $n-s$ is even; in particular, $A_s$ can be zero only when $n-s$ is even. Since the only even integer with absolute value at most one is $0$, and the only odd ones are $\pm1$, a Barker sequence has $A_s=0$ whenever $n-s$ is even and $A_s=\pm1$ whenever $n-s$ is odd. Its echoes are as weak as they can possibly be; for $(1,1,1,-1)$, where $n-s=3,2,1$, the correlations are $1,0,-1$.

\pauseq{Is $(1,1,-1,-1)$ a Barker sequence?}{No. At shift two, $n-s=2$ is even, so a Barker sequence would need $A_2=0$. But $A_2=a_0a_2+a_1a_3=(1)(-1)+(1)(-1)=-2$. The correlations $A_1=1$ and $A_3=-1$ are fine, but one bad shift is enough to fail the definition.}
