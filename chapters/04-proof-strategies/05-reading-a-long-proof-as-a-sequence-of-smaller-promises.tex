\section{Reading a long proof as a sequence of smaller promises}\label{ch04:sec:promises}
Long proofs are usually assembled from shorter results: lemmas, propositions and theorems (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:roles}). Each works like a small promise: supply its hypotheses, and it delivers its conclusion. So whenever a proof uses an earlier result, ask three questions. What does the result need? Why does the proof have it at this point? What does the result give back?

Here is that check for the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sqrt2}, which uses one starting fact once and one lemma twice.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.19\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Result used & What it needs & Why it holds here & What it gives\\
\midrule
Well-ordering property & a nonempty set of positive integers & the assumed fraction for $\sqrt2$, with both signs changed if necessary, supplies a positive denominator & a smallest positive denominator $b$\\
Lemma~\ref{thm:parity-square} & an integer whose square is even & $a$ is an integer, and $a^2=2b^2$ is twice the integer $b^2$ & $a$ is even\\
Lemma~\ref{thm:parity-square} again & an integer whose square is even & $b$ is an integer, and $b^2=2c^2$ is twice the integer $c^2$ & $b$ is even\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The third column must be checked, not assumed. A sentence such as ``by a standard result, $a$ is even'' makes this impossible: the reader cannot tell which result is meant, so cannot see what it needs. A careful proof names the result and shows that its hypotheses hold.

\begin{principle}{Habit 4: ask for a local contract, not just more words}
Habit~2 (Section~\ref{sec:magnitude}) asked for the contract of a whole statement. When a single step of a proof (your own, a book's, or an assistant's) is hard to follow, ask for the contract of that step. What is known at this point? What is the next goal? Which exact result is used, and why do its hypotheses hold here? Which quantity is constructed, and from what? These questions locate the missing mathematics better than a request for a ``simpler'' explanation.
\end{principle}

For example, take this step from the proof of Lemma~\ref{ch04:lem:even-or-odd}: ``If $m-1=2k+1$ for an integer $k$, then $m=2k+2=2(k+1)$, so $m$ is even.'' Known: $m-1=2k+1$, where $k$ is an integer. Next goal: $m$ is even or odd. Result used: none; Definition~\ref{def:parity} alone says that $m$ is even once it is twice an integer. Quantity constructed: the witness $k+1$, an integer because $k$ is. With these four answers written down, each part of the step can be checked on its own.
