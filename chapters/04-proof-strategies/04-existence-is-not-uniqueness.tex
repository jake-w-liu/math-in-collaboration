\section{Existence is not uniqueness}\label{ch04:sec:unique}
The equation $x^2=1$ has real solutions, but not a \emph{unique} one, that is, exactly one. It has exactly two, $1$ and $-1$: both square to $1$, and there are no others, because a solution makes $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ equal to zero, and a product of real numbers is zero only when at least one factor is zero. In general, ``there is a unique solution'' makes two separate claims:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{existence}: at least one number satisfies the condition;
\item \emph{uniqueness}: any two numbers that satisfy the condition are equal, so there is at most one.
\end{itemize}
This is the split of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}, where surjectivity gave every element of the codomain at least one preimage and injectivity at most one. Each claim needs its own argument.

Consider the equation
\[
x=\frac{x+1}{2},
\]
where $x$ is a real number. \emph{Existence} is settled by a witness: $1$ is a solution, because $(1+1)/2=1$.

For \emph{uniqueness}, suppose that real numbers $x$ and $y$ both satisfy the equation:
\[
x=\frac{x+1}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=\frac{y+1}{2}.
\]
Subtract the second equation from the first. The constant terms $1$ cancel, leaving
\[
x-y=\frac{x-y}{2}.
\]
Multiplying by two gives $2(x-y)=x-y$, and subtracting $x-y$ gives $x-y=0$, so $x=y$. Together, the two parts show that the equation has exactly one real solution, namely $1$.

The uniqueness argument never solves the equation: it takes two hypothetical solutions and shows that they cannot differ. This can be simpler than solving, because whatever the two equations share, such as the constant terms here, cancels on subtraction. Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction} turns this comparison into the uniqueness part of the contraction theorem, starting from the same equation $x=(x+1)/2$.

A uniqueness argument on its own proves only that there is \emph{at most one} solution, which is also true of an equation with no solutions. Take the equation $2x=1$, where $x$ must be an integer. If integers $x$ and $y$ satisfy $2x=1$ and $2y=1$, then $2(x-y)=0$, so $x=y$. The argument is correct, yet there is no integer solution: the only real solution, $1/2$, is not an integer. That is why the existence check cannot be skipped.

Be careful, too, with the phrase ``the solution'': the word ``the'' claims that there is exactly one. A proof that begins ``let $x$ be the solution of $x^2=1$'' has already gone wrong, since a conclusion such as ``so $x$ is positive'' may hold for one solution and fail for the other. Until existence and uniqueness are both established, say what you are really assuming, for example ``suppose $x$ is a solution'' or ``if a solution exists, then \ldots.''
