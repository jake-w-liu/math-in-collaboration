\section{Two directions of attention}\label{ch04:sec:directions}
Every proof has a \emph{target}, the statement it must establish, and a supply of facts it is allowed to use: the hypotheses, the definitions, and results proved earlier. When you do not know what to write next, there are two places to look. \emph{Working backward} starts from the target and asks: what would be enough to establish this? \emph{Working forward} starts from the facts and asks: what follows from what I already have? A proof is found when the two directions meet, that is, when something you can derive from the facts is exactly what working backward told you would be enough.

The target often tells you what to produce, once you unpack the definitions in it. To show that an integer is even, you must find an integer $w$ such that the number equals $2w$ (Definition~\ref{def:parity}). To show that a function $f$ is injective, you take two inputs $x$ and $y$ with $f(x)=f(y)$, and you must deduce that $x=y$ (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}). To show that an object with some property exists, the usual way is to name a candidate and check that it has the property; as in Chapter~\ref{ch:start}, such a candidate is called a witness. Occasionally existence is proved without naming any witness, by showing that if no suitable object existed, something impossible would follow. Chapter~\ref{ch:probability} contains proofs of this kind. In each case, recognizing what the target asks you to produce is a large part of finding the proof.

\subsection*{A proof state on paper}
Here is a small example. We prove:
\begin{center}
\emph{If $n$ is an even integer, then $n^2$ is even.}
\end{center}
Section~\ref{ch02:sec:implication} proved this in one line. This time we slow down to watch how such a proof is found. At each moment of the search, we keep two lists: the facts we already have, and what we still have to show. Together, these two lists are called the \emph{proof state}. Proof assistants such as Lean display exactly this pair after every step (Chapter~\ref{ch:lean1}), and keeping it on paper is just as useful.

At the start, the only fact is the hypothesis, ``$n$ is an even integer,'' and the target is ``$n^2$ is even.'' Working backward, we unpack the target with the definition of even. It becomes: \emph{find an integer $w$ with $n^2=2w$.} The letter $w$ is new, because we have not found this integer yet; finding it is the job. Working forward, we unpack the hypothesis in the same way: $n=2k$ for some integer $k$. Squaring gives $n^2=4k^2$, and factoring out a two gives $n^2=2(2k^2)$. This has exactly the shape the target asks for, so we choose $w=2k^2$. It is an integer, because a product of integers is an integer. Only now does the target move from the ``still to show'' list to the ``known'' list. The table records the search.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Step & Known & Still to show\\
\midrule
Start & $n$ is an even integer & $n^2$ is even\\
Unpack the target & $n$ is an even integer & an integer $w$ with $n^2=2w$\\
Unpack the hypothesis & $n=2k$, where $k$ is an integer & an integer $w$ with $n^2=2w$\\
Square and factor & $n=2k$ and $n^2=2(2k^2)$ & an integer $w$ with $n^2=2w$\\
Choose $w=2k^2$ & $n^2=2w$, and $w$ is an integer & nothing: the proof is finished\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The search moved in both directions, but the written proof runs forward only:
\begin{quote}
Let $n$ be an even integer. Then $n=2k$ for some integer $k$, so $n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)$. Since $2k^2$ is an integer, $n^2$ is even.
\end{quote}
A proof like this one, which starts from the hypotheses and reaches the conclusion through a chain of deductions, is called a \emph{direct proof}. The rest of this chapter shows other ways to organize a proof when a direct chain is hard to find.

Keeping the target on the ``still to show'' list until it has been earned protects against a common mistake: writing ``since $n^2$ is even, $n^2=2w$ for some integer $w$'' in the middle of the proof. That sentence uses the conclusion as though it were already known, so the argument goes in a circle. Working backward is a good way to search, because asking what would be enough is how we found $w$. In the finished proof, however, every fact must come from the hypotheses, from definitions, or from earlier results.

\pauseq{Let $f:\ZZ\to\ZZ$ be given by $f(n)=n+3$. To prove that $f$ is surjective, what does the target ask you to produce? Find it by working backward, and then check it by working forward.}{By the definition of surjective (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}), the target is: for each integer $b$, find an integer $a$ with $f(a)=b$, that is, $a+3=b$. Working backward from this equation suggests $a=b-3$. Working forward, $b-3$ is an integer, because a difference of integers is an integer, and $f(b-3)=(b-3)+3=b$. So $b-3$ is a witness for every target $b$, and $f$ is surjective.}
