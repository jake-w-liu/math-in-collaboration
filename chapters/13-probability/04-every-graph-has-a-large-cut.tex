\section{Every graph has a large cut}\label{ch13:sec:cut}
Suppose a class is to be split between two rooms so that as many pairs of chatty students as possible end up in different rooms. In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}, with the students as vertices and the chatty pairs as edges, we want to divide the vertices into two groups with many edges running between them.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Cut]\label{ch13:def:cut}
Let $V$ be the vertex set of a graph. A \emph{cut} of the graph is a pair of sets $L$ and $R$ with $L\cup R=V$ and $L\cap R=\varnothing$, so that every vertex lies in exactly one of them. An edge \emph{crosses} the cut when one of its endpoints lies in $L$ and the other in $R$. The \emph{size} of the cut is the number of crossing edges.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

The letters $L$ and $R$ suggest ``left'' and ``right''; one of them may be empty. In the example graph $G$ of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:graph}, with edges $ab$, $bc$, $ca$, and $cd$, the cut $L=\{a,d\}$, $R=\{b,c\}$ has size $3$, since only $bc$ does not cross, and the cut $L=\{a,b,c,d\}$, $R=\varnothing$ has size $0$. No cut of $G$ has size $4$, since then $ab$, $bc$, and $ca$ would all cross; but if $ab$ and $bc$ cross, then $a$ and $c$ both lie on the side opposite to $b$, hence on the same side, and $ca$ does not cross.

In a small graph we can find a largest cut by trying every possibility. But each vertex can go to either side, so a graph with $100$ vertices has $2^{100}=(2^{10})^{10}>(10^3)^{10}=10^{30}$ cuts, and no fast method is known that finds a largest cut in every graph. The following theorem asks for less, and an averaging argument proves it in a few lines.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cut}
Every graph with $m$ edges has a cut of size at least $m/2$.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

\noindent\textit{Plan.} Put each vertex on the left or on the right by tossing a fair coin for it. Count the crossing edges with indicators, find their expected number by linearity, and use the fact that some outcome is at least the average.
\begin{proof}
Let $V$ be the vertex set, $E$ the edge set, and $n=|V|$. We use the model of independent fair labels from Section~\ref{ch13:sec:model}: an outcome is a labeling that gives each vertex the label $0$ or $1$, and each of the $2^n$ labelings has probability $1/2^n$. Each labeling determines a cut: $L$ is the set of vertices labeled $0$, and $R$ is the set of vertices labeled $1$.

Fix an edge $e=uv$, and let $A_e$ be the event that $e$ crosses the cut, that is, that $u$ and $v$ get different labels. Since $u$ and $v$ are different vertices, the rule for independent fair labels with $k=2$ shows that $u$ and $v$ get the labels $0,1$ with probability $1/4$, and the labels $1,0$ with probability $1/4$. These two events are disjoint and make up $A_e$, so $\Prob(A_e)=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12$.

Let $X$ be the size of the cut, viewed as a random variable on the labelings. At each labeling, $X$ counts the crossing edges, so
\[
X=\sum_{e\in E}I_{A_e}.
\]
By linearity of expectation and $\E I_{A_e}=\Prob(A_e)$,
\[
\E X=\sum_{e\in E}\Prob(A_e)=m\cdot\frac12=\frac m2.
\]
By Proposition~\ref{ch13:prop:above-average}, some labeling satisfies $X\ge\E X=m/2$. The cut determined by that labeling has at least $m/2$ crossing edges.
\end{proof}

The expected size of the cut is $m/2$, but labeling every vertex $0$ gives a cut of size $0$: the argument guarantees only that \emph{some} labeling does at least as well as the average. Confusing this with a claim about every labeling is a common mistake; watch for it whenever you, or an AI assistant, summarize such a proof.

The proof used independence only to find $\Prob(A_e)=1/2$ for a single edge; it never asked how the crossing events of different edges are related, and they can be closely linked. In the \emph{triangle}, the graph with vertices $a$, $b$, $c$ and edges $ab$, $bc$, $ca$, the three edges never all cross, as we saw for $G$, although each crosses with probability $1/2$; if each edge had a separate fair coin of its own, all three would cross with probability $1/8$. Yet the expected number of crossing edges is still $3/2$.

Since cut sizes are integers, for odd $m$ the theorem gives a cut of size at least $(m+1)/2$. For the triangle this is $2$, the best possible.
