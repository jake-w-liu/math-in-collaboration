\section{Expectation is a weighted average}\label{ch13:sec:expectation}
Often we care about a number computed from the outcome: how many coins show $1$, the score of the student who was picked, how many edges of a graph have some property.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Random variable and expectation]\label{ch13:def:expectation}
A \emph{random variable} on a finite probability space is a function $X:\Omega\to\RR$. Its \emph{expectation}, or \emph{expected value}, is the number
\[
\E X=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)X(\omega).
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Despite its name, a random variable is an ordinary function, in the sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:maps}; the word ``random'' refers to the way its input is chosen. The expectation is a \emph{weighted average} of the values of $X$, in which the probabilities act as \emph{weights}: they are nonnegative and add up to $1$, and a more likely outcome counts for more. In a uniform model every weight is $1/|\Omega|$, so
\[
\E X=\frac1{|\Omega|}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}X(\omega),
\]
the ordinary average of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:pigeonhole}. From now on we often write $\sum_\omega$ for $\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}$. A random variable that takes the same value $c$ at every outcome is called \emph{constant}, and its expectation is $\sum_\omega p(\omega)c=c\sum_\omega p(\omega)=c$.

For two coins, let $X$ count how many coins show $1$. Then $X(00)=0$, $X(01)=X(10)=1$, and $X(11)=2$, so
\[
\E X=\tfrac14\cdot0+\tfrac14\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2=1.
\]
The two middle terms could have been combined into $\tfrac12\cdot1$, and this works in general. Write $\Prob(X=x)$ for the probability of the event $\{\omega\in\Omega:X(\omega)=x\}$. If the different values of $X$ are $x_1,\ldots,x_k$, the terms $p(\omega)X(\omega)$ with $X(\omega)=x_i$ have the common factor $x_i$, and the probabilities left over add up to $\Prob(X=x_i)$. Grouping the terms in this way gives
\[
\E X=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_i\,\Prob(X=x_i).
\]
For two coins this is $0\cdot\tfrac14+1\cdot\tfrac12+2\cdot\tfrac14=1$, the calculation we would make in the space $\{0,1,2\}$ of Section~\ref{ch13:sec:model}. Because of this formula, a random variable is often described just by listing its values and their probabilities; any finite probability space that produces these values with these probabilities gives the same expectation.

The expectation need not be a value of the variable. Toss one fair coin, and let $Z$ be $0$ if the coin shows $0$ and $2$ if it shows $1$. Then $\E Z=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot2=1$, but $Z$ never equals $1$: the expectation summarizes the values, but does not predict the one you will see.

\subsection*{Linearity}
Random variables on the same space are added, and multiplied by numbers, one outcome at a time: $X+Y$ has the value $X(\omega)+Y(\omega)$ at $\omega$, and for a real number $c$, $cX$ has the value $cX(\omega)$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Linearity of expectation]\label{ch13:prop:linearity}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on the same finite probability space, and let $c$ be a real number. Then
\[
\E(X+Y)=\E X+\E Y\qquad\text{and}\qquad\E(cX)=c\,\E X.
\]
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Multiply out each term, and then split the finite sum into two sums:
\begin{align*}
\E(X+Y)&=\sum_\omega p(\omega)\bigl(X(\omega)+Y(\omega)\bigr)\\
&=\sum_\omega\bigl(p(\omega)X(\omega)+p(\omega)Y(\omega)\bigr)\\
&=\sum_\omega p(\omega)X(\omega)+\sum_\omega p(\omega)Y(\omega)=\E X+\E Y.
\end{align*}
Splitting is allowed because a finite sum may be added up in any order. For the second identity, take the common factor $c$ out of every term: $\E(cX)=\sum_\omega p(\omega)cX(\omega)=c\sum_\omega p(\omega)X(\omega)=c\,\E X$.
\end{proof}

By induction on $m$, the first identity extends to any finite number of random variables on the same space:
\[
\E(X_1+\cdots+X_m)=\E X_1+\cdots+\E X_m;
\]
for the induction step, write $X_1+\cdots+X_m=(X_1+\cdots+X_{m-1})+X_m$ and apply the proposition.

For example, the results $X_1$ and $X_2$ of two coins each equal $1$ at two of the four outcomes, so $\E X_1=\E X_2=1/2$, and their sum, the number of coins showing $1$, has expectation $1/2+1/2=1$, as found above.

The proof never asked how $X$ and $Y$ are related, so linearity holds even when they are closely linked. Toss one fair coin, let $X$ be its result, and let $Y=1-X$, so that $X$ determines $Y$. Still, $X+Y$ is the constant $1$, so $\E(X+Y)=1=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=\E X+\E Y$. This is what makes linearity so useful: to find the expectation of a complicated count, split it into simple pieces and add their expectations, without working out how the pieces affect one another.

\subsection*{Comparing expectations}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity of expectation]\label{ch13:prop:monotone}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on the same finite probability space. If $X(\omega)\le Y(\omega)$ for every outcome $\omega$, then $\E X\le\E Y$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
For each outcome $\omega$, multiply the inequality $X(\omega)\le Y(\omega)$ by $p(\omega)$. Since $p(\omega)\ge0$, the inequality keeps its direction: $p(\omega)X(\omega)\le p(\omega)Y(\omega)$. Adding these inequalities over all outcomes gives $\E X\le\E Y$.
\end{proof}

An inequality that holds at every outcome, like the hypothesis $X(\omega)\le Y(\omega)$, is called \emph{pointwise}; this proposition is what justifies ``taking expectations of both sides'' of it. The reverse step is not allowed: $\E X\le\E Y$ does not make $X\le Y$ pointwise. In the one-coin example with $Y=1-X$, $\E X=\E Y=1/2$, but when the coin shows $1$ we have $X=1$ and $Y=0$.

\subsection*{Some outcome is at least the average}
By the averaging principle (Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:average}), among $n\ge1$ real numbers at least one is at least their average and at least one is at most it. The same is true for a weighted average.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch13:prop:above-average}
Let $X$ be a random variable on a finite probability space. Then some outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$ satisfies $X(\omega)\ge\E X$, and some outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$ satisfies $X(\omega)\le\E X$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that $X(\omega)<\E X$ for every outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$. There is at least one such outcome, because the probabilities add up to $1$ and so cannot all be $0$. Multiplying each of these strict inequalities by its positive weight gives $p(\omega)X(\omega)<p(\omega)\,\E X$, while for an outcome with $p(\omega)=0$ both sides are $0$. Now add over all outcomes. At least one of the inequalities being added is strict, so the total is strict (if $x<y$ and $x'\le y'$, then $x+x'<y+x'\le y+y'$):
\[
\E X=\sum_{\omega}p(\omega)X(\omega)<\sum_{\omega}p(\omega)\,\E X=\E X\cdot\sum_\omega p(\omega)=\E X,
\]
which is impossible. The second claim is proved in the same way, with every inequality reversed.
\end{proof}

The condition $p(\omega)>0$ makes sure that the outcome found can actually occur; an outcome of probability $0$ contributes nothing to $\E X$, so the expectation carries no information about it. For the variable $Z$ above, $\E Z=1$, and only the outcome where the coin shows $1$ has $Z\ge\E Z$: the proposition promises at least one such outcome and nothing more, and, like the pigeonhole principle, its proof does not tell us which.
