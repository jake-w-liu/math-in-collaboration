\section{Expectation is a weighted average}\label{ch13:sec:expectation}
Often we care less about the outcome itself than about a number computed from it: how many coins show $1$, the score of the student who was picked, how many edges of a graph have some property.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Random variable and expectation]\label{ch13:def:expectation}
A \emph{random variable} on a finite probability space is a function $X:\Omega\to\RR$. Its \emph{expectation}, or \emph{expected value}, is the number
\[
\E X=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)X(\omega).
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Despite its name, a random variable is an ordinary function, in the sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:maps}. The word ``random'' refers to the way its input is chosen. Once the outcome $\omega$ is known, $X(\omega)$ is an ordinary number. The expectation is a weighted average of these numbers: the value at each outcome is weighted by the probability of that outcome. In a uniform model every weight is $1/|\Omega|$, so
\[
\E X=\frac1{|\Omega|}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}X(\omega),
\]
which is the ordinary average of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:pigeonhole}, taken over all the outcomes. A random variable that takes the same value $c$ at every outcome is called \emph{constant}, and its expectation is $\sum_\omega p(\omega)c=c\sum_\omega p(\omega)=c$.

For the two-coin experiment, let $X$ count how many coins show $1$. Its values are $X(00)=0$, $X(01)=1$, $X(10)=1$, and $X(11)=2$, so
\[
\E X=\tfrac14\cdot0+\tfrac14\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2=1.
\]
The two middle terms have the same value of $X$, and we could have combined them into the single term $\tfrac12\cdot1$. Combining terms in this way works in general. For a number $x$, write $\Prob(X=x)$ for the probability of the event $\{\omega\in\Omega:X(\omega)=x\}$. If the different values of $X$ are $x_1,\ldots,x_k$, group the terms of the sum defining $\E X$ according to the value of $X$. The group for the value $x_i$ is the sum of the terms $p(\omega)x_i$ over the outcomes with $X(\omega)=x_i$. Taking out the common factor $x_i$ leaves the sum of the probabilities of these outcomes, so the group equals $x_i\Prob(X=x_i)$. Therefore
\[
\E X=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_i\,\Prob(X=x_i).
\]
For the two coins this reads $\E X=0\cdot\tfrac14+1\cdot\tfrac12+2\cdot\tfrac14=1$. This is also the calculation we would make in the non-uniform space $\{0,1,2\}$ of Section~\ref{ch13:sec:model}, which records only the number of coins showing $1$ and describes the same tosses. Because of this formula, a random variable is often described just by listing its values and their probabilities, as in ``a random variable that equals $0$ or $2$, each with probability $1/2$.'' Any finite probability space that produces these values with these probabilities gives the same expectation.

In the two-coin example, the expectation $1$ happens to be one of the values of $X$. It need not be. Toss one fair coin, and let $Z$ be $0$ if the coin shows $0$ and $2$ if it shows $1$. Then $\E Z=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot2=1$, but $Z$ never equals $1$. The expectation summarizes all the values; it does not predict the value you will see.

\subsection*{Linearity}
Random variables on the same space can be added, and multiplied by numbers, one outcome at a time. The sum $X+Y$ is the random variable whose value at $\omega$ is $X(\omega)+Y(\omega)$; both $X$ and $Y$ are evaluated at the same outcome. For a real number $c$, the random variable $cX$ has the value $cX(\omega)$ at $\omega$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Linearity of expectation]\label{ch13:prop:linearity}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on the same finite probability space, and let $c$ be a real number. Then
\[
\E(X+Y)=\E X+\E Y\qquad\text{and}\qquad\E(cX)=c\,\E X.
\]
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Multiply out each term, and then split the finite sum into two sums:
\begin{align*}
\E(X+Y)&=\sum_\omega p(\omega)\bigl(X(\omega)+Y(\omega)\bigr)\\
&=\sum_\omega\bigl(p(\omega)X(\omega)+p(\omega)Y(\omega)\bigr)\\
&=\sum_\omega p(\omega)X(\omega)+\sum_\omega p(\omega)Y(\omega)=\E X+\E Y.
\end{align*}
Splitting is allowed because a finite sum may be added up in any order. For the second identity, take the common factor $c$ out of every term: $\E(cX)=\sum_\omega p(\omega)cX(\omega)=c\sum_\omega p(\omega)X(\omega)=c\,\E X$.
\end{proof}

Applying the first identity repeatedly (formally, by induction on $m$) gives
\[
\E(X_1+\cdots+X_m)=\E X_1+\cdots+\E X_m
\]
for any finite number of random variables on the same space. For example, with two coins let $X_1$ be the result of the first coin and $X_2$ the result of the second. Each of them equals $1$ at two of the four outcomes, so $\E X_1=\E X_2=1/2$. Their sum $X_1+X_2$ is the number of coins showing $1$, and its expectation is $1/2+1/2=1$, as we found directly above.

The proof never asked how $X$ and $Y$ are related, and linearity holds even when they are as closely linked as possible. Toss one fair coin, let $X$ be its result, and let $Y=1-X$, so that $Y$ is $1$ exactly when $X$ is $0$. Knowing $X$ tells us $Y$ completely. Still, $X+Y$ is the constant $1$, so $\E(X+Y)=1=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=\E X+\E Y$. This is what makes linearity so useful: to find the expected value of a complicated count, we can split the count into simple pieces and add their expectations, without first working out how the pieces affect one another.

\subsection*{Comparing expectations}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity of expectation]\label{ch13:prop:monotone}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on the same finite probability space. If $X(\omega)\le Y(\omega)$ for every outcome $\omega$, then $\E X\le\E Y$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
For each outcome $\omega$, multiply the inequality $X(\omega)\le Y(\omega)$ by $p(\omega)$. Since $p(\omega)\ge0$, the inequality keeps its direction: $p(\omega)X(\omega)\le p(\omega)Y(\omega)$. Adding these inequalities over all outcomes gives $\E X\le\E Y$.
\end{proof}

An inequality that holds at every outcome separately, like the hypothesis $X(\omega)\le Y(\omega)$ here, is called a \emph{pointwise} inequality. Monotonicity says that a pointwise inequality survives averaging. Whenever we ``take expectations of both sides'' of an inequality, this proposition is the justification. The reverse step is not allowed: $\E X\le\E Y$ does not imply that $X\le Y$ pointwise. In the one-coin example with $Y=1-X$ above, $\E X=\E Y=1/2$, but when the coin shows $1$ we have $X=1$ and $Y=0$.

\subsection*{Some outcome is at least the average}
The averaging principle (Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:average}) says that among finitely many numbers, at least one is at least their average and at least one is at most their average. An expectation is a weighted average, and the same is true for it.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch13:prop:above-average}
Let $X$ be a random variable on a finite probability space. Then some outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$ satisfies $X(\omega)\ge\E X$, and some outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$ satisfies $X(\omega)\le\E X$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that $X(\omega)<\E X$ for every outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$. There is at least one such outcome, because the probabilities add up to $1$ and so cannot all be $0$. Multiply each of these strict inequalities by its positive weight, to get $p(\omega)X(\omega)<p(\omega)\,\E X$. An outcome with $p(\omega)=0$ gives $0$ on both sides instead. Now add over all outcomes. At least one of the inequalities being added is strict, so the total is strict (if $x<y$ and $x'\le y'$, then $x+x'<y+x'\le y+y'$). This gives
\[
\E X=\sum_{\omega}p(\omega)X(\omega)<\sum_{\omega}p(\omega)\,\E X=\E X\cdot\sum_\omega p(\omega)=\E X,
\]
so $\E X<\E X$, which is impossible. The second claim is proved in the same way, with every inequality reversed.
\end{proof}

The proposition asks for an outcome with $p(\omega)>0$, so that the outcome it finds is one that can actually occur. An outcome of probability $0$ contributes nothing to $\E X$, so the expectation carries no information about it.

In the two-coin example, $\E X=1$, and the outcomes $01$, $10$, and $11$ all have $X\ge1$. In the one-coin example with values $0$ and $2$, $\E Z=1$, and only the outcome in which the coin shows $1$ has $Z\ge1$. The proposition promises at least one such outcome and nothing more. Like the pigeonhole principle, its proof shows that a good outcome exists without telling us which one it is. Section~\ref{ch13:sec:cut} uses the proposition to prove that a certain object exists, and Section~\ref{ch13:sec:construction} shows how, in that example, a good outcome can actually be found.
