\section{A finite probability model}\label{ch13:sec:model}
When we toss a coin or pick a student at random, we cannot predict the result. Probability does not try to: it lists everything that could happen, gives each possibility a weight, and calculates. In this chapter the list of possibilities is always finite, so every calculation is a finite sum.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Finite probability space]\label{ch13:def:space}
A \emph{finite probability space} consists of a finite set $\Omega$, whose members are called \emph{outcomes}, together with a number $p(\omega)$ for each outcome $\omega\in\Omega$, called the \emph{probability} of $\omega$, such that
\[
p(\omega)\ge0\quad\text{for every }\omega\in\Omega,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)=1.
\]
The set $\Omega$ is called the \emph{sample space}. An \emph{event} is a subset $A\subseteq\Omega$, and the \emph{probability} of the event $A$ is
\[
\Prob(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega).
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

The symbols $\Omega$ and $\omega$ are the capital and lowercase Greek letter omega. As in Section~\ref{ch07:sec:double}, $\sum_{\omega\in A}$ means that we add one term for each member $\omega$ of $A$. A sum with no terms is $0$ (Section~\ref{ch05:sec:first-induction}), so $\Prob(\varnothing)=0$, and the sample space cannot be empty, since the probabilities add up to $1$.

Think of the experiment as producing exactly one outcome; an event $A$ \emph{occurs} when that outcome belongs to $A$. So $\Omega$ always occurs, and $\Prob(\Omega)=1$, while $\varnothing$ never occurs.

In the simplest models, called \emph{uniform}, every outcome has the same probability. These $|\Omega|$ equal probabilities add up to $1$, so each is $1/|\Omega|$, and for every event $A$
\[
\Prob(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|},
\]
because the sum defining $\Prob(A)$ has $|A|$ terms, each equal to $1/|\Omega|$. A member of a finite set is chosen \emph{uniformly at random} when that set is the sample space and the model is uniform; for example, if one student is chosen uniformly at random from a class of $30$, each student is chosen with probability $1/30$.

\subsection*{Two coins}
Toss two fair coins and record each result as $1$ (heads) or $0$ (tails). Write an outcome as two digits, the result of the first coin followed by the result of the second. The sample space is
\[
\Omega=\{00,\,01,\,10,\,11\}.
\]
Because the coins are fair, we use the uniform model: each outcome has probability $1/4$. For example, the event ``the two results differ'' is $\{01,10\}$, with probability $2/4=1/2$.

\subsection*{Which outcomes are equally likely?}
The outcomes $01$ and $10$ are different. It is tempting to merge them into ``one of each'' and to give each possible number $0$, $1$, $2$ of coins showing $1$ the probability $1/3$, but this is a mistake: ``one of each'' covers two of the four equally likely outcomes. We may still use the three counts as outcomes, with the right weights: the sample space $\{0,1,2\}$ with
\[
p(0)=\tfrac14,\qquad p(1)=\tfrac12,\qquad p(2)=\tfrac14,
\]
describes the same two tosses, and is a finite probability space that is not uniform. Giving each count the probability $1/3$ also produces a finite probability space, but not one that describes two fair coins. A probability model is a piece of mathematics, and whether it fits a real experiment is a separate question, which no calculation inside the model can answer.

\subsection*{Disjoint events and complements}
Recall from Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets} that two sets are \emph{disjoint} when they have no member in common. If two events $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, every outcome of $A\cup B$ lies in exactly one of them, so the sum defining $\Prob(A\cup B)$ splits into a sum over $A$ and a sum over $B$:
\[
\Prob(A\cup B)=\Prob(A)+\Prob(B).
\]

The \emph{complement} of an event $A$ is the event $\Omega\setminus A$ of the outcomes not in $A$; it occurs exactly when $A$ does not. Since $A$ and $\Omega\setminus A$ are disjoint and their union is $\Omega$,
\[
\Prob(A)+\Prob(\Omega\setminus A)=\Prob(\Omega)=1.
\]
Consequently, if $\Prob(A)<1$, then $\Prob(\Omega\setminus A)=1-\Prob(A)>0$. An event of positive probability contains at least one outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$, since otherwise its probability would be a sum of zeros, which is $0$. Together these give a small but important rule: \emph{if $\Prob(A)<1$, then some outcome of positive probability lies outside $A$.}

\subsection*{Independent fair labels}
Several experiments in this chapter give a label, $0$ or $1$, to each of $n$ objects, as if a fair coin were tossed for each object. An outcome is then a list of $n$ labels. There are $2^n$ such lists, by the reasoning that counted the groups of students in Section~\ref{ch09:sec:condition}, and we give every list the same probability $1/2^n$. The two-coin example is the case $n=2$.

Now fix $k$ of the objects and a label for each of them. A list giving these $k$ objects their chosen labels may have any labels at the other $n-k$ objects, so there are $2^{n-k}$ such lists, and the probability that the $k$ objects carry the chosen labels is
\[
\frac{2^{n-k}}{2^n}=\frac1{2^k}=\underbrace{\frac12\cdot\frac12\cdots\frac12}_{k\text{ factors}}.
\]
So each object has each label with probability $1/2$ (the case $k=1$), and the probabilities for different objects multiply. For example, with $n=3$, the lists in which the first object has label $0$ and the third has label $1$ are $001$ and $011$, so this happens with probability $2/8=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$. This is what we mean when we say that the labels are chosen \emph{independently and fairly}.

In general, two events $A$ and $B$ are called \emph{independent} when $\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(A)\Prob(B)$. For example, with two coins, the events $A=\{10,11\}$ (the first coin shows $1$) and $B=\{00,10\}$ (the second shows $0$) are independent, since $\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(\{10\})=\tfrac14=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\Prob(A)\Prob(B)$.
