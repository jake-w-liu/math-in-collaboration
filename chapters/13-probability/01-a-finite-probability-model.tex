\section{A finite probability model}\label{ch13:sec:model}
When we toss a coin or pick a student at random, we cannot predict the result. Probability does not try to predict it. Instead, it lists everything that could happen, gives each possibility a weight, and then calculates. In this chapter the list of possibilities is always finite, so every calculation is a finite sum.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Finite probability space]\label{ch13:def:space}
A \emph{finite probability space} consists of a finite set $\Omega$, whose members are called \emph{outcomes}, together with a number $p(\omega)$ for each outcome $\omega\in\Omega$, called the \emph{probability} of $\omega$, such that
\[
p(\omega)\ge0\quad\text{for every }\omega\in\Omega,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)=1.
\]
The set $\Omega$ is called the \emph{sample space}. An \emph{event} is a subset $A\subseteq\Omega$, and the \emph{probability} of the event $A$ is
\[
\Prob(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega).
\]
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

The symbol $\Omega$ is the Greek capital letter omega, and $\omega$ is the lowercase omega. They are simply the traditional names for the sample space and for one of its outcomes. As in Section~\ref{ch07:sec:double}, the notation $\sum_{\omega\in A}$ means that we add one term for each member $\omega$ of $A$. Since the probabilities of all the outcomes add up to $1$, while a sum with no terms is $0$ (Section~\ref{ch05:sec:first-induction}), the sample space cannot be empty.

Think of the experiment as producing exactly one outcome. An event $A$ \emph{occurs} when that outcome belongs to $A$. Two events are extreme cases. The whole sample space is an event that always occurs, and $\Prob(\Omega)=1$. The empty set is an event that never occurs, and $\Prob(\varnothing)=0$, again because a sum with no terms is $0$.

In the simplest models, called \emph{uniform}, every outcome has the same probability. Since there are $|\Omega|$ outcomes and their probabilities add up to $1$, each outcome then has probability $1/|\Omega|$, and for every event $A$
\[
\Prob(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|},
\]
because the sum defining $\Prob(A)$ has $|A|$ terms, each equal to $1/|\Omega|$. In a uniform model, finding a probability is a counting problem: count the outcomes in the event and divide by the number of all outcomes. When we say that a member of a finite set is chosen \emph{uniformly at random}, we mean that this set is the sample space and that the model is uniform. For example, if one student is chosen uniformly at random from a class of $30$, each student is chosen with probability $1/30$.

\subsection*{Two coins}
Toss two fair coins, a first one and a second one, and record each result as $0$ or $1$ (say $1$ for heads and $0$ for tails). Write an outcome as two digits: the result of the first coin followed by the result of the second. The sample space is
\[
\Omega=\{00,\,01,\,10,\,11\}.
\]
Because the coins are fair, we use the uniform model, in which each outcome has probability $1/4$. The event ``the two results differ'' is $\{01,10\}$, so its probability is $2/4=1/2$. The event ``at least one coin shows $1$'' is $\{01,10,11\}$, with probability $3/4$. We will use this example throughout the chapter.

\subsection*{Which outcomes are equally likely?}
The outcomes $01$ and $10$ are different. In $01$ the first coin shows $0$ and the second shows $1$; in $10$ it is the other way round. It is tempting to merge them into a single description, ``one of each,'' and to argue that the number of coins showing $1$ is $0$, $1$, or $2$, so that each of these has probability $1/3$. This is a mistake. The description ``one of each'' covers two of the four equally likely outcomes, so its probability is $2/4=1/2$, while ``two $0$s'' and ``two $1$s'' have probability $1/4$ each.

We may still use the three descriptions as outcomes, provided we give them the right weights. The sample space $\{0,1,2\}$, which records only the number of coins showing $1$, with
\[
p(0)=\tfrac14,\qquad p(1)=\tfrac12,\qquad p(2)=\tfrac14,
\]
describes the same two tosses. It is a finite probability space that is not uniform. Giving each of $0$, $1$, $2$ the probability $1/3$ also produces a finite probability space, but it describes a different experiment, not two tosses of fair coins.

A probability model is a piece of mathematics: we choose the outcomes and their probabilities, and everything else follows by calculation. Whether a model fits a real coin, a real survey, or a real game is a separate question, and no calculation inside the model can answer it.

\subsection*{Disjoint events and complements}
Recall from Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets} that two sets are \emph{disjoint} when they have no member in common. If two events $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, then every outcome of $A\cup B$ lies in exactly one of them. So the sum defining $\Prob(A\cup B)$ splits into a sum over $A$ and a sum over $B$, and
\[
\Prob(A\cup B)=\Prob(A)+\Prob(B).
\]
With two coins, for example, the events $\{00\}$ and $\{01,10\}$ are disjoint, and their union $\{00,01,10\}$, the event that at most one coin shows $1$, has probability $1/4+1/2=3/4$.

The \emph{complement} of an event $A$ is the event $\Omega\setminus A$, consisting of the outcomes that are not in $A$. It occurs exactly when $A$ does not. The events $A$ and $\Omega\setminus A$ are disjoint and their union is $\Omega$, so the rule just proved gives
\[
\Prob(A)+\Prob(\Omega\setminus A)=\Prob(\Omega)=1.
\]
In the two-coin example, the complement of ``at least one coin shows $1$'' is $\{00\}$, and indeed $3/4+1/4=1$.

Consequently, if $\Prob(A)<1$, then $\Prob(\Omega\setminus A)=1-\Prob(A)>0$. An event of positive probability contains at least one outcome $\omega$ with $p(\omega)>0$: if it had no outcomes, or if all of its outcomes had probability $0$, its probability would be a sum of zeros, which is $0$. Putting these two facts together gives a small but important rule: \emph{if $\Prob(A)<1$, then some outcome of positive probability lies outside $A$.} Section~\ref{ch13:sec:indicators} uses this rule to show that some outcome avoids several unwanted events at once.

\subsection*{Independent fair labels}
Several experiments in this chapter give one of two labels, $0$ or $1$, to each of $n$ objects, as if a fair coin were tossed separately for each object. An outcome is then a list of $n$ labels, one for each object. Each object has two possible labels, so there are $2\cdot2\cdots2=2^n$ such lists; this is the same reasoning that counted the groups of students in Section~\ref{ch09:sec:condition}. We give every list the same probability $1/2^n$. The two-coin example is the case $n=2$.

Now pick some $k$ of the objects, and decide in advance which label each of them should get. A list in which these $k$ objects carry the chosen labels may have any labels at the other $n-k$ objects, so there are $2^{n-k}$ such lists. Hence the probability that the $k$ objects carry the chosen labels is
\[
\frac{2^{n-k}}{2^n}=\frac1{2^k}=\underbrace{\frac12\cdot\frac12\cdots\frac12}_{k\text{ factors}}.
\]
For $k=1$, this says that each object has label $0$ with probability $1/2$ and label $1$ with probability $1/2$. For larger $k$, it says that the probabilities for different objects multiply. For example, take $n=3$ objects, and ask for the first object to have label $0$ and the third to have label $1$. The lists that do this are $001$ and $011$, since the second label is free. There are $2^{3-2}=2$ of them, so the probability is $2/8=1/4=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$. This is what we mean when we say that the labels are chosen \emph{independently and fairly}.

In general, two events $A$ and $B$ are called \emph{independent} when $\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(A)\Prob(B)$. With two coins, for example, let $A=\{10,11\}$ be the event that the first coin shows $1$, and let $B=\{00,10\}$ be the event that the second coin shows $0$. Each has probability $1/2$. Their intersection is $A\cap B=\{10\}$, so $\Prob(A\cap B)=1/4=(1/2)(1/2)$, and the two events are independent.
