\section{Turning the average argument into a construction}\label{ch13:sec:construction}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut} shows that a good labeling exists, but it does not tell us how to find one, and trying all $2^n$ labelings is out of the question for large graphs. Instead, we can build a good labeling one vertex at a time. At each step we choose a side so that a certain score never goes down. The score is the expected final size of the cut, computed as if the remaining vertices were going to be placed by tossing coins.

\subsection*{The score of a partial placement}
Suppose some of the vertices have already been placed on the left or on the right, and the others have not been placed yet. Call an edge \emph{settled} when both of its endpoints have been placed, and \emph{unsettled} otherwise. Let $C$ be the number of settled edges that cross (that is, whose endpoints were placed on different sides), and let $U$ be the number of unsettled edges. Define
\[
F=C+\frac U2.
\]
Equivalently, $F$ is a sum with one term for each edge: a settled edge that crosses contributes $1$, a settled edge that does not cross contributes $0$, and an unsettled edge contributes $\tfrac12$.

Here is where this formula comes from. Imagine that each vertex not yet placed were now put on the left or on the right by tossing a fair coin, independently for different vertices. A settled edge already crosses or already does not, whatever the coins show. An unsettled edge would cross with probability $1/2$. If one endpoint has been placed, the edge crosses exactly when the coin for the other endpoint sends it to the opposite side, which happens with probability $1/2$. If neither endpoint has been placed, the edge crosses exactly when the two coins disagree, which happens with probability $1/2$, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut}. By linearity of expectation, the expected size of the final cut would be $C+U/2=F$.

We will never actually toss any coins. The construction only needs the formula $F=C+U/2$, which we can compute at every stage. Before any vertex is placed, every edge is unsettled, so $C=0$, $U=m$, and $F=m/2$. After every vertex has been placed, every edge is settled, so $U=0$ and $F=C$ is the size of the cut we have built. So if we can place the vertices one at a time without ever decreasing $F$, the final cut will have size at least $m/2$.

\subsection*{Placing one vertex}
Let $v$ be the next vertex to be placed. Suppose $v$ has $\ell$ neighbors that have already been placed on the left and $r$ neighbors that have already been placed on the right. Sort the edges that have $v$ as an endpoint into three kinds, and see how the contribution of each one to $F$ changes if we place $v$ on the left.
\begin{itemize}
\item The $\ell$ edges joining $v$ to placed neighbors on the left were unsettled, so each contributed $1/2$. Afterwards they are settled, with both endpoints on the left, so they do not cross and contribute $0$.
\item The $r$ edges joining $v$ to placed neighbors on the right also contributed $1/2$ each. Afterwards they are settled and cross, so each contributes $1$.
\item The edges joining $v$ to vertices that have not been placed remain unsettled, and still contribute $1/2$ each.
\end{itemize}
Edges that do not have $v$ as an endpoint are not affected at all. So placing $v$ on the left changes $F$ by
\[
r\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac12\Bigr)+\ell\cdot\Bigl(0-\frac12\Bigr)=\frac{r-\ell}{2}.
\]
In the same way, placing $v$ on the right changes $F$ by $(\ell-r)/2$. These two changes are negatives of each other, so at least one of them is nonnegative. This gives a simple rule: \emph{put $v$ on the side opposite to the majority of its placed neighbors}, that is, on the right if $\ell>r$ and on the left if $r>\ell$; if $\ell=r$, either side will do. With this choice $F$ changes by $|\ell-r|/2\ge0$, so it never decreases.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch13:prop:greedy}
Place the vertices of a graph with $m$ edges one at a time, in any order, putting each vertex on the side opposite to the majority of its already placed neighbors, and on either side in case of a tie. The resulting cut has size at least $m/2$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Before any vertex is placed, $F=m/2$. By the calculation above, each placement made according to the rule changes $F$ by $|\ell-r|/2\ge0$, so $F$ never decreases, and after the last placement $F\ge m/2$. At that point every edge is settled, so $U=0$ and $F=C$ is the size of the cut. Hence the cut has size at least $m/2$.
\end{proof}

This is a deterministic construction: it makes no random choices, and it always produces a cut of size at least $m/2$. It is also fast. To place $v$, we only need to look at the $\deg(v)$ neighbors of $v$, and by the handshake theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:handshake}) these numbers add up to $2m$ over all the vertices. For a large graph this is far less work than checking $2^n$ labelings. The idea of keeping track of the expected value while the choices are made one at a time is called the \emph{method of conditional expectations}. An expected value computed with the choices made so far held fixed, and only the remaining choices left to chance, is called a \emph{conditional expectation}; here it is simply $F$.

\subsection*{A full trace on the triangle}
Take the triangle with vertices $a$, $b$, $c$ and edges $ab$, $bc$, $ca$, and place the vertices in the order $a$, $b$, $c$. Initially nothing is placed, so $C=0$, $U=3$, and $F=3/2$.

Vertex $a$ has no placed neighbors, so $\ell=r=0$, and either side will do; put $a$ on the left. No edge has both endpoints placed yet, so $C=0$, $U=3$, and $F$ stays $3/2$.

Vertex $b$ has one placed neighbor on the left, namely $a$, and none on the right: $\ell=1$, $r=0$. Put $b$ on the right. Edge $ab$ is now settled and crosses, while $bc$ and $ca$ remain unsettled. Thus $C=1$, $U=2$, and $F=1+2/2=2$. The increase is $(\ell-r)/2=1/2$, as predicted.

Vertex $c$ has one placed neighbor on each side, $a$ on the left and $b$ on the right, so $\ell=r=1$, and either choice changes $F$ by $0$. Put $c$ on the left. Now $bc$ crosses and $ca$ does not, so $C=2$, $U=0$, and $F=2$. The final cut is $L=\{a,c\}$, $R=\{b\}$, of size $2$, which by Section~\ref{ch13:sec:cut} is the largest possible size for a cut of the triangle.

\subsection*{The construction need not find a largest cut}
On the triangle the construction found a largest cut, but it does not always do so. Take the graph with vertices $a,b,c,d$ and edges $ab$, $bc$, $cd$, whose three edges form the path $a,b,c,d$. Place the vertices in the order $a,d,b,c$, and break ties by choosing the left side. Neither $a$ nor $d$ has a placed neighbor when its turn comes, so both go to the left. Then $b$ has its placed neighbor $a$ on the left, so it goes to the right. Finally $c$ has one placed neighbor on each side, $b$ on the right and $d$ on the left, so it goes to the left. The result is $L=\{a,c,d\}$ and $R=\{b\}$, a cut of size $2$, since $ab$ and $bc$ cross and $cd$ does not. The cut $L=\{a,c\}$, $R=\{b,d\}$ is better: all three edges cross it.
