\section{Turning the average argument into a construction}\label{ch13:sec:construction}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut} shows that a good labeling exists, but it does not tell us how to find one. Trying all $2^n$ labelings is hopeless for large graphs: a graph with $100$ vertices has $2^{100}$ labelings, which is more than $10^{30}$. Instead, we can build a good labeling one vertex at a time. At each step we choose a side so that a certain score never goes down. The score is the expected final size of the cut, computed as if the remaining vertices were going to be placed by tossing coins.

\subsection*{The score of a partial placement}
Suppose some of the vertices have already been placed on the left or on the right, and the others have not been placed yet. Call an edge \emph{settled} when both of its endpoints have been placed, and \emph{unsettled} otherwise. Let $C$ be the number of settled edges that cross (that is, whose endpoints were placed on different sides), and let $U$ be the number of unsettled edges. Define
\[
F=C+\frac U2.
\]

Here is where this formula comes from. Imagine that each vertex not yet placed were now put on the left or on the right by tossing a fair coin, independently for different vertices. A settled edge already crosses or already does not, whatever the coins show. An unsettled edge would cross with probability $1/2$. If one endpoint has been placed, the edge crosses exactly when the coin for the other endpoint sends it to the opposite side, which happens with probability $1/2$. If neither endpoint has been placed, the edge crosses exactly when the two coins disagree, which happens with probability $1/2$, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut}. By linearity of expectation, the expected size of the final cut would be $C+U/2=F$. In other words, $F$ is the expected final size of the cut, given the choices made so far.

We will never actually toss any coins. The construction only needs the formula $F=C+U/2$, which we can compute at every stage. Before any vertex is placed, every edge is unsettled, so $C=0$, $U=m$, and $F=m/2$. After every vertex has been placed, every edge is settled, so $U=0$ and $F=C$ is the size of the cut we have built. So if we can place the vertices one at a time without ever decreasing $F$, the final cut will have size at least $m/2$.

\subsection*{Placing one vertex}
Let $v$ be the next vertex to be placed. Suppose $v$ has $\ell$ neighbors that have already been placed on the left and $r$ neighbors that have already been placed on the right. Sort the edges that have $v$ as an endpoint into three kinds, and see how the contribution of each one to $F$ changes if we place $v$ on the left.
\begin{itemize}
\item The $\ell$ edges joining $v$ to placed neighbors on the left were unsettled, so each contributed $1/2$. Afterwards they are settled, with both endpoints on the left, so they do not cross and contribute $0$.
\item The $r$ edges joining $v$ to placed neighbors on the right also contributed $1/2$ each. Afterwards they are settled and cross, so each contributes $1$.
\item The edges joining $v$ to vertices that have not been placed remain unsettled, and still contribute $1/2$ each.
\end{itemize}
Edges that do not have $v$ as an endpoint are not affected at all. So placing $v$ on the left changes $F$ by
\[
r\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac12\Bigr)+\ell\cdot\Bigl(0-\frac12\Bigr)=\frac{r-\ell}{2}.
\]
In the same way, placing $v$ on the right changes $F$ by $(\ell-r)/2$. These two changes are negatives of each other, so at least one of them is nonnegative. This gives a simple rule: \emph{if more of the placed neighbors of $v$ are on the left, put $v$ on the right; if more are on the right, put $v$ on the left; if the numbers are equal, either side will do.} In short, put $v$ on the side opposite to the majority of its placed neighbors. With this choice $F$ changes by $|\ell-r|/2\ge0$, so it never decreases.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch13:prop:greedy}
Place the vertices of a graph with $m$ edges one at a time, in any order, putting each vertex on the side opposite to the majority of its already placed neighbors, and on either side in case of a tie. The resulting cut has size at least $m/2$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
The score $F$ starts at $m/2$, never decreases, and at the end equals the size of the cut.
\end{proof}

This is a deterministic construction: it makes no random choices, and it always produces a cut of size at least $m/2$. It is also fast. To place $v$, we only need to look at the $\deg(v)$ neighbors of $v$, and by the handshake theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:handshake}) these numbers add up to $2m$ over all the vertices. For a large graph this is far less work than checking $2^n$ labelings. The idea of following the expected value as the choices are made one at a time is called the \emph{method of conditional expectations}; the expected value given the choices made so far is called a conditional expectation, and here it is simply $F$. Every step of the calculation is visible, so no further probability theory is needed.

The construction guarantees $m/2$ crossing edges, but it does not always find a largest cut. On the graph with vertices $a,b,c,d$ and edges $ab$, $bc$, $cd$, placing the vertices in the order $a,d,b,c$ and breaking ties by choosing the left side gives $L=\{a,c,d\}$ and $R=\{b\}$, a cut of size $2$. The cut $L=\{a,c\}$, $R=\{b,d\}$ is better: all three edges cross it.

\subsection*{A full trace on the triangle}
Take the triangle with vertices $a$, $b$, $c$ and edges $ab$, $bc$, $ca$, and place the vertices in the order $a$, $b$, $c$. Initially nothing is placed, so $C=0$, $U=3$, and $F=3/2$.

Vertex $a$ has no placed neighbors, so $\ell=r=0$, and either side will do; put $a$ on the left. No edge has both endpoints placed yet, so $C=0$, $U=3$, and $F$ stays $3/2$.

Vertex $b$ has one placed neighbor on the left, namely $a$, and none on the right: $\ell=1$, $r=0$. Put $b$ on the right. Edge $ab$ is now settled and crosses, while $ac$ and $bc$ remain unsettled. Thus $C=1$, $U=2$, and $F=1+2/2=2$. The increase is $(\ell-r)/2=1/2$, as predicted.

Vertex $c$ has one placed neighbor on each side, $a$ on the left and $b$ on the right, so $\ell=r=1$, and either choice changes $F$ by $0$. Put $c$ on the left. Now $bc$ crosses and $ac$ does not, so $C=2$, $U=0$, and $F=2$. The final cut is $L=\{a,c\}$, $R=\{b\}$, of size $2$, which by Section~\ref{ch13:sec:cut} is the largest possible size for a cut of the triangle.

Notice how the three quantities behaved. The count $C$ of settled crossing edges never went down, and the count $U$ of unsettled edges never went up. The score $F$ combines them, and the rule kept it from decreasing: it was $3/2$ at the start and after placing $a$, then $2$ after placing $b$ and after placing $c$. At the end nothing was left unsettled, and $F$ was no longer an expectation about choices still to be made but the actual size of the cut.
