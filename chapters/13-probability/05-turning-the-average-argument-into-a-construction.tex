\section{Turning the average argument into a construction}\label{ch13:sec:construction}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut} does not tell us how to find a good labeling. Instead of searching through all $2^n$ labelings, we can build one a vertex at a time, choosing each side so that a certain score never goes down: the expected final size of the cut if the remaining vertices were placed at random.

\subsection*{The score of a partial placement}
Suppose some of the vertices have been placed on the left or on the right, and the others not yet. Call an edge \emph{settled} when both of its endpoints have been placed, and \emph{unsettled} otherwise. Let $C$ be the number of settled edges that cross, let $U$ be the number of unsettled edges, and define
\[
F=C+\frac U2.
\]
So $F$ has one term for each edge: $1$ for a settled edge that crosses, $0$ for a settled edge that does not, and $\tfrac12$ for an unsettled edge.

To see where this formula comes from, imagine that each vertex not yet placed were now placed by tossing a fair coin, independently. A settled edge crosses or not, whatever the coins show, while an unsettled edge would cross with probability $1/2$: if one endpoint has been placed, it crosses when the coin sends the other endpoint to the opposite side, and if neither has, when the two coins disagree, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut}. By linearity, the expected size of the final cut would be $C+U/2=F$. We never actually toss these coins; we only compute $F$.

\subsection*{Placing one vertex}
Let $v$ be the next vertex to be placed, with $\ell$ neighbors already placed on the left and $r$ on the right. If we place $v$ on the left, the edges at $v$ change their contributions to $F$ as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item The $\ell$ edges to placed neighbors on the left go from $1/2$ to $0$: they become settled and do not cross.
\item The $r$ edges to placed neighbors on the right go from $1/2$ to $1$: they become settled and cross.
\item The edges to vertices not yet placed stay unsettled, and still contribute $1/2$ each.
\end{itemize}
Other edges are not affected. So placing $v$ on the left changes $F$ by
\[
r\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac12\Bigr)+\ell\cdot\Bigl(0-\frac12\Bigr)=\frac{r-\ell}{2},
\]
and, in the same way, placing $v$ on the right changes $F$ by $(\ell-r)/2$. These two changes are negatives of each other, so one of them is nonnegative. This gives a simple rule: \emph{put $v$ on the side opposite to the majority of its placed neighbors}, that is, on the right if $\ell>r$ and on the left if $r>\ell$; if $\ell=r$, either side will do. With this choice $F$ changes by $|\ell-r|/2\ge0$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch13:prop:greedy}
Place the vertices of a graph with $m$ edges one at a time, in any order, putting each vertex on the side opposite to the majority of its already placed neighbors, and on either side in case of a tie. The resulting cut has size at least $m/2$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Before any vertex is placed, every edge is unsettled, so $F=m/2$. By the calculation above, each placement made according to the rule changes $F$ by $|\ell-r|/2\ge0$, so after the last placement $F\ge m/2$. At that point every edge is settled, so the size of the cut is $C=F\ge m/2$.
\end{proof}

This construction makes no random choices, and it is fast: placing $v$ needs only its $\deg(v)$ neighbors, and by the handshake theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:handshake}) these numbers add up to $2m$, far less work than checking $2^n$ labelings. An expected value computed with the choices made so far held fixed, and only the remaining choices left to chance, is called a \emph{conditional expectation}; here it is $F$. Keeping it from decreasing while the choices are made one at a time is the \emph{method of conditional expectations}.

\subsection*{A full trace on the triangle}
Place the vertices of the triangle in the order $a$, $b$, $c$. Initially $C=0$, $U=3$, and $F=3/2$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Vertex $a$ has no placed neighbors, so $\ell=r=0$; put $a$ on the left. No edge is settled yet, so $F$ stays $3/2$.
\item Vertex $b$ has $a$ on the left, so $\ell=1$ and $r=0$; put $b$ on the right. Now $ab$ is settled and crosses, so $C=1$, $U=2$, and $F=2$, an increase of $(\ell-r)/2=1/2$, as predicted.
\item Vertex $c$ has $a$ on the left and $b$ on the right, so $\ell=r=1$, and either side leaves $F$ unchanged; put $c$ on the left. Now $bc$ crosses and $ca$ does not, so $C=2$, $U=0$, and $F=2$.
\end{itemize}
The final cut $L=\{a,c\}$, $R=\{b\}$ has size $2$, which by Section~\ref{ch13:sec:cut} is the largest possible for the triangle.

\subsection*{The construction need not find a largest cut}
Take the path with vertices $a,b,c,d$ and edges $ab$, $bc$, $cd$, place the vertices in the order $a,d,b,c$, and break ties by choosing the left side. Neither $a$ nor $d$ has a placed neighbor, so both go to the left; then $b$ has $a$ on the left, so it goes to the right; finally $c$ has one placed neighbor on each side, so it goes to the left. The resulting cut $L=\{a,c,d\}$, $R=\{b\}$ has size $2$, since $cd$ does not cross, while all three edges cross the cut $L=\{a,c\}$, $R=\{b,d\}$.
