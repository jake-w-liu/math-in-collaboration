\section{A bound on rare large values}\label{ch13:sec:markov}
The expectation also limits how often a quantity that is never negative can be much larger than its average. A random variable $X$ is \emph{nonnegative} when $X(\omega)\ge0$ for every outcome $\omega$. For a real number $a$, we write $\Prob(X\ge a)$ for the probability of the event $\{\omega\in\Omega:X(\omega)\ge a\}$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Markov's inequality]\label{ch13:prop:markov}
Let $X$ be a nonnegative random variable on a finite probability space, and let $a>0$ be a real number. Then
\[
\Prob(X\ge a)\le\frac{\E X}{a}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Split the sum defining the expectation into the outcomes where $X\ge a$ and the rest:
\[
\E X=\sum_{\omega:\,X(\omega)\ge a}p(\omega)X(\omega)+\sum_{\omega:\,X(\omega)<a}p(\omega)X(\omega).
\]
In the first sum $X(\omega)\ge a$ and $p(\omega)\ge0$, so $p(\omega)X(\omega)\ge p(\omega)a$ for each term, and the sum is at least
\[
\sum_{\omega:\,X(\omega)\ge a}p(\omega)\,a=a\,\Prob(X\ge a).
\]
In the second sum every term is nonnegative, because $p(\omega)\ge0$ and $X(\omega)\ge0$, so the second sum is at least $0$. Together, $\E X\ge a\,\Prob(X\ge a)$, and dividing by the positive number $a$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

For example, suppose that the scores on a test are all nonnegative and that the class average is $20$. If $X$ is the score of a student chosen uniformly at random, then $\E X=20$, and $\Prob(X\ge80)$ is the fraction of the class that scored $80$ or more. Markov's inequality says that this fraction is at most $20/80=1/4$: if more than a quarter of the class had scored at least $80$, those students alone would contribute more than $\tfrac14\cdot80=20$ to the average, and the others at least $0$, so the average would exceed $20$.

The bound need not be exact. For the number $X$ of coins showing $1$ in the two-coin experiment, $\E X=1$, so Markov's inequality with $a=2$ gives $\Prob(X\ge2)\le1/2$, while in fact $\Prob(X\ge2)=\Prob(\{11\})=1/4$. Exercise~\ref{ex:13.6} asks for a case in which the bound is exact.

In terms of multiples of the average: if $\E X>0$ and $t>0$, taking $a=t\,\E X$ gives
\[
\Prob(X\ge t\,\E X)\le\frac{\E X}{t\,\E X}=\frac1t.
\]
So a nonnegative quantity with a positive average is at least twice its average with probability at most $1/2$, and at least ten times its average with probability at most $1/10$.

The hypothesis that $X$ is nonnegative is needed, since otherwise negative values can balance positive ones. A random variable equal to $100$ or $-100$, each with probability $1/2$, has expectation $0$, yet $\Prob(X\ge100)=1/2>0/100$. The proof used the hypothesis to make the second sum at least $0$; here that sum is $-50$.

\begin{principle}{Habit 8: choose a quantity whose average is easier to find than its best value}
When an object with a desired property is hard to construct directly, set up a finite random experiment whose outcomes are candidate objects. Count the features you want, or want to avoid, with indicators, and find an average you can compute exactly; linearity often makes this easy even when the features interact. Then say precisely what you have proved. Three conclusions are easy to confuse: that a suitable outcome exists, that a random outcome is unlikely to be bad, and that a suitable object can be built efficiently. The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cut} reached only the first. The second comes from a small upper bound on the probability of a bad outcome, such as the union bound or Markov's inequality can give, and the third needed the construction of Section~\ref{ch13:sec:construction}.
\end{principle}
