\section{Indicators convert events into quantities}\label{ch13:sec:indicators}
Indicators connect events, which are sets of outcomes, with random variables, which assign numbers to outcomes. The \emph{indicator} of an event $A$ is the random variable $I_A$ given by
\[
I_A(\omega)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\omega\in A,\\0&\text{if }\omega\notin A.\end{cases}
\]
So $I_A$ is $1$ when $A$ occurs and $0$ when it does not. Its expectation is the probability of $A$:
\[
\E I_A=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)\cdot1+\sum_{\omega\notin A}p(\omega)\cdot0=\Prob(A).
\]

Indicators are useful for counting. For events $A_1,\ldots,A_m$, the sum $I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}$ adds $1$ at a given outcome for each event that occurs, so it counts how many of the events occur. By linearity of expectation, and because $\E I_{A_j}=\Prob(A_j)$,
\[
\E\bigl(I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}\bigr)=\Prob(A_1)+\cdots+\Prob(A_m).
\]
This holds even when the events overlap.

\subsection*{The union bound}
The union $A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m$ is the event that at least one of the $A_j$ occurs. Its indicator records only \emph{whether} some event occurs, not \emph{how many} do. To compare it with the count, let $A$ and $B$ be the events that the first coin and the second coin show $1$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
outcome & $I_A$ & $I_B$ & $I_A+I_B$ & $I_{A\cup B}$\\
\midrule
$00$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$\\
$01$ & $0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$10$ & $1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$11$ & $1$ & $1$ & $2$ & $1$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At every outcome $I_{A\cup B}\le I_A+I_B$, strictly at $11$, where both events occur. Taking expectations gives $\Prob(A\cup B)=3/4\le1=\Prob(A)+\Prob(B)$.

The same holds for any number of events. Where none of them occurs, both sides of
\[
I_{A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m}\le I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}
\]
are $0$; where at least one occurs, the left side is $1$ and the right side is at least $1$. So this pointwise inequality holds at every outcome. Taking expectations keeps it, by monotonicity (Proposition~\ref{ch13:prop:monotone}); the left side becomes $\Prob(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m)$, since the expectation of an indicator is the probability of its event, and by linearity the right side becomes $\Prob(A_1)+\cdots+\Prob(A_m)$. This proves the following inequality.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Union bound]\label{ch13:prop:union}
For any events $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ in a finite probability space,
\[
\Prob(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m)\le\sum_{j=1}^m\Prob(A_j).
\]
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

The union bound becomes an existence theorem when we think of $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ as \emph{bad events}, the ways in which an outcome can fail to be what we want. Suppose that
\[
\Prob(A_1)+\cdots+\Prob(A_m)<1.
\]
Then the union bound gives $\Prob(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m)<1$, so by the rule of Section~\ref{ch13:sec:model}, some outcome of positive probability lies outside the union. That outcome avoids every bad event.

For example, a class has $7$ committees of $4$ students each, and a student may sit on several. Can the class be split into two teams so that every committee has members in both? Give each student the label $0$ or $1$, independently and fairly, and let $A_j$ be the bad event that the four members of committee $j$ all get the same label. By the rule for independent fair labels with $k=4$, they all get the label $0$ with probability $1/2^4=1/16$, and all get $1$ with probability $1/16$; these two events are disjoint, so $\Prob(A_j)=1/8$. The seven probabilities add up to $7/8<1$, so some labeling avoids every bad event. Put the students labeled $0$ in one team and the others in the other; then no committee lies within one team. We never needed to know who sits on which committee.

\pauseq{Suppose the probabilities of the bad events add up to exactly $1$. Does it follow that some outcome avoids them all?}{Not necessarily. With one fair coin, the bad events ``the coin shows $0$'' and ``the coin shows $1$'' have probabilities adding up to $1$, and every outcome is bad. With a sum of $1$, the union bound says only that the union has probability at most $1$, which is true of every event.}
