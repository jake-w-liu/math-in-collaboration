\section{Indicators convert events into quantities}\label{ch13:sec:indicators}
Events are sets of outcomes, while random variables assign numbers to outcomes. Indicators connect the two. The \emph{indicator} of an event $A$ is the random variable $I_A$ given by
\[
I_A(\omega)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\omega\in A,\\0&\text{if }\omega\notin A.\end{cases}
\]
So $I_A$ is $1$ when $A$ occurs and $0$ when it does not. Its expectation is the probability of $A$:
\[
\E I_A=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)\cdot1+\sum_{\omega\notin A}p(\omega)\cdot0=\Prob(A).
\]
For two coins, let $A=\{10,11\}$ be the event that the first coin shows $1$. Then $I_A$ is $1$ at $10$ and $11$ and $0$ at $00$ and $01$, so $\E I_A=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12=\Prob(A)$.

Indicators are useful for counting. Let $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ be events. At a given outcome, the sum $I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}$ adds $1$ for each event that occurs and $0$ for each event that does not, so it counts how many of the events occur. By linearity of expectation,
\[
\E\bigl(I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}\bigr)=\Prob(A_1)+\cdots+\Prob(A_m).
\]
In words, the expected number of events that occur is the sum of their probabilities. This holds whether or not the events overlap, because linearity holds for all random variables, however they are related.

\subsection*{The union bound}
The union $A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m$ is the event that at least one of the $A_j$ occurs. Its indicator records only \emph{whether} some event occurs, not \emph{how many} do. To compare the two, return to two coins, and let $A$ be the event that the first coin shows $1$ and $B$ the event that the second coin shows $1$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
outcome & $I_A$ & $I_B$ & $I_A+I_B$ & $I_{A\cup B}$\\
\midrule
$00$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$\\
$01$ & $0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$10$ & $1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$11$ & $1$ & $1$ & $2$ & $1$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At every outcome, $I_{A\cup B}\le I_A+I_B$. The inequality is strict at $11$, where both events occur. Taking expectations gives $\Prob(A\cup B)=3/4$ on the left and $\Prob(A)+\Prob(B)=1/2+1/2=1$ on the right, and indeed $3/4\le1$.

The same comparison works for any number of events. At an outcome where none of the events occurs, both $I_{A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m}$ and $I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}$ are $0$. At an outcome where at least one occurs, the indicator of the union is $1$, while the sum is at least $1$. So the pointwise inequality
\[
I_{A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m}\le I_{A_1}+\cdots+I_{A_m}
\]
holds at every outcome. Now take expectations of both sides. By monotonicity (Proposition~\ref{ch13:prop:monotone}) the inequality is kept. The left side becomes $\Prob(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m)$, because the expectation of an indicator is the probability of its event, and by linearity the right side becomes $\Prob(A_1)+\cdots+\Prob(A_m)$. This proves the following inequality.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}[Union bound]\label{ch13:prop:union}
For any events $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ in a finite probability space,
\[
\Prob(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m)\le\sum_{j=1}^m\Prob(A_j).
\]
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

The union bound becomes an existence theorem when we think of $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ as \emph{bad events}, the different ways in which an outcome can fail to be what we want. Suppose that
\[
\Prob(A_1)+\cdots+\Prob(A_m)<1.
\]
Then the union bound gives $\Prob(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m)<1$. By the rule at the end of the discussion of complements in Section~\ref{ch13:sec:model}, some outcome of positive probability lies outside the union. That outcome belongs to none of the events $A_j$: it avoids every bad event. An upper bound on probabilities has turned into a proof that a good outcome exists, and once again the proof does not say which outcome it is.

\pauseq{Suppose the probabilities of the bad events add up to exactly $1$. Does it follow that some outcome avoids them all?}{Not necessarily. Toss one fair coin, and let the bad events be ``the coin shows $0$'' and ``the coin shows $1$.'' Each has probability $1/2$, so the sum is $1$, but the union of the two events is the whole sample space, and every outcome is bad. On the other hand, with two coins the events ``the first coin shows $1$'' and ``the second coin shows $1$'' also have probabilities adding up to $1$, and the outcome $00$ avoids both. When the sum is $1$, the union bound says only that the union has probability at most $1$, which is true of every event, so it decides nothing either way. The argument needs the sum to be strictly less than $1$.}
