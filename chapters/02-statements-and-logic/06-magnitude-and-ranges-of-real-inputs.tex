\section{Magnitude and ranges of real inputs}\label{sec:magnitude}
The \emph{absolute value} $|x|$ is the distance of the real number $x$ from zero. Thus $|3|=3$, $|-3|=3$, and $|0|=0$. Explicitly, $|x|=x$ for $x\ge0$, and $|x|=-x$ for $x<0$. The second formula gives a nonnegative result because the negative of a negative number is positive.

Changing the sign of a number does not change its distance from zero, so $|-x|=|x|$. Multiplication obeys $|cx|=|c|\,|x|$. To check this rule, if both factors are nonnegative, both sides equal $cx$. If both are negative, their product is positive and $|c|\,|x|=(-c)(-x)=cx$. If one factor is positive and the other negative, the product is negative and both sides equal $-cx$. If either factor is zero, both sides are zero. Thus all sign cases are covered. We will use this scaling rule when bounding errors.

For $b\ge0$, the statement $|x|\le b$ says that $x$ lies between $-b$ and $b$, including the endpoints. Thus $|x|\le2$ means $-2\le x\le2$. Its negation, $|x|>2$, means $x<-2$ or $x>2$. This is the two-sided conclusion needed when an equation or inequality involves a square.

Squaring preserves order for nonnegative numbers. If $0\le a<b$, then both factors in
\[
 b^2-a^2=(b-a)(b+a)
\]
are positive: $b-a>0$ because $a<b$, and $b+a>0$ because $b>0$ and $a\ge0$. So $b^2-a^2>0$, that is, $a^2<b^2$. The same factorization shows that $0\le a\le b$ implies $a^2\le b^2$. But we cannot square an arbitrary signed inequality without checking this condition: $-3<2$ while $9>4$.

These facts let us solve inequalities involving squares carefully. First, $x^2=|x|^2$ for every real $x$: if $x\ge0$ this says $x^2=x^2$, and if $x<0$ it says $x^2=(-x)^2$, which is true because $(-x)(-x)=x\cdot x$. Now consider the condition $x^2>4$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $|x|>2$, apply the strict rule with $a=2$ and $b=|x|$: we get $4<|x|^2=x^2$.
\item If $|x|\le2$, apply the non-strict rule with $a=|x|$ and $b=2$: we get $x^2=|x|^2\le4$.
\end{itemize}
Together these show that $x^2>4$ holds exactly when $|x|>2$, that is, when $x<-2$ or $x>2$. For instance, $x=-3$ satisfies $x^2=9>4$ even though $x$ is not greater than $2$, while $x=1.5$ gives $x^2=2.25$, which is not greater than $4$. A tempting shortcut, ``take square roots to get $x>2$,'' loses the whole negative half of the answer.

An \emph{interval} names a range on the real line. We write $[a,b]$ for all $x$ with $a\le x\le b$, and $(a,b)$ for all $x$ with $a<x<b$. A square bracket includes its endpoint; a round bracket excludes it. The notation $[0,\infty)$ means all nonnegative reals; the infinity symbol says there is no upper endpoint, not that infinity is another real number. Parentheses may also denote an ordered pair; the surrounding sentence tells you whether we mean a range or a pair of entries.

\begin{principle}{Habit 2: keep a statement contract}
Before investigating, write the domain, hypotheses, conclusion, and order of choices. After every proposed repair or generalization, compare with that contract. Ask which assumption was weakened or strengthened and whether the new conclusion is stronger or weaker. Truth alone does not establish fidelity to the original question.
\end{principle}
