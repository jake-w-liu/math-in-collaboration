\section{An implication is a conditional promise}\label{ch02:sec:implication}
In mathematics, a \emph{statement} is a sentence that is either true or false, such as ``seven is odd'' (true) or ``$2+2=5$'' (false). A sentence such as ``$n$ is even'' becomes a statement only once a value of $n$ is fixed. A question such as ``Is $15$ odd?'' or an instruction such as ``Add $3$ to both sides'' is not a statement at all.

Our first example of an ``if \ldots, then \ldots'' statement needs one new word. An integer $n$ is \emph{divisible by} an integer $d$ when $n=dk$ for some integer $k$. For example, $20$ is divisible by four, because $20=4\cdot5$. But $6$ is not: $6=4k$ forces $k=3/2$, which is not an integer. As with even and odd numbers in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:definition}, the number $k$ is called a witness, and it must be an integer.

Now consider the statement
\begin{center}
\emph{If an integer is divisible by four, then it is even.}
\end{center}
As in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:roles}, the part after ``if'' is the \emph{hypothesis}, the condition that is assumed, and the part after ``then'' is the \emph{conclusion}, what is promised once the hypothesis holds. For an integer $n$, let $P$ stand for ``$n$ is divisible by four'' and $Q$ for ``$n$ is even.'' A statement of the form ``if $P$, then $Q$'' is called an \emph{implication}. We write it $P\Rightarrow Q$ and read it ``$P$ implies $Q$.'' Like any statement, an implication can be true or false. Our sentence makes its promise for every integer $n$ at once; Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers} introduces a symbol for ``for every.''

The promise is easy to prove. Let $n$ be an integer that is divisible by four. Then $n=4k$ for some integer $k$, so $n=2(2k)$, and $2k$ is an integer. By the definition of even (Definition~\ref{def:parity}), $n$ is even.

The implication makes no promise about an integer that is not divisible by four, which may be even, like $6$, or odd, like $7$. A common error is to run it backward. The backward statement $Q\Rightarrow P$ is called the \emph{converse} of $P\Rightarrow Q$. Here the converse says ``if an integer is even, then it is divisible by four,'' and it is false, since $6$ is even but not divisible by four.

A particular input that shows a statement about every input to be false is called a \emph{counterexample}. For an implication, it is an input for which the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false. So $6$ is a counterexample to the converse above, and one counterexample is enough, because the converse claims to hold for \emph{every} integer. An input such as $7$, which does not satisfy the hypothesis of ``divisible by four implies even,'' can never be a counterexample to it. When you ask an assistant to test a claim, ask for inputs that satisfy the hypothesis; inputs chosen at random often miss it and so test nothing.

Sometimes an implication and its converse are both true. The statement $P\Leftrightarrow Q$, read ``$P$ if and only if $Q$,'' promises both $P\Rightarrow Q$ and $Q\Rightarrow P$. Here ``$P$ if $Q$'' means $Q\Rightarrow P$, while ``$P$ only if $Q$'' says that $P$ can hold only when $Q$ holds as well, which is $P\Rightarrow Q$. For example, ``an integer is divisible by four only if it is even'' means ``divisible by four implies even.'' When $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ holds, we say that $P$ and $Q$ are \emph{equivalent}. To prove an ``if and only if'' statement, we prove the two implications separately. Here is one such statement: for every integer $n$,
\begin{center}
\emph{$n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even.}
\end{center}
One direction is a short calculation: if $n=2k$ for an integer $k$, then $n^2=2(2k^2)$, which is twice an integer. The other direction is harder, because knowing that $n^2=2w$ for an integer $w$ gives no direct way to write $n$ itself as twice an integer. Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive} describes the idea that gets around this.

\subsection*{A result applies only after its hypothesis is checked}
To use ``divisible by four implies even'' for $20$, we must first check the hypothesis, $20=4\cdot5$; quoting the implication does not supply this evidence. Suppose an assistant writes: ``$30$ is even, because every integer divisible by four is even.'' The conclusion is true, but the reason is not: $30$ is not divisible by four ($30=4k$ would force $k=15/2$), so the result says nothing about $30$. A correct reason is $30=2\cdot15$.

\subsection*{Necessary and sufficient, stronger and weaker}
In a true implication $P\Rightarrow Q$, we say that $P$ is a \emph{sufficient} condition for $Q$: knowing $P$ is enough to guarantee $Q$. We also say that $Q$ is a \emph{necessary} condition for $P$: $P$ cannot hold unless $Q$ holds too. So divisibility by four is sufficient for evenness, and evenness is necessary for divisibility by four, but not sufficient for it, as $6$ shows. When $P\Leftrightarrow Q$ holds, $P$ is both necessary and sufficient for $Q$.

When every object satisfying $P$ also satisfies $Q$, but not the other way around, we call $P$ the \emph{stronger} condition and $Q$ the \emph{weaker} one; the stronger condition demands more. Divisibility by four is stronger than evenness. Whole statements are compared in the same way: a stronger statement promises more. For instance, a statement about every integer is stronger than the same statement about positive integers only.
