\section{And, or, and not}\label{ch02:sec:connectives}
Statements can be combined into longer ones. Throughout this section, $n$ is an integer, $P$ is the statement ``$n$ is even,'' and $Q$ is the statement ``$n$ is divisible by three.'' The letters $P$ and $Q$ stand for whole statements, not for numbers.

The \emph{conjunction} $P\land Q$, read ``$P$ and $Q$,'' is true when both $P$ and $Q$ are true. The \emph{disjunction} $P\lor Q$, read ``$P$ or $Q$,'' is true when at least one of them is true, including when both are: for $n=6$, which is even and divisible by three, $P\lor Q$ is true. In everyday speech, ``tea or coffee'' often means one but not both; in mathematics, ``or'' allows both unless the author says otherwise. Finally, the \emph{negation} $\neg P$, read ``not $P$,'' is true exactly when $P$ is false.

The table works these out for four integers, one for each way in which $P$ and $Q$ can be true (T) or false (F).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ccccccc@{}}
\toprule
$n$ & $P$ & $Q$ & $P\land Q$ & $P\lor Q$ & $\neg(P\lor Q)$ & $(\neg P)\land(\neg Q)$\\
\midrule
$6$ & T & T & T & T & F & F\\
$4$ & T & F & F & T & F & F\\
$3$ & F & T & F & T & F & F\\
$5$ & F & F & F & F & T & T\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last two columns apply the same rules to the earlier columns: $\neg(P\lor Q)$ is the opposite of the $P\lor Q$ column, and $(\neg P)\land(\neg Q)$ is true only in a row where $P$ and $Q$ are both false. These two columns agree in every row:
\[
\neg(P\lor Q)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (\neg P)\land(\neg Q).
\]
In words: an integer fails to be ``even or divisible by three'' exactly when it is not even \emph{and} not divisible by three. To show that $5$ lacks this property, it is not enough to show that $5$ is not even.

By contrast, an integer fails to be ``even and divisible by three'' as soon as one of the two requirements fails: $4$ fails because it is not divisible by three, and $3$ because it is not even. In symbols,
\[
\neg(P\land Q)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (\neg P)\lor(\neg Q).
\]
These two rules are called \emph{De Morgan's laws}. The long arrow $\Longleftrightarrow$ is the ``if and only if'' of Section~\ref{ch02:sec:implication}: the statements on its two sides are true in exactly the same situations.

The table proves the first law for \emph{any} two statements $P$ and $Q$, not only for these four integers: its last two columns were filled in using only the truth values of $P$ and $Q$ in the same row, and there are only four combinations of these, one per row. The second law can be checked in the same way: each side is false only in the row where $P$ and $Q$ are both true, since only there are $\neg P$ and $\neg Q$ both false.

The same bookkeeping describes how an implication fails, for any two statements $P$ and $Q$. The implication $P\Rightarrow Q$ fails exactly when its hypothesis holds and its conclusion fails:
\[
\neg(P\Rightarrow Q)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P\land(\neg Q).
\]
This is the description of a counterexample from Section~\ref{ch02:sec:implication}. In the other three situations the implication counts as true:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
\toprule
$P$ & $Q$ & $P\Rightarrow Q$\\
\midrule
T & T & T\\
T & F & F\\
F & T & T\\
F & F & T\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last two rows surprise many people. Think of the promise ``if it rains, I will bring an umbrella.'' On a dry day the promise is not broken, whether I bring an umbrella or not. Likewise, ``if $n$ is divisible by four, then $n$ is even'' is not broken by $n=6$ or by $n=7$: neither is divisible by four, so at both values the implication is true. Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive} looks at what this means when the hypothesis can never be true.

\subsection*{Parentheses and grouping}
Parentheses show which statements belong together, and moving them can change the meaning. With $P$ and $Q$ as at the start of this section, take $n=5$: the statement $\neg(P\land Q)$ is true, because $5$ is not both even and divisible by three, but $(\neg P)\land Q$ is false, because $5$ is not divisible by three. By convention, $\neg$ applies only to the statement immediately after it, so $\neg P\land Q$ means $(\neg P)\land Q$; we usually write such parentheses anyway.
