\section{The order of choices changes the theorem}
The symbol $\forall$ means ``for every,'' and $\exists$ means ``there exists.'' Write $x\in\RR$ to say that $x$ belongs to the real numbers. Compare
\begin{align*}
&\forall x\in\RR\ \exists y\in\RR:\quad y>x,\tag{A}\\
&\exists y\in\RR\ \forall x\in\RR:\quad y>x.\tag{B}
\end{align*}
In (A), someone supplies $x$ and we may respond with a suitable $y$. Taking $y=x+1$ works. The response depends on the input. In (B), we must first choose one $y$ that exceeds every real number. Once we choose it, the input $x=y+1$ defeats it. The two sentences contain the same inequality but make different promises.

Read a quantified statement as a sequence of choices, made from left to right. A choice introduced by $\exists$ may depend on every input that was chosen before it, but not on anything chosen after it. When a proof involving constants becomes confusing, one of the most useful questions to ask is: \emph{at what stage is this quantity chosen, and which other quantities is it allowed to depend on?}

\subsection*{Tolerance and the inequality moves it needs}
A \emph{tolerance} is a positive amount of error we are prepared to allow. The letter $\eps$, read ``epsilon,'' is often used for it, but it is just a name. It might stand for $1/10$, $1/1000$, or any other positive real number. ``For every positive $\eps$'' includes all of those choices; it does not refer to one unspecified fixed decimal.

Before using fractions in an inequality, check signs. Three rules cover almost everything we need:
\begin{itemize}
\item Adding the same number to both sides preserves the order: from $2<5$ we get $2+7<5+7$.
\item Multiplying or dividing both sides by the same \emph{positive} number preserves the order: from $2<5$ we get $6<15$.
\item Multiplying or dividing both sides by the same \emph{negative} number reverses the order: from $2<5$ we get $-2>-5$.
\end{itemize}
These rules explain a fact about reciprocals. Suppose $0<a<b$. The product $ab$ is positive, so we may divide both sides of $a<b$ by $ab$ without reversing the inequality:
\[
\frac{a}{ab}<\frac{b}{ab},\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac1b<\frac1a.
\]
So for positive numbers, taking reciprocals reverses the order: $2<5$ becomes $1/5<1/2$. The rule fails without positivity; for instance $-1<2$, but $1/(-1)=-1$ is not larger than $1/2$.

For example, to make $1/N<1/100$, choosing $N=101$ works, while $N=100$ gives equality and fails the strict inequality. For a general tolerance $\eps$, we will choose an integer $N$ larger than $1/\eps$. Such an integer always exists, because the integers are not bounded above on the real number line: beyond any real number, however large, there is an integer. We accept this as a basic property of the real numbers; it is called the \emph{Archimedean property}.

\begin{example}
The statement ``For every positive real $\eps$, there is a positive integer $N$ with $1/N<\eps$'' allows $N$ to depend on $\eps$. A smaller requested tolerance generally needs a larger integer. To find such an $N$, use the Archimedean property to choose an integer $N>1/\eps$. Both $N$ and $1/\eps$ are positive, so taking reciprocals reverses the inequality and gives $1/N<1/(1/\eps)=\eps$.
\end{example}

\pauseq{Can one positive integer $N$ satisfy $1/N<\eps$ for every positive $\eps$?}{No. After $N$ is fixed, take $\eps=1/(2N)$. Then $1/N=2\eps>\eps$. The failed claim demands one universal choice instead of a choice adapted to each tolerance.}
