\section{An alternating repair}\label{ch08:sec:augment}
In the Ada--Ben graph with the stuck matching $M=\{\text{Ada--}1\}$, Ben's only acceptable task is held by Ada. The repair is to move Ada to task~$2$ and give task~$1$ to Ben. This follows the route
\[
\text{Ben}\;\text{--}\;1\;\text{--}\;\text{Ada}\;\text{--}\;2,
\]
the path Ben, $1$, Ada, $2$ in the notation of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:walks}. Of its three edges, the first and third, Ben--$1$ and Ada--$2$, are not in $M$, while the second, Ada--$1$, is. The repair removes the edge that was in $M$ and adds the two that were not, giving $\{\text{Ben--}1,\ \text{Ada--}2\}$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Vertex & Before the repair & After the repair\\
\midrule
Ben & no task & task $1$\\
task $1$ & held by Ada & held by Ben\\
Ada & task $1$ & task $2$\\
task $2$ & unused & held by Ada\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The middle vertices, task~$1$ and Ada, still have one partner each, and the two ends, Ben and task~$2$, have become matched.

\subsection*{Alternating and augmenting paths}
To describe this repair in general, fix a matching $M$. We call the edges of $M$ \emph{matched edges}, and the other edges of the graph, the allowed pairs not currently chosen, \emph{unmatched edges}. Recall from Section~\ref{ch07:sec:walks} that a walk $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$ takes $k$ steps, the $i$th along the edge $v_{i-1}v_i$, and that a path is a walk with no repeated vertex.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Alternating walk, augmenting path]\label{ch08:def:augmenting}
Let $M$ be a matching. A walk is \emph{alternating} if its steps use matched and unmatched edges alternately: of any two consecutive steps, one uses an edge of $M$ and the other uses an edge not in $M$. An \emph{augmenting path} for $M$ is an alternating path that has at least one step and whose first and last vertices are both unmatched.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Let $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$ be an augmenting path. Its first edge $v_0v_1$ is unmatched, because $v_0$ is unmatched and so lies on no edge of $M$; for the same reason its last edge is unmatched. Since the edges alternate, the $i$th edge is unmatched when $i$ is odd and matched when $i$ is even. The last edge is unmatched, so $k$ is odd. Writing $k=2r+1$, the path has $r+1$ unmatched edges, in the odd positions, and $r$ matched edges, in the even positions.

In a bipartite graph every edge joins $L$ to $R$, so the vertices of a walk alternate between students and tasks, and a walk with an odd number of steps ends in the other part from where it started. So one end of an augmenting path is an unmatched student and the other an unused task. We always list the path starting from the student. The simplest augmenting path is a single edge from an unmatched student to an unused task, with $k=1$ and $r=0$; in the Ada--Ben repair, $k=3$ and $r=1$.

\pauseq{With $M=\{\text{Ada--}1\}$, is the single edge Ben--$1$ an augmenting path?}{No. Ben is unmatched, but task~$1$ is held by Ada, so adding Ben--$1$ to $M$ would give task~$1$ two owners. An augmenting path must end at an unused task, so here it must continue through Ada to task~$2$.}

\subsection*{Flipping along an augmenting path}
On a longer augmenting path the same exchange happens all along the path: each student inside it trades the task just before them for the task just after them. To \emph{flip} $M$ along a walk $P$ means to remove from $M$ the matched edges that $P$ uses and to add the unmatched edges that $P$ uses; edges not on $P$ stay as they were.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lemma}\label{thm:augment}
Let $M$ be a matching and let $P$ be an augmenting path for $M$. Flipping $M$ along $P$ gives a matching $M'$ with exactly one more edge than $M$.
\end{lemma}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Write $P$ as $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$. Every edge of $M'$ is in $M$ or on $P$, so it is an edge of the graph. To show that $M'$ is a matching, we check that every vertex lies on at most one edge of $M'$.

The vertices of a path are all different. Therefore $v_i$ lies on the edge $v_{i-1}v_i$ (if $i\ge1$) and on the edge $v_iv_{i+1}$ (if $i\le k-1$), and on no other edge of $P$: an edge $v_{j-1}v_j$ contains $v_i$ only if $v_i=v_{j-1}$ or $v_i=v_j$, that is, only if $j=i+1$ or $j=i$. There are three kinds of vertices to check.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A vertex $v$ not on $P$.} The flip changes no edge at $v$, so $v$ lies on at most one edge of $M'$, as it did for $M$.
\item \emph{An end of $P$.} Take $v_0$; the argument for $v_k$ is the same. The only edge of $P$ at $v_0$ is $v_0v_1$, which is unmatched, so the flip adds it. Since $v_0$ is unmatched, no edge of $M$ contains it, and the edges at $v_0$ not on $P$ stay outside $M'$. So $v_0$ lies on exactly one edge of $M'$.
\item \emph{A vertex $v_i$ inside $P$, with $0<i<k$.} Its edges $v_{i-1}v_i$ and $v_iv_{i+1}$ are consecutive edges of $P$, so one is in $M$ and the other is not; the flip removes the one in $M$ and adds the other. Because $M$ is a matching and $v_i$ already lies on a matched edge of $P$, no edge of $M$ off $P$ contains $v_i$, and the flip adds no edge off $P$. So the only edge of $M'$ at $v_i$ is the path edge that was added.
\end{itemize}
Hence no vertex lies on two edges of $M'$, and $M'$ is a matching.

Now count. The edges of $P$ are all different, because they join different pairs of vertices. As shown before the lemma, $P$ has $r+1$ unmatched edges and $r$ matched edges for some natural number $r$. The flip removes the $r$ matched edges and adds the $r+1$ unmatched ones, none of which was in $M$. Therefore $|M'|=|M|-r+(r+1)=|M|+1$.
\end{proof}

The proof needs $P$ to be a path: on an alternating walk that visits a vertex twice, the flip could give that vertex two partners (Exercise~\ref{ex:8.4}).

\subsection*{What is preserved and what makes progress}
Habit~5 (Section~\ref{ch05:sec:euclid}) asks of a repeated step what stays true and what measures progress. For repeated flips along augmenting paths, Lemma~\ref{thm:augment} answers both: the selected edges always form a matching, and their number $|M|$ grows by one at every flip. It cannot grow forever: each edge of a matching contains exactly one student, and no two edges contain the same one, so sending each edge of $M$ to its student is an injective map from $M$ to $L$, and therefore $|M|\le|L|$ (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}). So the nonnegative integer $|L|-|M|$ decreases by one at every flip, and a procedure that starts from the empty matching makes at most $|L|$ flips.
