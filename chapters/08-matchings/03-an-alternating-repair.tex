\section{An alternating repair}\label{ch08:sec:augment}
Return to the second Ada--Ben example and the stuck matching $M=\{\text{Ada--}1\}$. Ben is unmatched, and his only acceptable task is held by Ada. The repair is to move Ada to her other task, task~$2$, and then give task~$1$ to Ben. Read along the edges of the graph, this repair follows the route
\[
\text{Ben}\;\text{--}\;1\;\text{--}\;\text{Ada}\;\text{--}\;2,
\]
which is the path Ben, $1$, Ada, $2$ in the notation of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:walks}. Its first edge, Ben--$1$, is not in $M$. Its second edge, Ada--$1$, is in $M$. Its third edge, Ada--$2$, is not in $M$. The repair removes from $M$ the edge of the path that was in $M$ and adds the two edges of the path that were not. The result is $\{\text{Ben--}1,\ \text{Ada--}2\}$.

Here is what happens at each vertex of the path.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Vertex & Before the repair & After the repair\\
\midrule
Ben & no task & task $1$\\
task $1$ & held by Ada & held by Ben\\
Ada & task $1$ & task $2$\\
task $2$ & unused & held by Ada\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Task~$1$ changes owner; at no moment does it have two owners. Ada gives up one task and takes another, so she still has exactly one. The two vertices at the ends of the path, Ben and task~$2$, were unmatched and are now matched. One more student has a task, and nobody who had a task has lost it.

\subsection*{Alternating and augmenting paths}
To describe this repair in general, fix a matching $M$. We call the edges of $M$ \emph{matched edges} and the other edges of the graph \emph{unmatched edges}. Recall from Section~\ref{ch07:sec:walks} that a walk $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$ takes $k$ steps, the $i$th step running along the edge $v_{i-1}v_i$, and that a path is a walk with no repeated vertex.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Alternating walk, augmenting path]\label{ch08:def:augmenting}
Let $M$ be a matching. A walk is \emph{alternating} if its steps use matched and unmatched edges alternately: of any two consecutive steps, one uses an edge of $M$ and the other uses an edge not in $M$. An \emph{augmenting path} for $M$ is an alternating path that has at least one step and whose first and last vertices are both unmatched.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Let $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$ be an augmenting path. Its first edge $v_0v_1$ is unmatched, because $v_0$ is unmatched and so lies on no edge of $M$. For the same reason its last edge $v_{k-1}v_k$ is unmatched. Since the edges alternate, the list of edges reads
\[
\text{unmatched},\ \text{matched},\ \text{unmatched},\ \ldots,\ \text{matched},\ \text{unmatched}.
\]
So the $i$th edge is unmatched when $i$ is odd and matched when $i$ is even. The last edge, the $k$th, is unmatched, so $k$ is odd. Writing $k=2r+1$, the path has $r+1$ unmatched edges, in the odd positions $1,3,\ldots,2r+1$, and $r$ matched edges, in the even positions $2,4,\ldots,2r$.

In a bipartite graph every edge joins $L$ to $R$, so the vertices of a walk alternate between students and tasks. A walk with an odd number of steps therefore starts and ends on different sides. One end of an augmenting path is an unmatched student and the other is an unused task, and we always list the path starting from the student. The simplest augmenting path is a single edge joining an unmatched student to an unused task; then $k=1$ and $r=0$. In the Ada--Ben repair, $k=3$ and $r=1$.

\pauseq{With $M=\{\text{Ada--}1\}$, is the single edge Ben--$1$ an augmenting path?}{No. It is an alternating path with one step, and Ben is unmatched, but its other endpoint, task~$1$, is held by Ada. Adding Ben--$1$ to $M$ would give task~$1$ two owners. An augmenting path must end at an unused task, and here the only way to reach one is to continue through Ada to task~$2$.}

\subsection*{Flipping along an augmenting path}
For a longer augmenting path the same exchange happens all along the path. Each student in the middle of the path gives up the task just before it on the path and takes the task just after it. Each task in the middle of the path changes owner. The unmatched student at the start receives its first task, and the unused task at the end receives its first owner. To \emph{flip} $M$ along a walk $P$ means to remove from $M$ the matched edges that $P$ uses and to add the unmatched edges that $P$ uses; edges that $P$ does not use stay as they were. The next lemma checks that flipping along an augmenting path always gives a larger matching.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lemma}\label{thm:augment}
Let $M$ be a matching and let $P$ be an augmenting path for $M$. Flipping $M$ along $P$ gives a matching $M'$ with exactly one more edge than $M$.
\end{lemma}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Write $P$ as $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$. Every edge of $M'$ is an edge of the graph, because it is an edge of $M$ or an edge of $P$. To show that $M'$ is a matching, we check that every vertex lies on at most one edge of $M'$.

The vertices of a path are all different. Therefore a vertex $v_i$ of $P$ lies on the edge $v_{i-1}v_i$ (if $i\ge1$) and on the edge $v_iv_{i+1}$ (if $i\le k-1$), and on no other edge of $P$: an edge $v_{j-1}v_j$ of $P$ contains $v_i$ only if $v_i=v_{j-1}$ or $v_i=v_j$, that is, only if $j=i+1$ or $j=i$. With this in hand, there are three kinds of vertices to check.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A vertex $v$ that is not on $P$.} No edge of $P$ contains $v$, so the flip does not change any edge at $v$. The edges of $M'$ at $v$ are exactly the edges of $M$ at $v$, and there is at most one of those.
\item \emph{An end of $P$.} Take $v_0$; the argument for $v_k$ is the same. The only edge of $P$ at $v_0$ is $v_0v_1$, which is unmatched, so the flip adds it. Since $v_0$ is unmatched, no edge of $M$ contains $v_0$, and the edges at $v_0$ that are not on $P$ remain outside $M'$. So $v_0$ lies on exactly one edge of $M'$.
\item \emph{A vertex $v_i$ inside $P$, with $0<i<k$.} Its two path edges are consecutive edges of $P$, so one of them is in $M$ and the other is not. The flip removes the one in $M$ and adds the other. Because $M$ is a matching and $v_i$ already lies on a matched edge of $P$, no edge of $M$ outside $P$ contains $v_i$. So the only edge of $M'$ at $v_i$ is the path edge that was added.
\end{itemize}
Hence no vertex lies on two edges of $M'$, and $M'$ is a matching.

Now count. The edges of $P$ are all different, because they join different pairs of vertices. As shown before the lemma, $P$ has $r+1$ unmatched edges and $r$ matched edges for some natural number $r$. The flip removes the $r$ matched edges from $M$ and adds the $r+1$ unmatched edges, none of which was in $M$. Therefore $|M'|=|M|-r+(r+1)=|M|+1$.
\end{proof}

Here is the lemma at work on a longer path, which we will meet again in Section~\ref{sec:matching-trace}. Let students $a,b,c$ accept the tasks $N(a)=\{1,2\}$, $N(b)=\{1\}$, $N(c)=\{2,3\}$, and let $M=\{a\text{--}1,\ c\text{--}2\}$. Then
\[
b\;\text{--}\;1\;\text{--}\;a\;\text{--}\;2\;\text{--}\;c\;\text{--}\;3
\]
is an augmenting path: $b$ is unmatched, task~$3$ is unused, and the edges $b$--$1$, $a$--$1$, $a$--$2$, $c$--$2$, $c$--$3$ are unmatched, matched, unmatched, matched, unmatched in turn. Before the flip, $a$ holds task~$1$ and $c$ holds task~$2$. Afterwards $b$ holds~$1$, $a$ holds~$2$, and $c$ holds~$3$. The inner vertices $1$, $a$, $2$, $c$ each exchange one matched edge for another, the end $b$ gains a task, and the end $3$ gains an owner. The path has $k=5$ edges, three unmatched and two matched, so $r=2$ and the matching grows from two edges to three.

The proof used twice that the vertices of $P$ are all different: to know that an inner vertex lies on exactly two edges of $P$, and to know that the edges of $P$ are all different. If a vertex appeared twice on an alternating walk, it could lie on two unmatched edges of the walk; flipping would add both of them and give that vertex two partners. Exercise~\ref{ex:8.4} asks you to build such an example and to show how the problem can be avoided.

\subsection*{What is preserved and what makes progress}
Habit~5 (Section~\ref{ch05:sec:euclid}) asks two questions about a repeated step, and for a procedure that repeatedly flips along augmenting paths, Lemma~\ref{thm:augment} answers both. The property preserved at every step is ``the selected edges form a matching.'' The measure of progress is the number $|M|$ of selected edges, which grows by exactly one at every flip. It cannot grow forever. Each edge of a matching contains exactly one student, and no two edges of a matching contain the same student, so sending each edge of $M$ to its student is an injective map from $M$ to $L$, and therefore $|M|\le|L|$ (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}). Equivalently, the nonnegative integer $|L|-|M|$ decreases by one at every flip. Starting from the empty matching, there can therefore be at most $|L|$ flips. Two questions remain: how to find an augmenting path, and what to conclude when there is none.
