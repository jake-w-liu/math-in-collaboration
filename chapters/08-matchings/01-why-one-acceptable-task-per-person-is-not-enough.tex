\section{Why one acceptable task per person is not enough}\label{ch08:sec:model}
Section~\ref{ch02:sec:negation} looked at the statement that all the students of a class can be given different tasks, each acceptable to the student who receives it. The \emph{assignment problem} asks when this can be done, and how to find such an assignment. The simplest failure is the kind Exercise~\ref{ex:2.5} asked you to build: Ada and Ben each accept only task~$1$. Each of them has an acceptable task, but they cannot be given different ones.

Now let Ada accept tasks $1$ and $2$, while Ben still accepts only task~$1$. A solution exists: Ada receives task~$2$ and Ben task~$1$. But suppose we hand out tasks \emph{greedily}: one student at a time, each receives some acceptable task that is still free, and no task is ever taken back. If Ada comes first and happens to get task~$1$, nothing is left for Ben. Getting stuck does not prove that there is no solution; it shows only that the choices made so far cannot be extended without changing an earlier one.

\subsection*{The graph of acceptable pairs}
We describe such problems by a graph in the sense of Definition~\ref{ch07:def:graph}. The vertices are the students and the tasks, and an edge joins a student to a task exactly when the student accepts that task. No edge joins two students, and no edge joins two tasks.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Bipartite graph]\label{ch08:def:bipartite}
A graph is \emph{bipartite}, with \emph{parts} $L$ and $R$, if its vertex set is the union of two disjoint sets $L$ and $R$ and every edge has one endpoint in $L$ and the other endpoint in $R$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Throughout this chapter, $L$ (drawn on the left) is the set of students and $R$ (on the right) is the set of tasks. We write the edge joining a student $s$ to a task $t$ as $s$--$t$, with the student first; the notation $st$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs} is hard to read when the vertices are called Ada or $1$. Here is the graph of the second example:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[every node/.style={font=\small},line width=.7pt]
\node[rectangle,rounded corners,draw=ink,minimum width=11mm,minimum height=6mm] (ada) at (0,1) {Ada};
\node[rectangle,rounded corners,draw=ink,minimum width=11mm,minimum height=6mm] (ben) at (0,0) {Ben};
\node[circle,draw=ink,minimum size=6mm,inner sep=1pt] (t1) at (3,1) {$1$};
\node[circle,draw=ink,minimum size=6mm,inner sep=1pt] (t2) at (3,0) {$2$};
\draw[ink] (ada)--(t1) (ada)--(t2) (ben)--(t1);
\node at (0,-0.75) {students $L$};
\node at (3,-0.75) {tasks $R$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Its three edges are Ada--$1$, Ada--$2$ and Ben--$1$, and we call it the \emph{Ada--Ben graph}; the point where two lines cross is not a vertex. The neighbors of a student $s$ are exactly the tasks that $s$ accepts, and we write $N(s)$ for this set. Here $N(\text{Ada})=\{1,2\}$ and $N(\text{Ben})=\{1\}$.

\subsection*{Matchings}
An assignment selects some of the edges. Since no task goes to two students and no student receives two tasks, the selected edges never share an endpoint.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Matching]\label{ch08:def:matching}
A \emph{matching} in a graph is a set $M$ of edges of the graph, no two of which share an endpoint. A vertex is \emph{matched} by $M$ if it is an endpoint of some edge of $M$, and \emph{unmatched} otherwise. Two vertices joined by an edge of $M$ are \emph{partners}. In a bipartite graph with parts $L$ and $R$, a matching \emph{saturates} $L$ if every vertex of $L$ is matched.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

A matched vertex lies on exactly one edge of $M$: on at least one because it is matched, and on at most one because no two edges of $M$ share an endpoint. So a matched student holds exactly one task, its partner, and a matched task is held by exactly one student, its \emph{owner}. For tasks we also say \emph{used} and \emph{unused} instead of matched and unmatched. The empty set of edges is a matching too, since it has no two edges that could share an endpoint; it leaves every vertex unmatched.

In the Ada--Ben graph, $\{\text{Ada--}2,\ \text{Ben--}1\}$ is a matching that saturates $L$, while $\{\text{Ada--}1,\ \text{Ben--}1\}$ is not a matching, because both of its edges have the endpoint~$1$. A matching that saturates $L$ is exactly a solution of the assignment problem, and we call it a \emph{full assignment}. It need not use every task.
