\section{Definition, example, claim, theorem, proof}\label{ch01:sec:roles}
Mathematical sentences do different jobs, and a reader who mixes up the jobs can easily misjudge what has been shown.

\emph{A definition sets a meaning.} Definition~\ref{def:parity} is an agreement about how the words \emph{even} and \emph{odd} will be used, not a discovery about numbers, so there is nothing to prove before we adopt it.

\emph{An example shows the meaning in one instance}, as $7=2\cdot3+1$ does for the word \emph{odd}. A \emph{nonexample} is an object that does not meet the definition. For instance, $4$ is not odd: if $4=2k+1$, then $2k=3$ and $k=3/2$, which is not an integer, so no integer witness exists. Examples and nonexamples together show where the boundary of a definition lies.

\emph{A claim asserts something that needs justification.} ``Adding two odd integers always gives an even integer'' is not part of any definition. To establish it, we must show that whenever two integers are each one more than twice an integer, their sum is twice an integer.

\emph{A theorem is a claim that has been proved.} The heading \emph{theorem} is usually kept for the most important results. A \emph{proposition} is a proved result of more moderate importance, a \emph{lemma} is proved mainly to help prove something else, and a \emph{corollary} follows quickly from an earlier result. All are held to the same standard of proof. A \emph{conjecture}, by contrast, has been proposed but not yet proved. In logic and in the proof assistant Lean (Chapter~\ref{ch:lean1}), the word \emph{proposition} has a second meaning: any statement that is either true or false, whether or not anyone has proved it, such as the false proposition ``$2+2=5$.'' Context makes clear which meaning is intended.

\emph{A proof supplies the justification.} It starts from what we are given and from facts already accepted, makes deductions that can each be justified, and arrives at exactly the promised conclusion, so that the reader can see for themselves that no allowed case has been left out. The square $\square$ marks the end of a proof.

A \emph{hypothesis}, also called an assumption or premise, is a condition under which a result is asserted. In Proposition~\ref{thm:odd-sum} in the next section, the hypotheses are that the two numbers are integers and that both are odd; the \emph{conclusion} is that their sum is even. A proof may use the hypotheses at any point, but not the conclusion, because that is exactly what it must establish.

Facts accepted without proof as the starting rules of a theory are called \emph{axioms}. We accept the familiar rules of arithmetic, such as $a+b=b+a$, and four further \emph{starting facts} about numbers, listed in \emph{To the reader}; the first, about square roots, appears in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation}. A claim does not become a starting fact just because it is hard to prove: an argument that relies on a claim that is neither proved nor accepted as a starting fact has a gap.
