\section{Definition, example, claim, theorem, proof}\label{ch01:sec:roles}
Mathematical sentences do different jobs, and a reader who mixes up the jobs can easily misjudge what has been shown. Before we read our first numbered result, it helps to tell five of these jobs apart.

\emph{A definition sets a meaning.} ``Odd means one more than twice an integer'' is a rule for using a word. There is nothing to check before we adopt the rule. Showing that a particular number meets the rule, however, may take some work.

\emph{An example shows the meaning in one instance.} The equation $7=2\cdot3+1$ shows the definition of odd at work for the number $7$. A \emph{nonexample} is an object that does not meet the definition. For instance, $4$ is not odd: if $4=2k+1$, then subtracting one gives $2k=3$ and dividing by two gives $k=3/2$, which is not an integer. So no integer witness exists. Examples and nonexamples together show where the boundary of a definition lies.

\emph{A claim asserts something that needs justification.} The sentence ``Adding two odd integers always gives an even integer'' is not part of any definition. To establish it, we must show that whenever two integers are each one more than twice an integer, their sum is twice an integer. A few agreeing examples do not do this, because the claim is about every pair of odd integers.

\emph{A theorem is a claim that has been proved.} Books use several different headings for proved results, and all of them are held to exactly the same standard of proof. The heading \emph{theorem} is usually kept for results the author wants to emphasize. A \emph{proposition} is a proved result of more moderate importance. A \emph{lemma} is a result proved mainly to help prove something else. A \emph{corollary} is a result that follows quickly from an earlier one. These words describe the role a result plays in the book. None of them is more or less certain than the others, and none of them allows steps to be skipped. A \emph{conjecture}, by contrast, is a statement that has been proposed but not yet proved.

The word \emph{proposition} has a second meaning, which we will meet when we study logic and the proof assistant Lean: any statement that is either true or false, whether or not anyone has proved it. In that sense ``$2+2=5$'' is a proposition, a false one. So calling a statement a proposition in this second sense says nothing about whether it is true. Context makes clear which meaning is intended.

\emph{A proof supplies the justification.} It starts from what we are given and from facts already accepted, makes deductions that can each be justified, and arrives at exactly the promised conclusion. A proof does not ask the reader to trust the author; it lets the reader see for themselves why no allowed case has been left out. The small square $\square$ at the end of a proof marks where the argument ends.

A \emph{hypothesis}, also called an assumption or premise, is a condition under which a result is asserted. In Proposition~\ref{thm:odd-sum}, which we prove in the next section, the hypotheses are that the two numbers are integers and that both are odd. The \emph{conclusion} is that their sum is even. A proof may use the hypotheses at any point. It may not use the conclusion, because the conclusion is exactly what it is supposed to establish.

Some facts are accepted as the starting rules of a theory; these are called \emph{axioms}. In this course we also accept familiar facts of arithmetic, such as $a+b=b+a$, without deriving them from more basic principles. Whenever we accept such a starting fact, we say so openly. A difficult claim does not become a starting fact just because we lack a proof of it: we always keep separate what we have agreed to assume and what an argument is supposed to establish.
