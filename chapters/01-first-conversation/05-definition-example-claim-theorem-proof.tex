\section{Definition, example, claim, theorem, proof}\label{ch01:sec:roles}
Mathematical sentences do different jobs, and a reader who mixes up the jobs can easily misjudge what has been shown. Before we prove our first result, it helps to tell five of these jobs apart.

\emph{A definition sets a meaning.} Definition~\ref{def:parity} is a rule for using the words \emph{even} and \emph{odd}, so there is nothing to prove before we adopt it. Checking that a particular number meets the rule may still take some work.

\emph{An example shows the meaning in one instance.} The equation $7=2\cdot3+1$ shows the definition of odd at work for the number $7$. A \emph{nonexample} is an object that does not meet the definition. For instance, $4$ is not odd: if $4=2k+1$, then subtracting one gives $2k=3$ and dividing by two gives $k=3/2$, which is not an integer. So no integer witness exists. Examples and nonexamples together show where the boundary of a definition lies.

\emph{A claim asserts something that needs justification.} The sentence ``Adding two odd integers always gives an even integer'' is not part of any definition. To establish it, we must show that whenever two integers are each one more than twice an integer, their sum is twice an integer.

\emph{A theorem is a claim that has been proved.} Books use several headings for proved results. The heading \emph{theorem} is usually kept for the results the author most wants to emphasize. A \emph{proposition} is a proved result of more moderate importance, a \emph{lemma} is proved mainly to help prove something else, and a \emph{corollary} follows quickly from an earlier result. These words describe the role a result plays in the book; all of them are held to the same standard of proof, and none allows steps to be skipped. A \emph{conjecture}, by contrast, is a statement that has been proposed but not yet proved.

The word \emph{proposition} has a second meaning, used in logic and in the proof assistant Lean (Chapter~\ref{ch:lean1}): any statement that is either true or false, whether or not anyone has proved it. In that sense ``$2+2=5$'' is a proposition, a false one. Context makes clear which meaning is intended.

\emph{A proof supplies the justification.} It starts from what we are given and from facts already accepted, makes deductions that can each be justified, and arrives at exactly the promised conclusion. A proof does not ask the reader to trust the author; it lets the reader see for themselves why no allowed case has been left out. The small square $\square$ at the end of a proof marks where the argument ends.

A \emph{hypothesis}, also called an assumption or premise, is a condition under which a result is asserted. In Proposition~\ref{thm:odd-sum}, which we prove in the next section, the hypotheses are that the two numbers are integers and that both are odd. The \emph{conclusion} is that their sum is even. A proof may use the hypotheses at any point. It may not use the conclusion, because the conclusion is exactly what it is supposed to establish.

Some facts are accepted without proof as the starting rules of a theory; these are called \emph{axioms}. In this course we accept two kinds of facts in this way: the familiar rules of arithmetic, such as $a+b=b+a$, and four further \emph{starting facts} about numbers, which are listed in \emph{To the reader}. The first starting fact, about square roots, appears in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation}. A claim does not become a starting fact just because it is hard to prove. If an argument relies on a claim that has not been proved and is not one of the facts we have agreed to accept, the argument has a gap.
