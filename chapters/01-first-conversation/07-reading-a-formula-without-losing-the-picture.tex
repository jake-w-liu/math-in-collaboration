\section{Sums, number systems, and inequalities}\label{ch01:sec:notation}
Sometimes we need to add many quantities. The notation
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_j=a_1+a_2+a_3+a_4
\]
means: add the numbers named $a_1$, $a_2$, $a_3$, and $a_4$. The large symbol $\sum$ is the Greek capital letter sigma, an S for ``sum.'' The letter $j$ is a temporary \emph{index}, a counter that takes the values $1,2,3,4$ in turn: it starts at the value written below the sigma and stops at the value written above it. A small number or letter written below and to the right of a symbol, such as the $2$ in $a_2$, is called a \emph{subscript}. A subscript is a label, not a multiplication: $a_2$ is the second number in the list, whereas $2a$ is twice $a$.

Suppose the numbers are $a_1=1$, $a_2=3$, $a_3=5$, and $a_4=7$. Then the sum is $1+3+5+7=16$. Renaming the index does not change the sum: $\sum_{r=1}^{4}a_r$ is also $16$, because the index is only a counter. Changing the upper limit from $4$ to $3$ does change it: $\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_j=1+3+5=9$. A small change in the notation can be a real change in what is being claimed.

Notice also that the totals $1+3+5=9=3^2$ and $1+3+5+7=16=4^2$ are perfect squares. Is the sum of the first $n$ odd positive integers always $n^2$? This is exactly the situation of Section~\ref{ch01:sec:pattern}: the examples suggest a pattern, but only a proof can settle it. Theorem~\ref{thm:odds} in Chapter~\ref{ch:induction} supplies that proof.

The quantity being added can be any expression involving the index. To evaluate such a sum, substitute each value of the index in turn and add the results. With the same numbers as before,
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{3}(a_j+5)=(a_1+5)+(a_2+5)+(a_3+5)=6+8+10=24.
\]
The index itself may also appear in the expression:
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{3}j^2=1^2+2^2+3^2=1+4+9=14.
\]

Several collections of numbers have standard names. A \emph{set} is a collection of objects, called its \emph{members}; Chapter~\ref{ch:maps} studies sets carefully. Curly braces are used to list the members of a set.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\NN=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}$ is the set of \emph{natural numbers}. The dots mean that the displayed pattern continues forever. In this book zero counts as a natural number; when we want only the whole numbers $1,2,3,\ldots$, we will say so explicitly.
\item $\ZZ=\{\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\}$ is the set of integers from Section~\ref{ch01:sec:pattern}.
\item $\QQ$ is the set of \emph{rational numbers}: numbers that can be written as a fraction $a/b$ in which $a$ and $b$ are integers and $b\ne0$. Examples are $3/4$, $-5/2$, and $7=7/1$.
\item $\RR$ is the set of \emph{real numbers}: all the numbers on the number line. Every integer and every fraction is a real number, but the number line also contains numbers that are not fractions of integers. For example, by Pythagoras' theorem the diagonal of a square with sides of length $1$ has a length whose square is $1^2+1^2=2$. That length is $\sqrt2$, the positive number whose square is $2$. (Square roots are discussed at the end of this section.) Theorem~\ref{thm:sqrt2} in Chapter~\ref{ch:proof} proves that $\sqrt2$ cannot be written as a fraction of integers.
\end{itemize}
Each of these sets contains the one before it: every natural number is an integer, every integer is a rational number, and every rational number is a real number. When a letter is said to be real, every point of the number line is an allowed value, including negative numbers, zero, and numbers that are not fractions.

To compare real numbers we use four inequality signs. The statement $x<y$ means that $x$ is strictly smaller than $y$, and $x\le y$ means that $x$ is smaller than or equal to $y$, that is, at most $y$. Likewise, $x>y$ means that $x$ is strictly larger than $y$, and $x\ge y$ means that $x$ is at least $y$. For example, $3\le3$ is true, but $3<3$ is false. The signs $<$ and $>$ give \emph{strict} inequalities, while $\le$ and $\ge$ give \emph{non-strict} ones. The two kinds disagree only when the two sides are equal, and that boundary case is where many mistakes in proofs occur. A chain such as $0\le x\le1$ states two conditions at once: $0\le x$ and $x\le1$.

A number $x$ is \emph{positive} if $x>0$ and \emph{negative} if $x<0$. It is \emph{nonnegative} if $x\ge0$. So zero is nonnegative, but it is neither positive nor negative.

Later we will use square roots. If $t$ is a nonnegative real number, the symbol $\sqrt t$ denotes the nonnegative real number whose square is $t$. For example, $\sqrt9=3$: both $3$ and $-3$ have square $9$, and the square-root sign always picks the nonnegative one. We accept without proof the familiar fact that every nonnegative real number $t$ has exactly one nonnegative square root. A negative number has no real square root at all, because the square of a real number is never negative.

Because the square-root sign always gives a nonnegative answer, $\sqrt{x^2}$ is not always equal to $x$. At $x=-3$, for instance, $\sqrt{(-3)^2}=\sqrt9=3$. In general, $\sqrt{x^2}=x$ when $x\ge0$, and $\sqrt{x^2}=-x$ when $x<0$; in the second case $-x$ is the right answer because it is nonnegative and $(-x)^2=x^2$. In both cases $\sqrt{x^2}$ is the distance from $x$ to $0$ on the number line. Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} calls this distance the \emph{absolute value} of $x$ and writes it $|x|$.
