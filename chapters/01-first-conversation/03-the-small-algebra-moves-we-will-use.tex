\section{The small algebra moves we will use}\label{ch01:sec:algebra}
To evaluate an expression such as $2k+1$ at $k=3$, replace every occurrence of $k$ by $3$, getting $2\cdot3+1=7$. This is called \emph{substitution}.

Writing $f(n)=n+1$ names a rule $f$ that adds one to its input, so $f(3)=4$. Read $f(n)$ as ``the output of $f$ at the input $n$,'' not as $f$ times $n$. Such a rule is called a \emph{function}; Chapter~\ref{ch:maps} studies functions.

To rewrite an expression without changing its value, we may read any arithmetic rule from Section~\ref{ch01:sec:letters} in either direction. Reading the distributive rule from left to right, as in $2(k+1)=2k+2$, is called \emph{expanding} or \emph{multiplying out}. Reading it from right to left, as in $2k+2=2(k+1)$, is called \emph{factoring}; in a proof, factoring is often useful because it brings a common factor, here $2$, into the open.

An equality that holds for every allowed value of its letters, such as $2(k+1)=2k+2$, is called an \emph{identity}. An equation to be solved is different: $2k+1=7$ asks which values of $k$ make it true, and subtracting one and then dividing by two gives $k=3$.

For a longer example of multiplying out, take the product $(2k+1)(2\ell+1)$. The letter $\ell$ is a curly lowercase L, read ``ell,'' so that it is not mistaken for the digit $1$. Two different letters can stand for different numbers; Section~\ref{ch01:sec:dependence} explains why that matters. We multiply out one bracket at a time:
\begin{align*}
 (2k+1)(2\ell+1)
 &=2k(2\ell+1)+1\cdot(2\ell+1)\\
 &=(4k\ell+2k)+(2\ell+1)\\
 &=4k\ell+2k+2\ell+1.
\end{align*}
The first line uses $(a+b)c=ac+bc$ with $a=2k$, $b=1$, and $c=2\ell+1$. The second multiplies out each copy of $2\ell+1$ with $a(b+c)=ab+ac$, using $2k\cdot2\ell=(2\cdot2)(k\ell)=4k\ell$, since factors may be reordered and regrouped. The third drops the brackets, since addition may be regrouped.

A \emph{power} records repeated multiplication: $2^3=2\cdot2\cdot2=8$. The \emph{exponent} $3$ counts equal factors, not additions: $2+2+2=6$ is $2\cdot3$, not $2^3$. In general, for a positive integer $n$, $a^n$ is the product of $n$ factors each equal to $a$, and $a$ is called the \emph{base}; in particular, $a^1=a$. Counting factors gives two rules. Two factors of $2$ followed by three more make five, so $2^2\cdot2^3=2^5$, and in general $a^na^m=a^{n+m}$ for positive integers $n$ and $m$. Division cancels factors instead:
\[
\frac{2^5}{2^2}=\frac{2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2}{2\cdot2}=2^3.
\]
In general, if $n>m$, the $m$ factors below the line cancel $m$ of the $n$ factors above it, so $a^n/a^m=a^{n-m}$. Cancelling a factor $a$ means dividing by $a$, so this rule requires $a\ne0$.

We extend the notation to zero and negative exponents in the only way that keeps these rules working. Dividing $a^n$ by itself gives $1$, while the subtraction rule would give $a^0$; so we agree that $a^0=1$ whenever $a\ne0$. Similarly, the subtraction rule would turn $1/a^n=a^0/a^n$ into $a^{0-n}=a^{-n}$; so we agree that $a^{-n}=1/a^n$ for each positive integer $n$ and $a\ne0$. For example, $2^{-3}=1/8$. With these agreements, both rules hold for all integers $n$ and $m$, as long as $a\ne0$; for instance, $2^3\cdot2^{-1}=8\cdot\tfrac12=4=2^2$. We skip the routine check, which sorts the signs of $n$ and $m$ into cases, and use both rules freely.
