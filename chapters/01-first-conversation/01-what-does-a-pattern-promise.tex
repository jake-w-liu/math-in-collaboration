\section{What does a pattern promise?}\label{ch01:sec:pattern}
Suppose two people each have an odd number of tokens. If they put their tokens together, must the total be even? Try a few cases: $3+5=8$, $7+9=16$, and $1+1=2$. Every total is even. But there are infinitely many pairs of odd numbers, and no list of examples can include them all. To know what must happen for every pair, including those nobody has tried, we need a \emph{proof}: an argument that explains why the total is always even.

First we must say exactly which numbers the pattern is about. An \emph{integer} is a whole number that may be positive, negative, or zero: $\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots$, where the dots mean that the list continues forever in both directions. The collection of all integers is written $\ZZ$. The pattern becomes a precise statement:
\begin{center}
\emph{For every pair of odd integers, their sum is even.}
\end{center}
This covers more than the token story: nobody holds $-3$ tokens, but the statement includes sums such as $-3+5=2$. Once negative numbers are allowed, ``odd'' and ``even'' need exact meanings too; Section~\ref{ch01:sec:definition} supplies them.

So when you bring a claim to a classmate or to an AI assistant, the first question is not ``Can you prove this?'' but ``Which numbers does the claim talk about, and what exactly does it say about them?'' An assistant that quietly replaces ``integers'' by ``positive integers'' and then argues correctly has answered a different question: its proof says nothing about $-3+5$.
