\section{A definition that makes a proof possible}\label{ch01:sec:definition}
To prove anything about odd and even numbers, we need to know exactly what those words mean. A \emph{definition} gives an exact meaning to a word or symbol; ours must let us decide, for any integer at all, whether that integer qualifies. Definitions are set apart under numbered headings; in this book, definitions, examples, and proved results share one numbering within each chapter, while exercises are numbered separately.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Even and odd integers]\label{def:parity}
An integer $a$ is \emph{even} if $a=2k$ for some integer $k$. It is \emph{odd} if $a=2k+1$ for some integer $k$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

For example, $8$ is even because $8=2\cdot4$, and $7$ is odd because $7=2\cdot3+1$. Negative integers are included: $-3$ is odd because $-3=2\cdot(-2)+1$. Zero is even because $0=2\cdot0$. Whether an integer is even or odd is called its \emph{parity}. Every integer is in fact either even or odd, but not both; that separate fact needs its own argument, given in Lemma~\ref{ch04:lem:even-or-odd} of Chapter~\ref{ch:proof}, and this chapter does not need it.

The words ``for some integer $k$'' mean that at least one integer $k$ makes the equation true, and the requirement that $k$ be an \emph{integer} is essential. If $k$ could be any number, every integer $a$ would have the form $2k$, simply by choosing $k=a/2$; for instance, $7=2\cdot3.5$. The definition asks not whether a number can be divided by two, but whether the result is an integer, and $3.5$ is not.

When a definition asks for \emph{some} integer $k$ with a certain property, a particular integer with that property is called a \emph{witness}. For the odd integer $7$, the witness is $k=3$, because $7=2\cdot3+1$. The witness is not $7$ itself, but the extra integer that makes $7=2k+1$ true. For $-3$, the witness is $k=-2$.

A definition can be used in two directions. If we are told that $a$ is odd, we may introduce an integer $k$ with $a=2k+1$ and reason with that equation; this \emph{unpacks} the information. To show that a given integer $a$ is odd, it is enough to name an integer $k$ and check that $a=2k+1$; this \emph{recognizes} that the definition is satisfied. Our first proof does both: it unpacks the information that two numbers are odd, and it recognizes that their sum is even.
