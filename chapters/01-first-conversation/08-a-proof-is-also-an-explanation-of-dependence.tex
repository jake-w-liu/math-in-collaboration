\section{A proof is also an explanation of dependence}
In the parity proof, the two inputs may vary. The witnesses $k$ and $\ell$ vary with them. The final witness for evenness is built from those earlier witnesses. This dependence is part of the argument, not an incidental choice of letters.

Suppose an assistant writes both odd inputs as $2k+1$ using the same $k$. That only describes the special case in which the two inputs agree. The algebra could be correct and the argument still fail to cover the intended claim. A useful question is: ``Which quantities are independent, and which are chosen in response to others?'' Chapter~2 turns this question into a general method.

Here is a short exercise in keeping witnesses separate. Suppose we want to show that the sum of three odd integers is odd. Name them $a=2k+1$, $b=2\ell+1$, and $c=2m+1$, with three different letters because the three inputs may be different. Adding gives
\[
a+b+c=2k+2\ell+2m+3.
\]
At this point it is tempting to say that the expression ``looks odd,'' but that is not yet the definition. The definition asks for the form \emph{twice an integer plus one}. Split $3$ as $2+1$ and factor the terms that contain a $2$:
\[
2k+2\ell+2m+3=2(k+\ell+m+1)+1.
\]
The number $k+\ell+m+1$ is an integer, so $a+b+c$ has exactly the form required by Definition~\ref{def:parity}, and the proof is complete. Notice where the work happened: the arithmetic was easy, and the important step was recognizing which form the definition demands.
