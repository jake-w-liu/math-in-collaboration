\section*{Exercises}
Exercises~\ref{ex:1.1}--\ref{ex:1.4} are core exercises, and Exercises~\ref{ex:1.5} and~\ref{ex:1.6} are transfer exercises. Complete solutions are in Appendix~\ref{app:sola}.
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1.1}Evaluate $2(x+3)$ and $2x+3$ at $x=4$. Explain in words why the two answers differ.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1.2}Prove that the sum of an even integer and an odd integer is odd. Name the integer that serves as the witness for the oddness of the sum.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1.3}An assistant argues that the product of two odd integers is always odd, because $(2k+1)^2$ is odd for every integer $k$. Explain which products of odd integers this argument fails to cover, and then give a complete proof that the product of any two odd integers is odd.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1.4}Let $a_1=2$, $a_2=-1$, and $a_3=4$. Write out the sum $\sum_{j=1}^3(2a_j+1)$ term by term, and then evaluate it.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1.5}Find every real solution of the equation $x(x-2)=0$. Then explain why dividing both sides by $x$ does not, on its own, give a complete answer. You may use the fact that a product of two real numbers is zero only when at least one of the two factors is zero.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:1.6}Someone wants to know whether a formula $f(n)$ is zero for every positive integer $n$. They check $n=1,2,\ldots,10$, find $f(n)=0$ each time, and conclude that $f(n)=0$ always. Show that this reasoning is unsafe: construct a formula $f(n)$ with $f(n)=0$ for $n=1,2,\ldots,10$ but $f(11)\ne0$, and compute $f(11)$. Hint: use a product with one factor for each checked value of $n$.\end{exercise}
