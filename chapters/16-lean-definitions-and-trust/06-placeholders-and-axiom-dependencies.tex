\section{Placeholders and axiom dependencies}
While a proof is being developed, Lean allows a gap to be filled temporarily with the word \code{sorry}. Lean then accepts the declaration, with a warning, by treating the missing step as if it had been assumed. Such a declaration is not a completed proof. To find out whether a theorem secretly depends on a gap of this kind, use the command
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
#print axioms theoremName
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\par
which lists every \emph{axiom}---every statement accepted without proof---that the theorem's proof ultimately relies on. A theorem that used \code{sorry} anywhere in its dependencies shows the axiom \code{sorryAx} in this list.

Not every axiom signals a problem. Lean's foundations include a few standard axioms that ordinary mathematics uses routinely: \code{propext} (logically equivalent propositions are equal), \code{Quot.sound} (used to build quotient objects such as the residue classes of Chapter~6), and \code{Classical.choice} (which supports proofs by contradiction and choices of objects whose existence has been proved). Seeing these in the list is normal. The research repository of Chapter~7, for example, explicitly permits exactly these three axioms for its formal proof of Seymour's conjecture~\cite{oaiconfig}. What matters is to know what each listed axiom means~\cite{leanaxioms}.

For the examples in Chapters~15 and~16, the commands
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
#print axioms MathCourse.inj_comp
#print axioms MathCourse.surj_comp
#print axioms MathCourse.preimage_meet
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\par
report that these theorems depend on no axioms at all: their proofs use only the rules of Lean's logic and our own definitions.

An axiom list is not a substitute for reading the theorem's statement. The impossible-context theorem of the previous section depends on no axioms, yet it assumes \code{False} as a hypothesis. Conversely, a theorem may use a standard axiom without assuming anything about its desired conclusion. Both the assumptions written in the statement and the dependencies of the proof must be understood.

When you check a file, read everything Lean reports: errors, warnings (a \code{sorry} produces a warning, not an error), and the output of any \code{\#print axioms} commands. Wait until checking has finished before concluding that there are no problems; an editor that has not yet reached a line shows no error there. Larger projects, or proofs from sources you do not trust, call for the stronger validation procedures described in the official documentation~\cite{leanvalidate}.
