\section{Encoding the exclusions in a second out-neighborhood}\label{ch16:sec:second}
A directed graph whose vertices form a type \code{V} can be described in Lean by its edge relation \code{r : V -> V -> Prop}, a function of two inputs (Section~\ref{ch15:sec:injcomp}): it takes vertices \code{v} and \code{w} and returns the proposition \code{r v w}, read as $v\to w$. Nothing in such a relation rules out a loop $v\to v$, which Definition~\ref{ch07:def:directed} forbids, or opposite edges $v\to w$ and $w\to v$, which an oriented graph forbids, so a statement that needs to exclude them must say so in extra hypotheses.

Recall from Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour} that $w$ belongs to $N_2^+(v)$ when three conditions hold: $w\ne v$; $w$ is not an out-neighbor of $v$; and $v\to u\to w$ for some vertex $u$. In Lean they become a definition:
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
def Second {V : Type} (r : V -> V -> Prop)
    (v w : V) : Prop :=
  And (Not (w = v)) (And (Not (r v w))
    (Exists (fun u : V => And (r v u) (r u w))))
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\par
Here \code{Not P} is the negation of \code{P}, so \code{Not (w = v)} expresses $w\ne v$ and \code{Not (r v w)} says that there is no edge $v\to w$; the last part asks for at least one route $v\to u\to w$, not a unique one.

When the definition is unfolded, for instance with the tactic \code{unfold Second}, the infoview displays the body with the usual symbols:
\begin{center}
$\neg$\,\code{w = v} $\land$ $\neg$\,\code{r v w} $\land$ $\exists$\,\code{u, r v u} $\land$ \code{r u w}
\end{center}
Three conventions replace the parentheses: $\neg$ applies only to the proposition right after it, such as the whole equation \code{w = v}, and stops at the next $\land$; $\exists$\,\code{u,} reaches as far to the right as possible, so it covers both \code{r v u} and \code{r u w}; and $\land$ groups to the right, so $P\land Q\land R$ means $P\land(Q\land R)$. In this book's Lean code we write \code{And}, \code{Not}, and \code{Exists} instead, with parentheses that make the grouping visible.

To read the nesting, give the three conditions temporary names:
\[
 P:\ w\ne v,\qquad Q:\ \text{there is no edge }v\to w,\qquad
 R:\ v\to u\to w\text{ for some vertex }u.
\]
The definition has the shape \code{And P (And Q R)}. A proof \code{h} of it is a pair (Section~\ref{ch15:sec:conjunction}) whose first component proves $P$ and whose second component is itself a pair of proofs of $Q$ and $R$. So \code{h.left} proves $P$ and \code{h.right} proves \code{And Q R}; taking components once more, \code{h.right.left} proves $Q$ and \code{h.right.right} proves $R$. Taking a component of a pair in this way is called a \emph{projection}.

\pauseq{Take the oriented graph of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour} with edges $a\to b$, $b\to c$, $a\to c$, and $c\to d$, and let $v=a$. For each vertex $w$, decide which of the conditions $P$, $Q$, $R$ hold. For which $w$ does \code{Second r a w} hold?}{The out-neighbors of $a$ are $b$ and $c$, and neither has an edge to $a$ or to $b$, so $R$ fails for $w=a$ and for $w=b$. For $w=a$, $P$ fails and $Q$ holds; for $w=b$, $P$ holds and $Q$ fails, because of the edge $a\to b$. For $w=c$, $P$ and $R$ hold (through $a\to b\to c$), but $Q$ fails because of the edge $a\to c$. For $w=d$, all three hold: $d\ne a$, there is no edge $a\to d$, and $a\to c\to d$ is a route. So \code{Second r a w} holds only for $w=d$, in agreement with $N_2^+(a)=\{d\}$; the vertex $c$ is excluded by $Q$ alone, a condition that the shortcut of Section~\ref{ch16:sec:statement} leaves out.}

A useful consequence is that a second out-neighbor is never a first out-neighbor:
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem second_not_first {V : Type}
    (r : V -> V -> Prop) (v w : V)
    (h : Second r v w) : Not (r v w) := by
  exact h.right.left
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\par
The proof is the projection \code{h.right.left}, which extracts the proof of $Q$, exactly the claim \code{Not (r v w)}. Exercise~\ref{ex:16.3} asks you to extract the other two conditions.

Besides a type \code{V} of vertices and an edge relation \code{r}, the formal statement of Seymour's conjecture in the repository of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour}~\cite{oai173} has hypotheses saying that there are finitely many vertices, that there is at least one, and that \code{r} is oriented. Its condition for second out-neighbors is exactly the one above; its statement file~\cite{oaistatement}, named in the repository's configuration~\cite{oaiconfig}, even joins the three conditions by $\land$ in the same order. So the piece of the statement that the shortcut gets wrong can be checked on its own, before anyone studies the long proof (Section~\ref{ch17:sec:research} reads the full statement).
