\section{Which differences matter?}\label{ch06:sec:differences}
The fractions $1/2$ and $2/4$ look different, but they name the same rational number. Here is a second example. Three people, Alice, Ben, and Chen, are linked by acquaintance: Alice knows Ben and Ben knows Chen, but Alice and Chen have never met. If we replace Alice, Ben, and Chen by the numbers $1$, $2$, $3$, in that order, the description becomes ``$1$ is linked to $2$, and $2$ is linked to $3$.'' The labels have changed, but the pattern of connections has not. (Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs} studies such patterns under the name \emph{graphs}.) Finally, think of a necklace with three beads, two red and one blue. Reading the colors around the necklace gives red, red, blue, or red, blue, red, or blue, red, red, depending on the bead where we start. The written lists differ, yet it is the same necklace.

In each example we have chosen to ignore certain differences: how a fraction is written, what the people are called, where we start reading. Such a choice should be stated precisely, because whether two things count as ``the same'' depends on the question being asked. If the question is ``what is the numerator?'', then $1/2$ and $2/4$ give different answers, and one cannot be replaced by the other. Saying ``these are the same'' without saying in what sense can quietly change the problem.

\subsection*{Relations and equivalence relations}
To describe sameness precisely, we first need the more general idea of a relation. A \emph{relation} on a set $A$ is a rule that tells us, for every ordered pair $(x,y)$ of elements of $A$, whether $x$ is related to $y$. We write $x\sim y$, read ``$x$ is related to $y$,'' when it is. Two familiar examples are the order $x<y$ on the integers and the relation ``$x$ has the same birth month as $y$'' on a set of people. The pair is ordered because the answer can depend on the order: $1<2$ is true, but $2<1$ is false. One way to record a relation completely is to collect all the ordered pairs $(x,y)$ for which $x\sim y$. The result is a subset of the Cartesian product $A\times A$ of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets}.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Equivalence relation]\label{ch06:def:equivalence}
A relation $\sim$ on a set $A$ is an \emph{equivalence relation} if it has the following three properties.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Reflexivity:} $x\sim x$ for every $x\in A$.
\item \emph{Symmetry:} for all $x,y\in A$, if $x\sim y$, then $y\sim x$.
\item \emph{Transitivity:} for all $x,y,z\in A$, if $x\sim y$ and $y\sim z$, then $x\sim z$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

To show that a relation is an equivalence relation, we must check all three properties, and each of them for arbitrary elements of $A$, not only for a few convenient examples.

Consider the students taking a course, and let $x\sim y$ mean that $x$ and $y$ were born in the same month. To check the three properties, let $x$, $y$, and $z$ be any students in the course. Certainly $x$ was born in the same month as $x$, so the relation is reflexive. If $x$ has the same birth month as $y$, then $y$ has the same birth month as $x$, so the relation is symmetric. If $x$ and $y$ share a birth month, and $y$ and $z$ share a birth month, then all three were born in $y$'s month, so $x$ and $z$ share it as well. The relation is transitive, and therefore it is an equivalence relation.

An equivalence relation sorts the elements of its set into separate classes. The \emph{equivalence class} of an element $x\in A$ is the set of all elements of $A$ that are related to $x$:
\[
[x]=\{y\in A:y\sim x\}.
\]
For birth months, the class of a student born in March is the set of all students in the course who were born in March. There is one class for each month in which at least one student was born, and every student belongs to exactly one of these classes. Proposition~\ref{ch06:prop:classes} in Section~\ref{ch06:sec:residues} proves that every equivalence relation sorts its set in this clean way. Knowing that two students lie in the same class tells us that they share a birth month, and nothing more.

Many natural relations are not equivalence relations. The relation ``is a friend of'' usually fails transitivity: Alice may be friends with Ben, and Ben with Chen, while Alice and Chen are not friends at all. Relations that express rough similarity fail in the same way. On the real numbers, let $x\approx y$ mean $\abs{x-y}\le1$, so that $x$ and $y$ are within distance $1$ of each other. This relation is reflexive, because $\abs{x-x}=0$, and symmetric, because $\abs{x-y}=\abs{y-x}$. It is not transitive: $0\approx0.8$ and $0.8\approx1.6$, but $\abs{0-1.6}=1.6>1$.

So ``numbers close to each other'' cannot be sorted into separate classes. Suppose we tried, putting each number into the same class as every number close to it. Small steps lead from any number to any other: $0\approx0.8$, $0.8\approx1.6$, $1.6\approx2.4$, and so on. These steps would force $0$, $0.8$, $1.6$, $2.4$, and eventually every real number into one single class, although $0$ and $100$ are far from close. Looking at the numbers close to a single number shows the trouble in another way. The numbers close to $0$ form the interval $[-1,1]$, and the numbers close to $0.8$ form the interval $[-0.2,1.8]$. These two sets overlap, since both contain $0.5$, but they are not equal, since only the first contains $-1$. Proposition~\ref{ch06:prop:classes} will show that the classes of an equivalence relation never overlap in this partial way. Transitivity, the one property of Definition~\ref{ch06:def:equivalence} that $\approx$ lacks, is what rules it out.
