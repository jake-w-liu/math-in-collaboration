\section{Which differences matter?}\label{ch06:sec:differences}
The fractions $1/2$ and $2/4$ look different, but they name the same rational number. Similarly, suppose Alice knows Ben and Ben knows Chen, but Alice and Chen have never met. Replacing Alice, Ben, and Chen by $1$, $2$, $3$ gives ``$1$ is linked to $2$, and $2$ is linked to $3$.'' The labels have changed, but the pattern of connections has not. (Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs} studies such patterns under the name \emph{graphs}.)

In both examples we have chosen to ignore certain differences: how a fraction is written, or what the people are called. Such a choice should be stated precisely, because whether two things count as ``the same'' depends on the question: if the question is ``what is the numerator?'', then $1/2$ and $2/4$ give different answers.

\subsection*{Relations and equivalence relations}
To describe sameness precisely, we need the idea of a relation. A \emph{relation} on a set $A$ is a rule that tells us, for every ordered pair $(x,y)$ of elements of $A$, whether $x$ is related to $y$; when it is, we write $x\sim y$. Two familiar examples are the order $x<y$ on the integers and ``$x$ has the same birth month as $y$'' on a set of people. The pair is ordered because the answer can depend on the order: $1<2$ is true, but $2<1$ is false. A relation is recorded completely by the set of pairs $(x,y)$ with $x\sim y$, a subset of the Cartesian product $A\times A$ of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets}.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Equivalence relation]\label{ch06:def:equivalence}
A relation $\sim$ on a set $A$ is an \emph{equivalence relation} if it has the following three properties.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Reflexivity:} $x\sim x$ for every $x\in A$.
\item \emph{Symmetry:} for all $x,y\in A$, if $x\sim y$, then $y\sim x$.
\item \emph{Transitivity:} for all $x,y,z\in A$, if $x\sim y$ and $y\sim z$, then $x\sim z$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

To show that a relation is an equivalence relation, we must check all three properties for arbitrary elements of $A$, not only for a few convenient ones. For instance, on the students of a course, let $x\sim y$ mean that $x$ and $y$ were born in the same month. For any students $x$, $y$, $z$: certainly $x$ shares a birth month with $x$ (reflexivity); if $x$ shares a birth month with $y$, then $y$ shares one with $x$ (symmetry); and if $x$ and $y$ share a birth month, and so do $y$ and $z$, then all three were born in $y$'s month, so $x$ and $z$ share it (transitivity). So this is an equivalence relation.

An equivalence relation sorts the elements of its set into separate classes. The \emph{equivalence class} of an element $x\in A$ is the set of all elements of $A$ that are related to $x$:
\[
[x]=\{y\in A:y\sim x\}.
\]
For birth months, the class of a student born in March is the set of all students in the course born in March, and every student belongs to exactly one class. Proposition~\ref{ch06:prop:classes} in Section~\ref{ch06:sec:residues} proves that every equivalence relation sorts its set in this clean way.

Many natural relations are not equivalence relations; relations of rough similarity usually fail transitivity. For example, on the real numbers, let $x\approx y$ mean $\abs{x-y}\le1$. This relation is reflexive, because $\abs{x-x}=0$, and symmetric, because $\abs{x-y}=\abs{y-x}$. It is not transitive: $0\approx0.8$ and $0.8\approx1.6$, but $\abs{0-1.6}=1.6>1$. So ``numbers close to each other'' cannot be sorted into separate classes: the numbers close to $0$ form the interval $[-1,1]$, and those close to $0.8$ form $[-0.2,1.8]$, two sets that overlap (both contain $0.5$) without being equal (only the first contains $-1$). Proposition~\ref{ch06:prop:classes} will show that the classes of an equivalence relation never overlap in this partial way; transitivity, which $\approx$ lacks, is what rules it out.
