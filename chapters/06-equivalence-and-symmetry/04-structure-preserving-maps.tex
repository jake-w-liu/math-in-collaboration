\section{Structure-preserving maps}\label{ch06:sec:structure}
A \emph{structure} on a set is extra information that the set carries, such as an operation or a relation. An \emph{operation} on a set $S$ takes two elements of $S$ and returns an element of $S$; in the language of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets}, it is a function from $S\times S$ to $S$. Addition of integers and addition of residue classes are operations. A \emph{graph} (Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}) is a set of points, called \emph{vertices}, together with the information of which pairs of vertices are joined; joined vertices are called \emph{adjacent}.

For sets that have an addition, a map $T$ \emph{preserves addition} when
\[
T(x+y)=T(x)+T(y)\qquad\text{for all $x$ and $y$ in its domain.}
\]
The sum on the left is computed in the domain and the sum on the right in the codomain, and these may be different kinds of addition, as for the map $G$ at the end of Section~\ref{ch06:sec:well-defined}. For a simpler example, $V:\ZZ\to\ZZ$ with $V(x)=3x$ preserves addition, because $3(x+y)=3x+3y$. The map $U:\ZZ\to\ZZ$ with $U(x)=x+1$ does not: $U(x+y)=x+y+1$, while $U(x)+U(y)=x+y+2$. Preserving addition is part of the definition of a linear map in Chapter~\ref{ch:linear}.

For graphs, the structure is adjacency. An \emph{isomorphism} from one graph to another is a bijection $f$ from the vertices of the first graph to the vertices of the second that preserves adjacency in both directions: for all vertices $u$ and $v$ of the first graph, if $u$ and $v$ are adjacent, then $f(u)$ and $f(v)$ are adjacent, and if $f(u)$ and $f(v)$ are adjacent, then $u$ and $v$ are adjacent. In the acquaintance example of Section~\ref{ch06:sec:differences}, the map with $f(\text{Alice})=1$, $f(\text{Ben})=2$, $f(\text{Chen})=3$ is an isomorphism onto the pattern in which $1$ is linked to $2$ and $2$ to $3$. The bijection with $g(\text{Alice})=2$, $g(\text{Ben})=1$, $g(\text{Chen})=3$ is not: Ben and Chen know each other, but $1$ and $3$ are not linked. The second direction matters too: if $1$, $2$, and $3$ were all linked to one another, $f$ would still send linked people to linked numbers, but it would send Alice and Chen, who have never met, to the linked pair $1$ and $3$. So the second direction fails, as it should: a chain of three and a triangle are different patterns.

That second condition says that the inverse map $f^{-1}$ also preserves adjacency. In general, an \emph{isomorphism} is a bijection that preserves the structure and whose inverse also preserves it. What must be preserved depends on the subject: adjacency for graphs, addition for sets with an addition, distance for the sets with a distance of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}. Two objects joined by an isomorphism are called \emph{isomorphic}; they differ only in the names of their elements.

\subsection*{Symmetries}
A \emph{symmetry} of an object is an isomorphism from the object to itself. (This meaning of the word is unrelated to the symmetry property in Definition~\ref{ch06:def:equivalence}.) Picture four seats around a round table, labeled $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ in order, and take as the structure the information of which seats are next to each other, so that the seats are the vertices of a graph. Moving everyone one seat along is a symmetry. As a map on seat labels, it is
\[
r(i)=(i+1)\bmod4,
\]
so $r(0)=1$, $r(1)=2$, $r(2)=3$, and $r(3)=0$. Each seat is reached exactly once, so $r$ is a bijection. The neighboring pairs $0,1$ and $1,2$ and $2,3$ and $3,0$ go to $1,2$ and $2,3$ and $3,0$ and $0,1$, again neighbors, and the opposite pairs $0,2$ and $1,3$ go to $1,3$ and $2,0$, again opposite. So two seats are neighbors exactly when their images are. The inverse of $r$ is rotation by three seats, because $1+3=4\equiv0\pmod4$.

The symmetries of an object have four useful features. Let $f$, $g$, $h$ be symmetries.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Closure.} Doing $g$ and then $f$ is again a symmetry. The composition $f\circ g$ is a bijection, because compositions of injective maps are injective (Theorem~\ref{thm:injcomp}) and compositions of surjective maps are surjective (Exercise~\ref{ex:3.2}). It preserves the structure: for a graph, if $u$ and $v$ are adjacent, then so are $g(u)$ and $g(v)$, because $g$ is a symmetry, and then so are $f(g(u))$ and $f(g(v))$, because $f$ is one. Its inverse is $g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$, since $(f\circ g)(g^{-1}(f^{-1}(y)))=f(f^{-1}(y))=y$ and $g^{-1}(f^{-1}(f(g(x))))=g^{-1}(g(x))=x$ for all $x$ and $y$, and this inverse preserves the structure for the same reason.
\item \emph{Associativity.} $(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)$, because for every input $x$ both sides give $f(g(h(x)))$: apply $h$, then $g$, then $f$.
\item \emph{Identity.} The identity map of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:functions}, which we write simply $\id$ rather than $\id_A$, is a bijection, is its own inverse, and changes nothing, so it is a symmetry. Moreover $f\circ\id=\id\circ f=f$.
\item \emph{Inverses.} A symmetry $f$ is a bijection, so it has an inverse $f^{-1}$ (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}), and $f\circ f^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ f=\id$. These equations also say that $f$ is an inverse of $f^{-1}$, so $f^{-1}$ is a bijection, because a map with an inverse is bijective (Exercise~\ref{ex:3.5}). It preserves the structure, because an isomorphism preserves the structure in both directions. So $f^{-1}$ is again a symmetry.
\end{itemize}
These four features occur so often that they have a name.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Group]\label{ch06:def:group}
A \emph{group} is a set $S$ together with an operation $*$ on $S$, which combines any two elements $x,y\in S$ into an element $x*y\in S$, such that the following hold.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Associativity:} $(x*y)*z=x*(y*z)$ for all $x,y,z\in S$.
\item \emph{Identity:} there is an element $e\in S$ with $e*x=x*e=x$ for every $x\in S$.
\item \emph{Inverses:} for every $x\in S$ there is an element $y\in S$ with $x*y=y*x=e$, where $e$ is the identity element from the previous condition.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Closure is built into the definition, because an operation on $S$ must return an element of $S$. So the four features say that the symmetries of an object form a group under composition. The integers form a group under addition: addition is associative, $0$ is an identity, and $-a$ is an inverse of $a$. So does $\ZZ/m\ZZ$ under the addition of Section~\ref{ch06:sec:well-defined}, with identity $[0]_m$ and $[-a]_m$ as an inverse of $[a]_m$, since $[a]_m+[-a]_m=[0]_m$. Associativity is inherited from the integers: both $([a]_m+[b]_m)+[c]_m$ and $[a]_m+([b]_m+[c]_m)$ equal $[a+b+c]_m$. Not every operation gives a group. The integers under multiplication have the identity $1$, but $2$ has no inverse, because no integer $y$ satisfies $2y=1$.

The definition does not require that $x*y=y*x$. A group in which this holds for all $x$ and $y$ is called \emph{commutative}. The integers under addition form a commutative group, but many groups of symmetries do not. Place three letters in a row as $ABC$. Let $s$ swap the letters in the first two positions, and let $t$ swap the letters in the last two positions. Doing $s$ and then $t$ gives
\[
ABC\ \to\ BAC\ \to\ BCA,
\]
while doing $t$ and then $s$ gives
\[
ABC\ \to\ ACB\ \to\ CAB.
\]
So $t\circ s\ne s\circ t$. The rearrangements of three positions, that is, the bijections from a three-element set to itself, are the symmetries of a set with no extra structure, and they form a group that $s$ and $t$ show is not commutative.

Back at the table, let $r_j$ move everyone $j$ seats along, so that $r_j(i)=(i+j)\bmod4$ for $j=0,1,2,3$; thus $r_1=r$. Applying $r_3$ and then $r_2$ moves everyone five seats, which is the same as moving one seat, so $r_2\circ r_3=r_1$. In general, composing two rotations adds their amounts modulo four, so the result is again a rotation. The identity is $r_0$, the rotations $r_1$ and $r_3$ are inverses of each other, and $r_2$ is its own inverse (Exercise~\ref{ex:6.3}). Associativity holds for all compositions of maps. So the four rotations form a group on their own, and a commutative one, because addition of their amounts is commutative.

The rotation $r_j$ thus behaves exactly like the residue class $[j]_4$. To say this precisely, let $\varphi$ send $[j]_4$ to $r_j$, for $j=0,1,2,3$. Since every class modulo four is exactly one of $[0]_4,[1]_4,[2]_4,[3]_4$ (Section~\ref{ch06:sec:residues}), $\varphi$ is a well-defined function from $\ZZ/4\ZZ$ to the set of rotations, and a bijection, since the four classes go to the four rotations, one each. It preserves the group operation:
\[
\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)\circ\varphi(y)\qquad\text{for all classes $x$ and $y$ modulo four.}
\]
Indeed, take $x=[j]_4$ and $y=[k]_4$ with $j,k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, and let $c=(j+k)\bmod4$. Then $x+y=[c]_4$, so $\varphi(x+y)=r_c$, while $r_j\circ r_k$ moves everyone $k$ seats and then $j$ more, which is $r_c$ as well. Read backward, the same calculation shows that $\varphi^{-1}$ preserves the structure: $\varphi^{-1}(r_j\circ r_k)=[c]_4=[j]_4+[k]_4=\varphi^{-1}(r_j)+\varphi^{-1}(r_k)$. So $\varphi$ is an isomorphism from the group $\ZZ/4\ZZ$ to the group of rotations: the two differ only in the names of their elements.
