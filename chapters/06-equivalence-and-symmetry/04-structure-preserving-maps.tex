\section{Structure-preserving maps}\label{ch06:sec:structure}
A \emph{structure} on a set is extra information that the set carries, such as an operation or a relation. An \emph{operation} on a set $S$ takes two elements of $S$ and returns an element of $S$. In the language of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets}, it is a function from $S\times S$ to $S$. Addition of integers and addition of residue classes are operations. The integers carry several structures at once: addition, multiplication, and the order $\le$. A \emph{graph}, studied in Chapter~\ref{ch:graphs}, is a set of points, called \emph{vertices}, together with the information of which pairs of vertices are joined; joined vertices are called \emph{adjacent}. A set with a distance, studied in Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}, carries a number measuring how far apart any two of its points are. When you meet a new subject, ask what its objects carry beyond being members of a set.

For sets that have an addition, a map $T$ \emph{preserves addition} when
\[
T(x+y)=T(x)+T(y)\qquad\text{for all $x$ and $y$ in its domain.}
\]
The sum on the left is computed in the domain and the sum on the right in the codomain, and these may be different kinds of addition. The map $G$ from $\ZZ/4\ZZ$ to $\ZZ/2\ZZ$ at the end of Section~\ref{ch06:sec:well-defined} is an example. For a simpler one, the map $V:\ZZ\to\ZZ$ with $V(x)=3x$ preserves addition, because $3(x+y)=3x+3y$. The map $U:\ZZ\to\ZZ$ with $U(x)=x+1$ does not: $U(x+y)=x+y+1$, while $U(x)+U(y)=x+y+2$. Preserving addition is part of the definition of a linear map in Chapter~\ref{ch:linear}.

For graphs, the structure is adjacency. An \emph{isomorphism} from one graph to another is a bijection $f$ from the vertices of the first graph to the vertices of the second that preserves adjacency in both directions: for all vertices $u$ and $v$ of the first graph, if $u$ and $v$ are adjacent, then $f(u)$ and $f(v)$ are adjacent, and if $f(u)$ and $f(v)$ are adjacent, then $u$ and $v$ are adjacent. In the acquaintance example of Section~\ref{ch06:sec:differences}, the map with $f(\text{Alice})=1$, $f(\text{Ben})=2$, $f(\text{Chen})=3$ is an isomorphism onto the pattern in which $1$ is linked to $2$ and $2$ to $3$. The bijection with $g(\text{Alice})=2$, $g(\text{Ben})=1$, $g(\text{Chen})=3$ is not an isomorphism: Ben and Chen know each other, but $g(\text{Ben})=1$ and $g(\text{Chen})=3$ are not linked. The second direction matters as well. Suppose instead that $1$, $2$, and $3$ were all linked to one another. The map $f$ would still send linked people to linked numbers, but it would send Alice and Chen, who have never met, to the linked pair $1$ and $3$. A chain of three people and a triangle of three mutually linked numbers are different patterns, and the second direction is what keeps $f$ from counting as an isomorphism between them.

The first direction says that $f$ sends adjacent vertices to adjacent vertices. The second says the same about the inverse map $f^{-1}$, which sends $f(u)$ and $f(v)$ back to $u$ and $v$. In general, an \emph{isomorphism} is a bijection that preserves the structure and whose inverse also preserves it. What must be preserved depends on the subject: adjacency for graphs, addition for sets with an addition, distance for sets with a distance. Two objects joined by an isomorphism are called \emph{isomorphic}. They differ only in the names of their elements.

\subsection*{Symmetries}
A \emph{symmetry} of an object is an isomorphism from the object to itself. This is a second meaning of the word, unrelated to the symmetry property of a relation in Definition~\ref{ch06:def:equivalence}. Picture four seats around a round table, labeled $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ in order. As the structure, take the information of which seats are next to each other: $0$ and $1$, $1$ and $2$, $2$ and $3$, and $3$ and $0$. In other words, the seats are the vertices of a graph, and two seats are adjacent when they are next to each other. Moving everyone one seat along is a symmetry. As a map on seat labels, it is
\[
r(i)=(i+1)\bmod4,
\]
so $r(0)=1$, $r(1)=2$, $r(2)=3$, and $r(3)=0$. Let us check that $r$ is a symmetry. It is a bijection, because each of the four seats is reached exactly once. The four pairs of neighbors, $0,1$ and $1,2$ and $2,3$ and $3,0$, go to the pairs $1,2$ and $2,3$ and $3,0$ and $0,1$, which are again neighbors. The two pairs of seats that are not neighbors, namely the opposite pairs $0,2$ and $1,3$, go to the pairs $1,3$ and $2,0$, which are again opposite. So two seats are neighbors exactly when their images are neighbors. The inverse of $r$ is rotation by three seats, because moving one seat and then three more is a full turn: $1+3=4\equiv0\pmod4$.

The symmetries of an object, taken together, have four useful features. Let $f$, $g$, and $h$ be symmetries.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Closure.} Doing $g$ and then $f$ is again a symmetry. The composition $f\circ g$ is a bijection, because compositions of injective maps are injective (Theorem~\ref{thm:injcomp}) and compositions of surjective maps are surjective (Exercise~\ref{ex:3.2}). It preserves the structure. For a graph, for instance, if $u$ and $v$ are adjacent, then so are $g(u)$ and $g(v)$, because $g$ is a symmetry, and then so are $f(g(u))$ and $f(g(v))$, because $f$ is one. Its inverse is $g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$, which first undoes $f$ and then undoes $g$. (Check: $(f\circ g)(g^{-1}(f^{-1}(y)))=f(f^{-1}(y))=y$ for every $y$, and $g^{-1}(f^{-1}(f(g(x))))=g^{-1}(g(x))=x$ for every $x$.) This inverse preserves the structure for the same reason, since $f^{-1}$ and $g^{-1}$ do.
\item \emph{Associativity.} $(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)$. For every input $x$, both sides give $f(g(h(x)))$: the left side is $(f\circ g)(h(x))=f(g(h(x)))$, and the right side is $f((g\circ h)(x))=f(g(h(x)))$. Both mean ``apply $h$, then $g$, then $f$,'' so the brackets do not matter.
\item \emph{Identity.} Doing nothing, that is, applying the identity map of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:functions}, is a symmetry: it is a bijection, it is its own inverse, and it changes nothing, so it certainly preserves the structure. Section~\ref{ch03:sec:functions} wrote the identity map on a set $A$ as $\id_A$. Here the identity map always acts on the object whose symmetries we study, so we leave out the subscript and write simply $\id$. Moreover $f\circ\id=\id\circ f=f$.
\item \emph{Inverses.} A symmetry $f$ is a bijection, so it has an inverse $f^{-1}$ (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}), and $f\circ f^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ f=\id$. These two equations also say that $f$ is an inverse of $f^{-1}$, so $f^{-1}$ is a bijection, because a map with an inverse is bijective (Exercise~\ref{ex:3.5}). It preserves the structure, because an isomorphism preserves the structure in both directions. So $f^{-1}$ is again a symmetry.
\end{itemize}
These four features occur so often that they have a name.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Group]\label{ch06:def:group}
A \emph{group} is a set $S$ together with an operation $*$ on $S$, which combines any two elements $x,y\in S$ into an element $x*y\in S$, such that the following hold.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Associativity:} $(x*y)*z=x*(y*z)$ for all $x,y,z\in S$.
\item \emph{Identity:} there is an element $e\in S$ with $e*x=x*e=x$ for every $x\in S$.
\item \emph{Inverses:} for every $x\in S$ there is an element $y\in S$ with $x*y=y*x=e$, where $e$ is the identity element from the previous condition.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Closure is built into the definition, because an operation on $S$ must return an element of $S$. The four features above say that the symmetries of an object form a group under composition. The integers form a group under addition: the sum of two integers is an integer, addition is associative, $0$ is an identity, and $-a$ is an inverse of $a$. So does $\ZZ/m\ZZ$ under the addition of Section~\ref{ch06:sec:well-defined}. Its identity is $[0]_m$, and $[-a]_m$ is an inverse of $[a]_m$, because $[a]_m+[-a]_m=[0]_m$. Associativity is inherited from the integers: both $([a]_m+[b]_m)+[c]_m$ and $[a]_m+([b]_m+[c]_m)$ equal $[a+b+c]_m$. Not every operation gives a group. The integers under multiplication have the identity $1$, but $2$ has no inverse, because no integer $y$ satisfies $2y=1$.

The definition does not require that $x*y=y*x$. A group in which this holds for all $x$ and $y$ is called \emph{commutative}. The integers under addition form a commutative group, but many groups of symmetries are not commutative. Here is an example. Place three letters in a row as $ABC$. Let $s$ swap the letters in the first two positions, and let $t$ swap the letters in the last two positions. Doing $s$ and then $t$ gives
\[
ABC\ \to\ BAC\ \to\ BCA,
\]
while doing $t$ and then $s$ gives
\[
ABC\ \to\ ACB\ \to\ CAB.
\]
The results differ, so $t\circ s\ne s\circ t$. The rearrangements of three positions, that is, the bijections from a three-element set to itself, are the symmetries of a set that carries no extra structure. They form a group under composition, and since $s$ and $t$ are two of them, this group is not commutative.

Now let us see the four features in the rotations of the table. Write $r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3$, where $r_j$ moves everyone $j$ seats along, so that $r_j(i)=(i+j)\bmod4$ and $r_1$ is the map $r$ from before. Applying $r_3$ and then $r_2$ moves everyone five seats, which is the same as moving one seat, so $r_2\circ r_3=r_1$. In general, composing two rotations adds their amounts modulo four, so the result is always one of $r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3$; this is closure. The identity is $r_0$. The rotations $r_1$ and $r_3$ are inverses of each other, as we saw for $r$, and $r_2$ is its own inverse (Exercise~\ref{ex:6.3} asks you to check this). Associativity needs no separate check, because composition of maps is always associative. These particular symmetries also commute, because addition of their amounts does. The rotations are not the only symmetries of the table. For instance, the reflection that keeps seats $0$ and $2$ in place and swaps seats $1$ and $3$ also keeps neighbors next to each other and opposite seats opposite, so it is a symmetry as well. But the four rotations already form a group on their own.

Naming the pattern helps us recognize it elsewhere. The rotation $r_j$ behaves exactly like the residue class $[j]_4$: composing rotations adds their amounts modulo four, just as adding classes adds representatives modulo four. To say this precisely, let $\varphi$ send the class $[j]_4$ to the rotation $r_j$, for $j=0,1,2,3$. Every class modulo four is exactly one of $[0]_4,[1]_4,[2]_4,[3]_4$ (Section~\ref{ch06:sec:residues}), so the rule assigns each class exactly one rotation, whichever representative we start from. In other words, $\varphi$ is a well-defined function from $\ZZ/4\ZZ$ to the set of rotations. It is a bijection, because the four classes go to the four rotations, one each. The structure that matters here is the group operation: addition of classes on one side, composition of rotations on the other. So $\varphi$ preserves the structure when
\[
\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)\circ\varphi(y)\qquad\text{for all classes $x$ and $y$ modulo four.}
\]
To check this, take $x=[j]_4$ and $y=[k]_4$ with $j$ and $k$ in $\{0,1,2,3\}$, and let $c=(j+k)\bmod4$. Then $x+y=[j+k]_4=[c]_4$, so $\varphi(x+y)=r_c$. On the other side, $\varphi(x)\circ\varphi(y)=r_j\circ r_k$ moves everyone $k$ seats and then $j$ more, which is $r_c$ as well. The inverse of $\varphi$ preserves the structure too, by the same calculation read backward: $\varphi^{-1}(r_j\circ r_k)=\varphi^{-1}(r_c)=[c]_4=[j]_4+[k]_4=\varphi^{-1}(r_j)+\varphi^{-1}(r_k)$. So $\varphi$ is an isomorphism from the group $\ZZ/4\ZZ$ to the group of rotations. The two groups differ only in the names of their elements.
