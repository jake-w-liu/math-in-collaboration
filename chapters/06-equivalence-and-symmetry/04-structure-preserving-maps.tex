\section{Structure-preserving maps}
A structure is a set together with specified operations or relations. The integers have addition, multiplication, and order. A graph has vertices and adjacency. A space with distance has points and a distance function. When learning a new subject, ask what its objects carry in addition to membership in a set.

For objects with addition, a map $T$ preserves addition when
\[
T(x+y)=T(x)+T(y).
\]
This is part of the definition of a linear map in Chapter~10. For a graph, an isomorphism is a bijective renaming of vertices that preserves adjacency in both directions. The general word \emph{isomorphism} means a reversible map preserving the specified structure; what must be preserved depends on the subject.

A symmetry is an isomorphism from an object to itself. Rotating four positions cyclically is a symmetry of their circular arrangement. Label the positions $0,1,2,3$ and set $r(i)=i+1\pmod4$. Applying $r$ four times returns to the original position. Its inverse is rotation by three positions, because $1+3\equiv0\pmod4$.

The symmetries of an object, taken together, have four useful features.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Closure.} Doing one symmetry and then another is again a symmetry of the same object: each step preserves the structure, so both steps together preserve it too. The combined operation stays inside the collection.
\item \emph{Associativity.} For three symmetries, $(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)$. Both sides mean ``apply $h$, then $g$, then $f$,'' so the brackets do not matter.
\item \emph{Identity.} Doing nothing, the identity map, is a symmetry, and composing it with any symmetry leaves that symmetry unchanged.
\item \emph{Inverses.} Every symmetry can be undone by another symmetry in the collection.
\end{itemize}
These four features occur so often that they have a name. A \emph{group} is a nonempty set together with an operation that combines any two of its elements into an element of the same set, in such a way that the operation is associative, there is an identity element, and every element has an inverse. Symmetries under composition form a group. So do the integers under addition: the sum of two integers is an integer, addition is associative, $0$ is the identity, and $-a$ undoes $a$.

The definition does not require that the order of combination is irrelevant. When $f\circ g=g\circ f$ for all elements, the group is called \emph{commutative}. The integers under addition are commutative, but many groups of symmetries are not. For example, rearrangements of three objects form a group under composition, and swapping the first two objects and then the last two does not give the same rearrangement as performing those two swaps in the opposite order.

Let us see the four features in the smallest interesting example. For the four rotations, write $r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3$, where $r_j$ rotates by $j$ positions. Applying $r_3$ and then $r_2$ rotates by five positions, which is the same as rotating by one position, so $r_2\circ r_3=r_1$. In general, composing two rotations adds their amounts modulo four, so the result is always one of $r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3$; this verifies closure. The identity is $r_0$; the inverse of $r_1$ is $r_3$; and $r_2$ is its own inverse. These particular rotations also commute, because addition of their amounts does. We do not need any general group theory to use these transformations, but naming the pattern helps us recognize it when it appears in other places.
