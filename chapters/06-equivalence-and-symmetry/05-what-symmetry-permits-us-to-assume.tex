\section{What symmetry permits us to assume}\label{ch06:sec:wlog}
Consider a sequence of signs $a=(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1})$ with $n\ge1$, where each entry $a_i$ is $1$ or $-1$; we number the positions from $0$, so the last entry is $a_{n-1}$. Rows of signs like this appeared in Section~\ref{ch05:sec:invariant}, and Chapter~\ref{ch:signs} studies them in depth. A \emph{pair product} is a product $a_ia_j$ of two entries of the sequence. Negating every sign turns $a$ into $-a=(-a_0,-a_1,\ldots,-a_{n-1})$ and does not change any pair product, because
\[
(-a_i)(-a_j)=a_ia_j.
\]
For example, $(-1,1,1,-1)$ and its negation $(1,-1,-1,1)$ have the same products of neighboring entries: $-1$, $1$, $-1$. So any quantity built only from pair products, such as the sum of neighboring products $a_0a_1+a_1a_2+\cdots+a_{n-2}a_{n-1}$, takes the same value on $a$ and on $-a$.

Say that two sign sequences $a$ and $b$ of the same length \emph{have the same pair products} if $a_ia_j=b_ib_j$ for all positions $i$ and $j$. A property of sign sequences \emph{depends only on the pair products} if any two sequences with the same pair products either both have the property or both lack it; ``the sum of neighboring products is $-1$'' is an example. When we study such a property, we may assume that the first sign is $1$: if a sequence starts with $-1$, negate it.

Replacing every object by a convenient standard form in this way is called a \emph{normalization}. Two facts make this one legitimate.
\begin{itemize}
\item The change preserves the property under study: since $a$ and $-a$ have the same pair products, a sequence has the property exactly when its negation has it.
\item Every case can be brought into the standard form: a sequence either begins with $1$ already, or it begins with $-1$ and its negation begins with $1$.
\end{itemize}
If either fact fails, the reduction fails too. For a failure of the first fact, consider the claim that the two signs of a sequence of length two add up to at least $0$. It holds for the sequences $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ that begin with $1$, with sums $2$ and $0$, but it is false for $(-1,-1)$, with sum $-2$. Negation turns the sum into its negative, so it does not preserve the property, and checking the sequences that begin with $1$ told us nothing about the others.

The second fact can fail as well. Negation can always make the first sign equal to $1$, but not always the first and the last: $(1,-1)$ and its negation $(-1,1)$ each have one sign of each kind. So the normalization ``the first and last signs are both $1$'' leaves such sequences out, even for a property preserved by negation. For example, the property $a_0a_{n-1}=1$ depends only on a pair product and holds whenever the first and last signs are both $1$, yet it fails for $(1,-1)$. Checking only the normalized sequences would wrongly suggest that it always holds.

\subsection*{What ``without loss of generality'' must justify}
A reduction like this is often announced with the phrase \emph{without loss of generality}: ``Without loss of generality, $a_0=1$.'' Read the phrase as a promise that no case has been lost, not as permission to add a convenient extra condition. It must be backed by the two facts above.

Here is the argument behind the promise for sign sequences. Let $P$ be a property of sign sequences that depends only on the pair products, and suppose we have proved that $P$ holds for every sign sequence of length $n$ whose first entry is $1$. Let $a$ be any sign sequence of length $n$. If $a_0=1$, we are done. If $a_0=-1$, put $b=-a$. Then $b_0=1$, so $P$ holds for $b$. Since $a$ and $b$ have the same pair products and $P$ depends only on these, $P$ holds for $a$ as well. So $P$ holds for every sign sequence of length $n$. Both facts were used: the second to produce $b$ with $b_0=1$, and the first to carry $P$ from $b$ back to $a$.
