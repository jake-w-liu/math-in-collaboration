\section{Vectors are ordered measurements}\label{ch10:sec:vectors}
Let $n$ be a positive integer. A \emph{vector} in $\RR^n$ is an ordered list $x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)$ of $n$ real numbers, called its \emph{coordinates}, and $\RR^n$ is the set of all such lists. We picture a vector $(x_1,x_2)$ of $\RR^2$ as the point of the plane $x_1$ units to the right of a fixed point, the \emph{origin}, and $x_2$ units above it (a negative coordinate means a step left or down). But coordinates need not describe a position: a shop could record $3$ kilograms of flour and $5$ kilograms of sugar as the vector $(3,5)$. As with the ordered pairs of Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets}, order matters: two vectors are equal exactly when they agree in every coordinate, so $(1,3)\ne(3,1)$.

Vectors are added coordinate by coordinate, and multiplying a vector by a real number $c$ multiplies every coordinate by $c$:
\begin{gather*}
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)+(y_1,\ldots,y_n)=(x_1+y_1,\ldots,x_n+y_n),\\
c(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=(cx_1,\ldots,cx_n).
\end{gather*}
For example, $(1,3)+(2,-1)=(3,2)$ and $2(1,3)=(2,6)$. In this setting a real number such as $c$ is called a \emph{scalar}. The \emph{zero vector}, written simply $0$, has every coordinate equal to zero; we write $-x=(-1)x$ and $x-y=x+(-y)$. The familiar rules of arithmetic, such as $x+y=y+x$, $x+0=x$, $x-x=0$, $c(x+y)=cx+cy$ and $(a+b)x=ax+bx$, carry over to vectors, because each can be checked one coordinate at a time.

A \emph{linear combination} of vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is a vector of the form
\[
a_1v_1+a_2v_2+\cdots+a_kv_k,
\]
where the scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_k$ are the \emph{coefficients} of the combination. Here the subscripts number the vectors in a list; each $v_j$ is a whole vector, not a coordinate. For example, $2(1,3)-(2,-1)=(0,7)$ is a linear combination of $(1,3)$ and $(2,-1)$ with coefficients $2$ and $-1$.

A list of vectors in $\RR^n$ is a \emph{basis} of $\RR^n$ when every vector of $\RR^n$ can be written as a linear combination of the list in exactly one way. The simplest example is the list $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, where $e_j$ has a $1$ in position $j$ and a $0$ in every other position; in the plane, $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$. The vector $x_je_j$ has $x_j$ in position $j$ and zeros elsewhere, so adding these $n$ vectors gives
\[
x_1e_1+x_2e_2+\cdots+x_ne_n=(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n).
\]
So every vector is a combination of $e_1,\ldots,e_n$. The coefficients are forced: if $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=a_1e_1+\cdots+a_ne_n$, the same calculation shows that the right side is $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$, and comparing coordinates gives $a_j=x_j$ for every $j$. Hence $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a basis, the \emph{standard basis} of $\RR^n$. In $\RR^3$, for instance, $(4,5,0)=4e_1+5e_2+0e_3$. (The number of coordinates of $e_j$ depends on $n$: $e_1$ is $(1,0)$ in the plane but $(1,0,0)$ in $\RR^3$.)

\subsection*{Two requirements: reaching every vector, and reaching it only once}
A basis meets two separate demands. Call a way of writing a vector as a linear combination of a given list a \emph{representation} of the vector. First, every vector must have a representation, as $(3,-2)=3e_1-2e_2$ does in the standard basis; a list with this property is said to \emph{span} $\RR^n$. Second, the representation must be \emph{unique}.

Neither demand implies the other. The list consisting of the single vector $(1,1)$ passes the uniqueness test, since $a(1,1)=b(1,1)$ means $(a,a)=(b,b)$, so $a=b$. But it does not span the plane: every multiple $(a,a)$ has equal coordinates, so none equals $(1,0)$. In the other direction, the list $(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$ spans the plane, since already $(x_1,x_2)=x_1(1,0)+x_2(0,1)+0(1,1)$. But it fails the uniqueness test: the vector $(1,1)$ is both $0(1,0)+0(0,1)+1(1,1)$ and $1(1,0)+1(0,1)+0(1,1)$.

Uniqueness has a convenient test. A list $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is called \emph{linearly independent} if the only linear combination of it that equals the zero vector is the one whose coefficients are all zero:
\[
a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k=0\quad\text{implies}\quad a_1=a_2=\cdots=a_k=0.
\]
A list is linearly independent exactly when no vector has two different representations. Indeed, suppose first that the list is linearly independent and that some vector $x$ has two representations,
\[
x=a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k\qquad\text{and}\qquad x=b_1v_1+\cdots+b_kv_k.
\]
Subtract the second from the first and collect the terms that contain each $v_j$:
\[
(a_1-b_1)v_1+\cdots+(a_k-b_k)v_k=x-x=0.
\]
By independence, every difference $a_j-b_j$ is zero, so the two representations were the same after all. Conversely, if the list is not linearly independent, then some combination with a nonzero coefficient equals $0$, and so does the combination with all coefficients zero: the zero vector has two representations. For the list $(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$, subtracting the two representations of $(1,1)$ above gives
\[
(-1)(1,0)+(-1)(0,1)+1(1,1)=(0,0),
\]
so this list is not linearly independent. In short, a basis is a list that spans and is linearly independent.

\pauseq{Is the list $(1,2),(2,4)$ a basis of $\RR^2$?}{No, and it fails both requirements. It is not linearly independent, because $2(1,2)+(-1)(2,4)=(0,0)$. It does not span the plane either: every combination $a(1,2)+b(2,4)=(a+2b,\,2a+4b)$ has second coordinate equal to twice its first, so $(1,0)$ is never reached.}

\subsection*{A basis can be chosen to fit the problem}
The standard basis is not the only basis of the plane. Take $w_1=(1,1)$ and $w_2=(1,-1)$. Which coefficients $a$ and $b$ give $aw_1+bw_2=(x_1,x_2)$? Since $aw_1+bw_2=(a+b,\,a-b)$, we need $a+b=x_1$ and $a-b=x_2$. Adding and subtracting these equations gives $2a=x_1+x_2$ and $2b=x_1-x_2$, so the only possible coefficients are
\[
a=\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b=\frac{x_1-x_2}{2},
\]
and these do work, since with them $a+b=x_1$ and $a-b=x_2$. So every vector of the plane has exactly one representation, and $w_1,w_2$ is a basis. For instance, $(3,1)=2w_1+1w_2$. The vectors themselves have not changed; only the way we describe them has.

This basis suits questions about totals: the shop's stock $aw_1+bw_2=(a+b,\,a-b)$ has total $(a+b)+(a-b)=2a$, which depends only on the first coefficient (for $(3,5)$, $a=4$ and the total is $8$ kilograms), while changing $b$ moves stock from one item to the other. Choosing a description that suits the question is Habit~1 (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:first-proof}) once more, now applied to vectors instead of even numbers.
