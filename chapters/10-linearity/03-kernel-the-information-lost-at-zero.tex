\section{Kernel: the information lost at zero}\label{ch10:sec:kernel}
The \emph{kernel} of a linear map $T:\RR^n\to\RR^m$ is the set of inputs that $T$ sends to the zero vector:
\[
\ker T=\{x\in\RR^n:T(x)=0\}.
\]
The kernel always contains $0$, because $T(0)=0$ (Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps}). For a matrix $A$, $\ker A$ means the kernel of the map $x\mapsto Ax$. As an example, let $P:\RR^2\to\RR^2$ be the map $P(x_1,x_2)=(x_1,0)$. It is linear, because
\[
P(ax+by)=(ax_1+by_1,\,0)=a(x_1,0)+b(y_1,0)=aP(x)+bP(y).
\]
Its output is the zero vector exactly when $x_1=0$, so $\ker P=\{(0,t):t\in\RR\}$, the vertical axis.

Why look at the kernel? If $T(x)=T(y)$, then linearity gives $T(x-y)=T(x)-T(y)=0$: two inputs with the same output differ by a vector of the kernel. For $P$, the inputs $(2,5)$ and $(2,7)$ have the same output and differ by $(0,-2)\in\ker P$. So the kernel collects in one place all the information that $T$ cannot see.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:kernel}
A linear map $T:\RR^n\to\RR^m$ is injective if and only if its kernel consists only of the zero vector, that is, $\ker T=\{0\}$.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $T$ is injective, and let $x\in\ker T$. Then $T(x)=0=T(0)$, and injectivity gives $x=0$. So the kernel contains no vector other than $0$.

Conversely, suppose that $\ker T=\{0\}$, and let $x,y\in\RR^n$ satisfy $T(x)=T(y)$. Linearity gives $T(x-y)=T(x)-T(y)=0$, so $x-y$ lies in the kernel. Hence $x-y=0$, that is, $x=y$. Therefore $T$ is injective.
\end{proof}

Both directions used linearity: the first needed $T(0)=0$, and the second turned $T(x)=T(y)$ into $T(x-y)=0$. Without linearity the theorem fails: the map $F:\RR\to\RR$ with $F(x)=x^2$ sends only $0$ to $0$, yet $F(1)=F(-1)$.

\subsection*{Uniqueness is not existence}
Fix a target $b\in\RR^m$. If $x_0$ is one solution of $T(x)=b$, then the solutions are exactly the vectors $x_0+k$ with $k\in\ker T$. Indeed, each such vector is a solution, because $T(x_0+k)=T(x_0)+T(k)=b+0=b$. And for every solution $x$, the vector $k=x-x_0$ satisfies $T(k)=T(x)-T(x_0)=b-b=0$, so $k\in\ker T$ and $x=x_0+k$. In particular, when $\ker T=\{0\}$, each equation $T(x)=b$ has at most one solution.

But at most one is not exactly one: a kernel equal to $\{0\}$ does not promise that every target is reached. Regarding $\RR$ as $\RR^1$, the map $t\mapsto(t,0)$ from $\RR$ to $\RR^2$ is linear, by the same check as for $P$, and its kernel is $\{0\}$, so it is injective. But no input reaches $(0,1)$, so it is not surjective.

It is tempting to think that two equations in two unknowns always have exactly one solution. The map $T(x_1,x_2)=(x_1+x_2,\,2x_1+2x_2)$ is linear, being recorded by the matrix with rows $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$. Solving $T(x_1,x_2)=(b_1,b_2)$ means solving $x_1+x_2=b_1$ and $2x_1+2x_2=b_2$, where the second left side is always twice the first; so the target $(1,0)$, which would need $0=2\cdot1$, is never reached. Both coordinates of $T(x_1,x_2)$ are zero exactly when $x_2=-x_1$, so $\ker T=\{(t,-t):t\in\RR\}$. The target $(2,4)$ has the solution $(2,0)$, so its solutions are exactly the vectors $(2,0)+(t,-t)=(2+t,\,-t)$ with $t\in\RR$. Counting equations is not enough: what matters is that the map loses information, sending a whole line of vectors $(t,-t)$ to zero.

\subsection*{Inverting the matrix of sums and differences}
For the matrix $A$ of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps}, apply its sum-and-difference rule twice:
\begin{align*}
 A(Ax)&=A(x_1+x_2,\ x_1-x_2)\\
 &=\big((x_1+x_2)+(x_1-x_2),\ (x_1+x_2)-(x_1-x_2)\big)\\
 &=(2x_1,\,2x_2)=2x.
\end{align*}
This identity shows that $A$ is a bijection and gives its inverse. If $Ax=0$, then $2x=A(Ax)=A0=0$, so $x=0$. Thus $\ker A=\{0\}$, and $A$ is injective by Theorem~\ref{thm:kernel}. For surjectivity, take any target $b\in\RR^2$ and try $x=\tfrac12Ab$. By linearity,
\[
Ax=A\bigl(\tfrac12Ab\bigr)=\tfrac12A(Ab)=\tfrac12\cdot2b=b.
\]
So $A$ is a bijection, and $b\mapsto\tfrac12Ab$ is its inverse: we have just checked that $A\bigl(\tfrac12Ab\bigr)=b$, and in the other order $\tfrac12A(Ax)=\tfrac12\cdot2x=x$.

In coordinates, the inverse sends $b=(b_1,b_2)$ to $\bigl((b_1+b_2)/2,\ (b_1-b_2)/2\bigr)$, and these are exactly the coefficients of $b$ in the basis $w_1,w_2$ of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:vectors}. That is no coincidence: the columns of $A$ are $w_1$ and $w_2$, so $Ax=x_1w_1+x_2w_2$, and solving $Ax=b$ means finding the coefficients of $b$ in this basis. Inverting $A$ and changing to the basis of its columns are the same problem, stated in two languages.
