\section{Deriving a bound by removing a component}\label{ch10:sec:cs}
How large can a dot product be, compared with the lengths of the two vectors? When $v=u$, the dot product $u\cdot u=\norm{u}_2^2$ is exactly the product $\norm{u}_2\norm{u}_2$ of the two lengths. A natural guess is that it can never be larger in size:
\[
|u\cdot v|\le\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2.
\]
Test it on $u=(3,1)$ and $v=(1,1)$: here $u\cdot v=4$, $\norm{u}_2=\sqrt{10}$ and $\norm{v}_2=\sqrt2$. To compare $4$ with $\sqrt{10}\sqrt2$ without decimals, compare squares: $4^2=16\le20=(\sqrt{10}\sqrt2)^2$, so the guess holds here. This is legitimate because for nonnegative $p$ and $q$, $p\le q$ exactly when $p^2\le q^2$: by the squaring rules of Section~\ref{sec:magnitude}, $0\le p\le q$ gives $p^2\le q^2$, while $p>q\ge0$ gives $p^2>q^2$, so $p^2\le q^2$ rules out $p>q$.

How could we find a reason that works for all vectors? Start from the fact that $u-tv$ has a nonnegative squared length for every real number $t$:
\[
(u-tv)\cdot(u-tv)\ge0.
\]
Each $t$ gives an inequality, and it says the most when the squared length is as small as possible, so we choose $t$ to make $u-tv$ as short as possible. Geometrically, this happens when the leftover piece $u-tv$ meets the line through $v$ at a right angle. So we look for the $t$ that makes $u-tv$ orthogonal to $v$:
\[
(u-tv)\cdot v=0.
\]
By linearity in the first argument, this says $u\cdot v-t(v\cdot v)=0$. When $v\ne0$, positivity gives $v\cdot v>0$, so we may divide and obtain
\[
t=\frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}.
\]
With this $t$, the vector $tv$ is called the \emph{component} of $u$ along $v$, and $u-tv$ is the \emph{residual}: what remains of $u$ after its component along $v$ is removed. In our example $t=4/2=2$, so the component is $2v=(2,2)$ and the residual is $(1,-1)$, which has dot product $1-1=0$ with $v$, as planned; in the picture it meets the dashed line through $v$ at a right angle.

Since a dot product can be negative, the theorem bounds $|u\cdot v|$; by Section~\ref{sec:magnitude}, this says that $u\cdot v$ lies between $-\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$ and $\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz inequality]\label{thm:cs}
For all $u,v\in\RR^n$, $|u\cdot v|\le\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

\noindent\textit{Plan.} The squared length of $u-tv$ is nonnegative for every real $t$. Expand it, and then substitute the value of $t$ found above.
\begin{proof}
If $v=0$, both sides of the inequality are zero. Assume from now on that $v\ne0$, so that $v\cdot v>0$. For any real $t$, expand the squared length of $u-tv$, using linearity first in the first argument and then in the second:
\begin{align*}
 (u-tv)\cdot(u-tv)
 &=u\cdot(u-tv)-t\bigl(v\cdot(u-tv)\bigr)\\
 &=u\cdot u-t(u\cdot v)-t(v\cdot u)+t^2(v\cdot v)\\
 &=u\cdot u-2t(u\cdot v)+t^2(v\cdot v).
\end{align*}
The last step uses symmetry, $v\cdot u=u\cdot v$. The left side is a squared length, so by positivity the right side is nonnegative for every real $t$.

Now choose $t=(u\cdot v)/(v\cdot v)$, which is defined because $v\cdot v>0$. Then $t(u\cdot v)=(u\cdot v)^2/(v\cdot v)$, and
\[
t^2(v\cdot v)=\frac{(u\cdot v)^2}{(v\cdot v)^2}\,(v\cdot v)=\frac{(u\cdot v)^2}{v\cdot v}.
\]
Substituting these two expressions gives
\begin{align*}
0&\le u\cdot u-2\,\frac{(u\cdot v)^2}{v\cdot v}+\frac{(u\cdot v)^2}{v\cdot v}\\
&=u\cdot u-\frac{(u\cdot v)^2}{v\cdot v}.
\end{align*}
Add the fraction to both sides and multiply by the positive number $v\cdot v$:
\[
(u\cdot v)^2\le(u\cdot u)(v\cdot v)=\norm{u}_2^2\,\norm{v}_2^2.
\]
Finally, $|u\cdot v|$ and $\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$ are nonnegative numbers whose squares are $(u\cdot v)^2$ (because $|a|^2=a^2$ for every real $a$) and $\norm{u}_2^2\norm{v}_2^2$. Nonnegative numbers compare in the same way as their squares, so $|u\cdot v|\le\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{When does equality hold?}
When is $|u\cdot v|$ exactly equal to $\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2$? For $v\ne0$, the proof contains the answer. With the chosen $t$, it showed that $\norm{u-tv}_2^2=u\cdot u-(u\cdot v)^2/(v\cdot v)$. Multiplying by $v\cdot v$ gives
\[
 (u\cdot u)(v\cdot v)-(u\cdot v)^2
 =(v\cdot v)\,\norm{u-tv}_2^2.
\]
The left side is the gap between the two sides of the squared inequality, and since nonnegative numbers are equal exactly when their squares are, equality holds in the theorem exactly when this gap is zero. As $v\cdot v>0$, that happens exactly when $\norm{u-tv}_2=0$, that is, when the residual $u-tv$ is the zero vector, or $u=tv$. So if equality holds, $u$ is a multiple of $v$. Conversely, if $u=sv$ for some number $s$, then $t=\bigl((sv)\cdot v\bigr)/(v\cdot v)=s$, so the residual $u-sv$ is zero, the gap is zero, and equality holds. For example, $u=(2,2)=2v$ with $v=(1,1)$ gives $u\cdot v=4$ and $\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2=\sqrt8\sqrt2=4$. When $v=0$, both sides are zero for every $u$. Altogether, equality holds exactly when $v=0$ or $u$ is a scalar multiple of $v$.

Section~\ref{ch09:sec:taste} recommended asking what happens when an inequality holds with equality. Here the answer also shows that the bound cannot be improved: when $u=v\ne0$, both sides equal $\norm{v}_2^2$, so no constant factor smaller than $1$, such as $0.9$, can be placed in front of the right-hand side.

\subsection*{From the dot-product bound to a distance bound}
In the plane, walking from one point to another by way of a third is never shorter than walking directly. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} needs this fact in $\RR^n$, and Cauchy--Schwarz gives it quickly. For any $u,v\in\RR^n$, expand using linearity in each argument and symmetry:
\begin{align*}
 \norm{u+v}_2^2
 &=(u+v)\cdot(u+v)\\
 &=u\cdot u+u\cdot v+v\cdot u+v\cdot v\\
 &=\norm{u}_2^2+2(u\cdot v)+\norm{v}_2^2\\
 &\le\norm{u}_2^2+2|u\cdot v|+\norm{v}_2^2\\
 &\le\norm{u}_2^2+2\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2+\norm{v}_2^2\\
 &=(\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2)^2.
\end{align*}
The first inequality holds because every real number $a$ satisfies $a\le|a|$ (if $a\ge0$ they are equal, and if $a<0$ then $a<0\le|a|$), and the second is Cauchy--Schwarz. Both $\norm{u+v}_2$ and $\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2$ are nonnegative, and nonnegative numbers compare in the same way as their squares. We obtain the \emph{triangle inequality}
\[
 \norm{u+v}_2\le\norm{u}_2+\norm{v}_2.
\]
To see where the name comes from, take three points $x,y,z\in\RR^n$ and put $u=x-y$ and $v=y-z$. Then $u+v=x-z$, and the inequality becomes
\[
\norm{x-z}_2\le\norm{x-y}_2+\norm{y-z}_2.
\]
In the plane, $\norm{x-z}_2$ is the ordinary distance between $x$ and $z$, so this says that in the triangle with corners $x$, $y$, $z$, the side from $x$ to $z$ is at most as long as the other two sides together. Exercise~\ref{ex:10.5} asks when equality holds.
