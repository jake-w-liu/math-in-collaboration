\section{The geometry of an inner product}\label{ch10:sec:inner}
Adding and scaling vectors tells us nothing about lengths or right angles; for those we need a way to multiply two vectors. For vectors $u,v\in\RR^n$, define their \emph{dot product} by
\[
u\cdot v=\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+\cdots+u_nv_n.
\]
The result is a number, not a vector. For example, $(1,2,3)\cdot(4,-5,6)=4-10+18=12$. The dot product is also called the standard \emph{inner product} on $\RR^n$. In this language, the row rule of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps} says that $(Ax)_i$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with $x$.

The proofs in the next section use only three properties of the dot product.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Symmetry:} $u\cdot v=v\cdot u$, because each product $u_jv_j$ equals $v_ju_j$.
\item \emph{Linearity in each argument:} we call $u$ the \emph{first argument} of $u\cdot v$ and $v$ the \emph{second argument} (an argument here means an input, not a piece of reasoning). If one argument is held fixed, the dot product is linear in the other. For the first argument, the definition gives
\begin{align*}
(au+bw)\cdot v
&=\sum_{j=1}^n(au_j+bw_j)v_j\\
&=a\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j+b\sum_{j=1}^n w_jv_j
=a(u\cdot v)+b(w\cdot v).
\end{align*}
The same expansion gives $u\cdot(av+bw)=a(u\cdot v)+b(u\cdot w)$. Also $0\cdot v=0$ for every $v$, where the $0$ on the left is the zero vector.
\item \emph{Positivity:} $u\cdot u=u_1^2+\cdots+u_n^2\ge0$, and $u\cdot u=0$ only when $u=0$, because a sum of squares is zero only if every square is zero, and $u_j^2=0$ only when $u_j=0$.
\end{itemize}

The \emph{Euclidean length}, or \emph{norm}, of a vector $u$ is
\[
\norm{u}_2=\sqrt{u\cdot u}=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+\cdots+u_n^2}.
\]
The subscript $2$ labels this way of measuring size, built from squares (Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} meets others); it is not a coordinate of $u$. By positivity, the number under the root is nonnegative, and $\norm{u}_2=0$ exactly when $u=0$. In the plane, $\norm{(3,4)}_2=\sqrt{9+16}=5$, which by Pythagoras' theorem is the distance from the origin to the point $(3,4)$.

Multiplying a vector by a scalar $c$ multiplies its length by $|c|$: $\norm{cu}_2=|c|\,\norm{u}_2$. Indeed, linearity in each argument gives $\norm{cu}_2^2=(cu)\cdot(cu)=c^2(u\cdot u)$. So $\norm{cu}_2$ and $|c|\,\norm{u}_2$ are nonnegative numbers with the same square $c^2\norm{u}_2^2$, and a nonnegative number is determined by its square (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation}).

Two vectors $u$ and $v$ are \emph{orthogonal} when $u\cdot v=0$. In the plane, two nonzero vectors are orthogonal exactly when they are perpendicular. To see why, look at the triangle with corners at the origin, $u$, and $v$. By Pythagoras' theorem, its sides from the origin have squared lengths $u_1^2+u_2^2$ and $v_1^2+v_2^2$, and the side from $u$ to $v$ has squared length
\[
(u_1-v_1)^2+(u_2-v_2)^2=(u_1^2+u_2^2)+(v_1^2+v_2^2)-2(u_1v_1+u_2v_2),
\]
by expanding each square. By Pythagoras' theorem and its converse, the angle at the origin is a right angle exactly when the squared length of the opposite side is the sum of the other two, that is, when $2(u_1v_1+u_2v_2)=0$, or $u\cdot v=0$. This needs a genuine triangle, so one case remains: if $u$ and $v$ lie on one line through the origin, then $v=cu$ for some $c\ne0$, and $u\cdot v=c(u\cdot u)\ne0$ by positivity; such arrows are not perpendicular either.

For example, a quarter turn counterclockwise takes $(a,b)$ to $(-b,a)$ (so $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$), so a nonzero $(a,b)$ is perpendicular to $(-b,a)$; and indeed $(a,b)\cdot(-b,a)=-ab+ba=0$. The algebraic definition makes sense in $\RR^n$ for every $n$, even when we can no longer draw a picture. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, since $0\cdot v=0$.
