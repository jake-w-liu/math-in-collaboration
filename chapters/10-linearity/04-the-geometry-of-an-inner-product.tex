\section{The geometry of an inner product}\label{ch10:sec:inner}
Adding and scaling vectors tells us nothing about lengths or right angles. For those we need a way to multiply two vectors. For vectors $u,v\in\RR^n$, define their \emph{dot product} by
\[
u\cdot v=\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+\cdots+u_nv_n.
\]
The result is a number, not a vector. For example, $(1,2,3)\cdot(4,-5,6)=4-10+18=12$. The dot product is also called the standard \emph{inner product} on $\RR^n$. In this language, the row rule of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps} says that $(Ax)_i$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with $x$.

The dot product has three basic properties, and these are the only facts about it that the proofs in the next section use.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Symmetry:} $u\cdot v=v\cdot u$, because each product $u_jv_j$ equals $v_ju_j$.
\item \emph{Linearity in each argument:} we call $u$ the \emph{first argument} of $u\cdot v$ and $v$ the \emph{second argument}. Here an argument means an input, not a piece of reasoning. If one argument is held fixed, the dot product is linear in the other. For instance, for a fixed $v$ the map $u\mapsto u\cdot v$ from $\RR^n$ to $\RR$ is linear. For the first argument, the definition of the dot product gives
\begin{align*}
(au+bw)\cdot v
&=\sum_{j=1}^n(au_j+bw_j)v_j\\
&=a\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j+b\sum_{j=1}^n w_jv_j
=a(u\cdot v)+b(w\cdot v).
\end{align*}
The same expansion gives $u\cdot(av+bw)=a(u\cdot v)+b(u\cdot w)$ for the second argument. Also $0\cdot v=0$ for every $v$, where the $0$ on the left is the zero vector, because each product $0\,v_j$ is zero.
\item \emph{Positivity:} $u\cdot u=u_1^2+\cdots+u_n^2\ge0$, and $u\cdot u=0$ only when $u=0$. Each square is nonnegative, so the sum is nonnegative. A sum of nonnegative numbers is zero only if every term is zero, and $u_j^2=0$ only when $u_j=0$.
\end{itemize}

The \emph{Euclidean length}, or \emph{norm}, of a vector $u$ is
\[
\norm{u}_2=\sqrt{u\cdot u}=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+\cdots+u_n^2}.
\]
The subscript $2$ in $\norm{u}_2$ is a label for this way of measuring size, which is built from squares. It is not the second coordinate of $u$, and it does not mean that $u$ has two coordinates. The label is useful because Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} meets other ways of measuring the size of a vector.

By positivity, the number under the root is nonnegative, so the square root exists. Since $\sqrt t=0$ only when $t=0$, positivity also shows that $\norm{u}_2=0$ exactly when $u=0$. In the plane, $\norm{(3,4)}_2=\sqrt{9+16}=5$. By Pythagoras' theorem this is the distance from the origin to the point $(3,4)$, because that distance is the longest side of a right triangle whose two shorter sides have lengths $3$ and $4$.

Multiplying a vector by a scalar $c$ multiplies its length by $|c|$: $\norm{cu}_2=|c|\,\norm{u}_2$. To see this, use linearity in each argument to get $\norm{cu}_2^2=(cu)\cdot(cu)=c^2(u\cdot u)$. Since $|c|^2=c^2$, the two numbers $\norm{cu}_2$ and $|c|\,\norm{u}_2$ are nonnegative and have the same square, namely $c^2\norm{u}_2^2$. A nonnegative number is determined by its square (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation}), so they are equal.

Two vectors $u$ and $v$ are \emph{orthogonal} when $u\cdot v=0$. In the plane, two nonzero vectors are orthogonal exactly when they are perpendicular in the usual sense. To see why, look at the triangle with corners at the origin, at the point $u$, and at the point $v$. By Pythagoras' theorem, as in the example of $(3,4)$, the sides from the origin have squared lengths $u_1^2+u_2^2$ and $v_1^2+v_2^2$, and the side from $u$ to $v$ has squared length $(u_1-v_1)^2+(u_2-v_2)^2$. Expanding each square by $(p-q)^2=p^2-2pq+q^2$ gives
\[
(u_1-v_1)^2+(u_2-v_2)^2=(u_1^2+u_2^2)+(v_1^2+v_2^2)-2(u_1v_1+u_2v_2).
\]
Pythagoras' theorem and its converse say that the angle at the origin is a right angle exactly when the squared length of the opposite side equals the sum of the squared lengths of the other two sides. By the display, this happens exactly when $2(u_1v_1+u_2v_2)=0$, that is, when $u\cdot v=0$.

This reasoning needs a genuine triangle, so one case remains: $u$ and $v$ might lie on one line through the origin. Then $v=cu$ for some number $c\ne0$, and $u\cdot v=c(u\cdot u)$ is not zero, because $u\cdot u>0$ by positivity. Two arrows along the same line are not perpendicular either, so the claim holds in this case too.

A quarter turn counterclockwise takes a vector $(a,b)$ to $(-b,a)$; for instance, it takes $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$ and $(0,1)$ to $(-1,0)$. So a nonzero vector $(a,b)$ is perpendicular to $(-b,a)$, and indeed
\[
(a,b)\cdot(-b,a)=-ab+ba=0.
\]
The algebraic definition makes sense in $\RR^n$ for every $n$, even when we can no longer draw a picture. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, since $0\cdot v=0$.

For $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(1,-1)$, the two columns of the matrix $A$ of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps}, we get $u\cdot v=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)=0$. The positive product from the first coordinate cancels the negative product from the second. Chapter~\ref{ch:signs} studies sums of products of entries $1$ and $-1$, called correlations, and asks when they are zero. Each such product is $1$ or $-1$, so a correlation is zero exactly when it contains as many products equal to $1$ as products equal to $-1$, as in this example.
