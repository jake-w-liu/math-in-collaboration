\section{The geometry of an inner product}\label{ch10:sec:inner}
For vectors $u,v\in\RR^n$, define their \emph{dot product} by
\[
u\cdot v=\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+\cdots+u_nv_n.
\]
The result is a number, not a vector. For example, $(1,2,3)\cdot(4,-5,6)=4-10+18=12$. The dot product is also called the standard \emph{inner product} on $\RR^n$. In this language, the row rule of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps} says that $(Ax)_i$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with $x$.

The dot product has three basic properties, and these are the only facts about it that the proofs in the next section use.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Symmetry:} $u\cdot v=v\cdot u$, because each product $u_jv_j$ equals $v_ju_j$.
\item \emph{Linearity in each argument:} if one of the two vectors is held fixed, the dot product is linear in the other one. For the first argument,
\begin{align*}
(au+bw)\cdot v
&=\sum_{j=1}^n(au_j+bw_j)v_j\\
&=a\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j+b\sum_{j=1}^n w_jv_j
=a(u\cdot v)+b(w\cdot v).
\end{align*}
The same expansion gives $u\cdot(av+bw)=a(u\cdot v)+b(u\cdot w)$ for the second argument. In particular, taking $a=b=0$ shows that $0\cdot v=0$ for every $v$, where the $0$ on the left is the zero vector.
\item \emph{Positivity:} $u\cdot u=u_1^2+\cdots+u_n^2\ge0$, and $u\cdot u=0$ only when $u=0$. Each square is nonnegative, so the sum is nonnegative. A sum of nonnegative numbers is zero only if every term is zero, and $u_j^2=0$ only when $u_j=0$.
\end{itemize}

The \emph{Euclidean length}, or \emph{norm}, of a vector $u$ is
\[
\norm{u}_2=\sqrt{u\cdot u}=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+\cdots+u_n^2}.
\]
By positivity, the number under the root is nonnegative, so the square root exists. Since $\sqrt t=0$ only when $t=0$, positivity also shows that $\norm{u}_2=0$ exactly when $u=0$. In the plane, $\norm{(3,4)}_2=\sqrt{9+16}=5$. By Pythagoras' theorem this is the distance from the origin to the point $(3,4)$, because that distance is the longest side of a right triangle whose two shorter sides have lengths $3$ and $4$.

Scaling a vector scales its length by the size of the scalar: $\norm{cu}_2=|c|\,\norm{u}_2$. To see this, use linearity in each argument to get $\norm{cu}_2^2=(cu)\cdot(cu)=c^2(u\cdot u)$. Since $|c|^2=c^2$, the two numbers $\norm{cu}_2$ and $|c|\,\norm{u}_2$ are nonnegative and have the same square, namely $c^2\norm{u}_2^2$. A nonnegative number is determined by its square (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation}), so they are equal.

The subscript $2$ in $\norm{u}_2$ is a label for this way of measuring size, which is built from squares. It is not the second coordinate of $u$, and it does not mean that $u$ has two coordinates. The label is useful because Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} meets other ways of measuring the size of a vector.

Two vectors $u$ and $v$ are \emph{orthogonal} when $u\cdot v=0$. In the plane, two nonzero vectors are orthogonal exactly when they are perpendicular in the usual sense. For instance, turning a vector $(a,b)$ through a right angle gives $(-b,a)$, and indeed
\[
(a,b)\cdot(-b,a)=-ab+ba=0.
\]
The algebraic definition makes sense in $\RR^n$ for every $n$, even when we can no longer draw a picture. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, since $0\cdot v=0$.

For $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(1,-1)$, the two columns of the matrix $A$ of Section~\ref{ch10:sec:linearmaps}, we get $u\cdot v=1\cdot1+1\cdot(-1)=0$. The positive product from the first coordinate cancels the negative product from the second. Cancellation of exactly this kind will express the vanishing of correlations between sign sequences in Chapter~\ref{ch:signs}.
