\section{Directed edges and a guaranteed sink}
An \emph{oriented graph} has a set of vertices and a set of directed edges, each written $u\to v$ with $u\ne v$. There are no loops, and no pair of vertices is joined in both directions: $u\to v$ and $v\to u$ never both occur. Some pairs of vertices may have no edge in either direction. The \emph{out-neighbors} of $v$ are the vertices $w$ with $v\to w$, and the \emph{outdegree} of $v$ is their number. A \emph{sink} is a vertex with no outgoing edges.

Walks and paths now follow the arrows. A \emph{directed walk} is a sequence of vertices $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_k$ with $v_{i-1}\to v_i$ for every step, and a \emph{directed path} is a directed walk with no repeated vertex. A \emph{directed cycle} is a directed walk with at least one step that returns to its start, $v_k=v_0$, and has no other repeated vertex. In an oriented graph a directed cycle needs at least three vertices, because loops and opposite pairs of edges are forbidden. An oriented graph is \emph{acyclic} when it has no directed cycle.

Here \emph{nonempty} means there is at least one vertex, and \emph{finite} means there are only finitely many vertices. \emph{Acyclic} does not mean there are no edges; a chain $a\to b\to c$ has edges but no route returning to its start. A directed cycle such as $a\to b\to c\to a$ does return and is forbidden by the hypothesis.

The conclusion asks for one vertex with no outgoing edge. It does not say every vertex has this property. In the chain, $c$ is a sink, whereas $a$ and $b$ are not. In a graph containing a single vertex and no edges, that vertex is a sink. This smallest case also explains why we exclude the empty graph: without vertices, there is no candidate to supply.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sink}
Every nonempty finite acyclic oriented graph has a sink.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

\noindent\textit{Plan.} Choose a directed path that cannot be made longer. Its last vertex cannot point to any vertex without either extending the path or creating a cycle.
\begin{proof}
A directed path never repeats a vertex, so it lists at most $|V|$ vertices. There are only finitely many lists of at most $|V|$ distinct vertices, so there are only finitely many directed paths. At least one exists, because the graph is nonempty and a single vertex counts as a path of length zero. We may therefore choose a directed path of greatest length. Write it $v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_k$. Suppose its final vertex $v_k$ had an outgoing edge to a vertex $w$. If $w$ were not on the path, appending it would produce a longer directed path. If $w=v_j$ were on the path, then $j<k$ because loops are forbidden, and $v_j\to\cdots\to v_k\to v_j$ would be a directed cycle. Both possibilities contradict our assumptions. Hence $v_k$ has no outgoing edge and is a sink.
\end{proof}
