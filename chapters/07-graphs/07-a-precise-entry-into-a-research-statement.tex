\section{A precise entry into a research statement}
Let $N_1^+(v)$ be the set of out-neighbors of $v$, which we call its \emph{first out-neighborhood}. Its \emph{second out-neighborhood} is
\[
N_2^+(v)=\{w:\ w\ne v,\ w\notin N_1^+(v),\ \text{and some }u\text{ satisfies }v\to u\to w\}.
\]
In words, $w$ belongs to $N_2^+(v)$ when it can be reached from $v$ in two steps but not in fewer: it is not $v$ itself and not a first neighbor. These are the vertices at directed distance \emph{exactly} two. They are counted as vertices, not as routes: if several intermediate vertices $u$ lead to the same $w$, then $w$ is still counted once.

Two small examples show how the exclusions work. In the graph with edges $a\to b$, $b\to c$, and $a\to c$, the vertex $c$ can be reached from $a$ by the two-step route $a\to b\to c$, but it is not in $N_2^+(a)$, because the edge $a\to c$ already makes it a first neighbor. Here $|N_1^+(a)|=2$ and $|N_2^+(a)|=0$. In the directed cycle $a\to b\to c\to a$, each vertex has exactly one first neighbor and exactly one second neighbor: for instance $N_1^+(a)=\{b\}$ and $N_2^+(a)=\{c\}$.

In 1990 Paul Seymour proposed the following statement, now known as \emph{Seymour's second-neighborhood conjecture}:
\begin{quote}
Every nonempty finite oriented graph has a vertex $v$ with $|N_2^+(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$.
\end{quote}
In words: some vertex reaches at least as many new vertices in its second step as in its first. A sink always satisfies the inequality, since both of its neighborhoods are empty, so the interesting graphs are those in which every vertex has an outgoing edge. For decades the statement remained open in general, although special cases were settled; the case of tournaments, for example, was proved in 1996.

In September 2026 a preprint claiming a proof of the full conjecture was released together with a formal proof in the Lean proof assistant, in OpenAI's public mathematics repository~\cite{oai173}. The repository's description of the formalized statement uses exactly the conventions above: the second neighborhood consists of vertices at directed distance exactly two, $v$ and its first neighbors are excluded, and sinks are allowed. This book does not reproduce or check that proof. Chapter~16 shows how its definitions can be read in Lean, and Chapter~17 describes how to begin reading such a proof responsibly.

What we \emph{can} prove completely already is a special case. If an oriented graph is nonempty, finite, and acyclic, Theorem~\ref{thm:sink} gives a sink $v$, and then $|N_2^+(v)|=0\ge0=|N_1^+(v)|$. This is a complete proof for the acyclic class of graphs, and no proof at all for graphs with directed cycles. Keeping track of exactly which class a proof covers is the same discipline as keeping track of the scope of a quantifier in Chapter~2.
