\section{Double counting begins with one precise collection}\label{ch07:sec:double}
Add up the degrees in the example graph $G$: $2+2+3+1=8$. The graph has four edges, and $8$ is exactly twice $4$. This is no accident. It is worth seeing why in this small case before we state the general fact.

Consider every pair $(v,e)$ in which $v$ is a vertex, $e$ is an edge, and $v$ is an endpoint of $e$. Because an edge is the set of its two endpoints, ``$v$ is an endpoint of $e$'' simply means $v\in e$. The table below has one row for each vertex of $G$ and one column for each edge. An entry $1$ marks a pair $(v,e)$ with $v\in e$, and an entry $0$ means that $v$ is not an endpoint of $e$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
 & $ab$ & $bc$ & $ca$ & $cd$ & row total\\
\midrule
$a$ & $1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $0$ & $2$\\
$b$ & $1$ & $1$ & $0$ & $0$ & $2$\\
$c$ & $0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $3$\\
$d$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $1$ & $1$\\
\midrule
column total & $2$ & $2$ & $2$ & $2$ & $8$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Now count the entries equal to $1$ in two ways. Row by row, the row of a vertex has a $1$ for each edge at that vertex, so its total is the degree of the vertex, and the row totals add up to $2+2+3+1=8$. Column by column, every edge has exactly two endpoints, so every column total is $2$, and the four columns give $2+2+2+2=8$. Both totals count the same eight entries, so they must agree. Nothing was counted differently: the same entries were grouped in two different ways.

This method, counting one collection of objects in two ways and setting the two answers equal, is called \emph{double counting}. Its essential step is to describe the collection precisely. Then we can check that each grouping counts every member exactly once, with nothing left out and nothing counted twice.

To state the general result we need one piece of notation. Chapter~\ref{ch:start} wrote sums such as $\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_j$, whose index runs through consecutive integers. The notation $\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)$ means: add the number $\deg(v)$ once for each vertex $v$ in $V$. For the example graph,
\[
\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c)+\deg(d)=2+2+3+1=8.
\]
The order in which the vertices are taken does not matter, because a finite sum may be reordered and regrouped freely (Section~\ref{ch01:sec:letters}). In the same way, $\sum_{e\in E}2$ adds the number $2$ once for each edge, so it equals $2|E|$. Recall from Section~\ref{ch03:sec:sets} that $|E|$ is the number of members of $E$, that is, the number of edges.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}[Handshake theorem]\label{thm:handshake}
In every graph with vertex set $V$ and edge set $E$,
\[
\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=2|E|.
\]
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

The name comes from a party. Make each guest a vertex, and join two guests by an edge when they shook hands. Then $\deg(v)$ is the number of hands that guest $v$ shook, and the theorem says that the total of these numbers is twice the number of handshakes: every handshake is counted once by each of the two people involved.

\begin{proof}
Let $P$ be the set of all pairs $(v,e)$ with $v\in V$, $e\in E$, and $v\in e$. We count $P$ in two ways.

First group the pairs by their vertex. For a fixed vertex $v$, the pairs in $P$ with first entry $v$ are the pairs $(v,e)$ in which $e$ is an edge with endpoint $v$. As Section~\ref{ch07:sec:graph} showed, there are exactly $\deg(v)$ such edges. Every pair in $P$ has exactly one first entry, so it lies in exactly one of these groups. Adding the sizes of the groups therefore counts each pair exactly once, and $|P|=\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)$.

Next group the pairs by their edge. For a fixed edge $e=\{u,w\}$, the pairs in $P$ with second entry $e$ are $(u,e)$ and $(w,e)$. These are two different pairs, because $u\ne w$. Again every pair lies in exactly one group, so $|P|=\sum_{e\in E}2=2|E|$.

Both expressions are equal to $|P|$, so they are equal to each other.
\end{proof}

Look back at where the proof used the word ``simple.'' The group of an edge has exactly two members because the two endpoints of an edge are different. The group of a vertex $v$ has exactly $\deg(v)$ members because of the second reading of the degree, which needed both halves of ``simple.''

The handshake theorem has a consequence that is not at all obvious from a drawing. In the example graph, the vertices of odd degree are $c$, of degree $3$, and $d$, of degree $1$: there are two of them. The number two here is no coincidence either.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch07:prop:odd}
In every graph, the number of vertices of odd degree is even.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Let $k$ be the number of vertices of odd degree. By Lemma~\ref{ch04:lem:even-or-odd}, every degree is either even or odd, and not both. So we can split the degree sum into two parts: the sum $S_{\mathrm{even}}$ of the even degrees and the sum $S_{\mathrm{odd}}$ of the odd degrees. Then $S_{\mathrm{even}}+S_{\mathrm{odd}}=2|E|$ by the handshake theorem.
\begin{itemize}
\item $S_{\mathrm{even}}$ is even, because a sum of even numbers is even: a factor $2$ can be taken out of every term. (If no degree is even, $S_{\mathrm{even}}$ is the empty sum $0$, which is even too.)
\item Therefore $S_{\mathrm{odd}}=2|E|-S_{\mathrm{even}}$ is the difference of two even numbers, and so it is even: $2x-2y=2(x-y)$.
\end{itemize}
Now write the $k$ odd degrees, using Definition~\ref{def:parity}, as $2\ell_1+1,\ldots,2\ell_k+1$ with integers $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_k$. Adding them gives
\[
S_{\mathrm{odd}}=(2\ell_1+1)+\cdots+(2\ell_k+1)=2(\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_k)+k,
\]
because each of the $k$ terms contributes one $1$. Hence $k=S_{\mathrm{odd}}-2(\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_k)$ is again the difference of two even numbers, so $k$ is even. (If $k=0$, the conclusion holds at once, since $0$ is even.)
\end{proof}

A drawing shows one graph at a time; the proposition speaks about every graph at once. It says, for instance, that no graph has exactly one vertex of odd degree. It also settles a puzzle: can seven people at a party each shake hands with exactly three of the others? Make each person a vertex and each handshake an edge. All seven vertices would have the odd degree $3$, so the number of vertices of odd degree would be $7$, which is odd. The proposition forbids this, so no arrangement of handshakes works. The handshake theorem gives the same answer even more directly: the degree sum would be $7\cdot3=21$, an odd number, but it must equal the even number $2|E|$.
