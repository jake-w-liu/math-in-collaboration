\section{Second out-neighborhoods and a research problem}\label{ch07:sec:seymour}
This section states a problem about directed graphs that resisted every attempt at a proof for more than thirty years. First we measure distance along the arrows.

The deletion argument of Lemma~\ref{ch07:lem:shortest-walk} works for directed walks too, since after the cut, the arrow that left the second visit to a vertex leaves the first visit, which is the same vertex. So whenever there is a directed walk from $v$ to $w$, a shortest one exists, and it is a directed path; its length is the \emph{directed distance} from $v$ to $w$. Unlike distance in a graph, it depends on the order of the two vertices: in the chain $a\to b\to c$, the directed distance from $a$ to $c$ is $2$, but no directed walk goes from $c$ to $a$.

Now let $v$ be a vertex of a directed graph with vertex set $V$. Write $N_1^+(v)$ for the set of out-neighbors of $v$, the vertices at directed distance $1$ from $v$, and call it the \emph{first out-neighborhood} of $v$. The \emph{second out-neighborhood} of $v$ is the set
\[
N_2^+(v)=\{w\in V:\ w\ne v,\ w\notin N_1^+(v),\ \text{and }v\to u\to w\text{ for some vertex }u\}.
\]
(The $+$ means that we follow the arrows outward from $v$; the subscript counts the steps.) Thus $N_2^+(v)$ is the set of vertices at directed distance exactly two from $v$: a two-step walk $v\to u\to w$ reaches $w$, but $w$ is neither $v$ itself, at distance $0$, nor a first out-neighbor, at distance $1$. It is a set of vertices, not of routes: if several vertices $u$ lead to the same $w$, then $w$ is still a single member of $N_2^+(v)$ and is counted once in $|N_2^+(v)|$.

In an oriented graph the condition $w\ne v$ holds automatically, because a route $v\to u\to v$ would need edges in both directions between $v$ and $u$. In other directed graphs it keeps $v$ from being its own second out-neighbor.

For example, in the oriented graph with edges $a\to b$, $b\to c$, $a\to c$, and $c\to d$, the two-step routes from $a$ are $a\to b\to c$ and $a\to c\to d$. The vertex $c$ ends the first route, but the edge $a\to c$ makes it a first out-neighbor, so it is not in $N_2^+(a)$. Thus
\[
N_1^+(a)=\{b,c\},\qquad N_2^+(a)=\{d\}.
\]

In 1990 Paul Seymour proposed the following statement, now known as \emph{Seymour's second-neighborhood conjecture}:
\begin{quote}
Every nonempty finite oriented graph has a vertex $v$ with $|N_2^+(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$.
\end{quote}
In words: some vertex reaches at least as many new vertices in its second step as in its first. In the four-vertex example above, $a$ fails the inequality, since $|N_2^+(a)|=1<2=|N_1^+(a)|$. The vertex $b$ satisfies it, since $N_1^+(b)=\{c\}$ and $N_2^+(b)=\{d\}$. So does $d$, for a simpler reason: it is a sink, and a sink has no out-neighbors and no two-step routes, so both of its out-neighborhoods are empty and $0\ge0$.

The word ``oriented'' cannot be dropped. In the directed graph with two vertices $u$ and $v$ and both edges $u\to v$ and $v\to u$, the only two-step route from $u$ returns to $u$, so $N_1^+(u)=\{v\}$ and $N_2^+(u)=\varnothing$. The same holds for $v$, so neither vertex satisfies the inequality.

For decades only special cases were settled; for example, David Fisher proved the case of tournaments in 1996. In September 2026, a \emph{preprint}, that is, a research paper made public before a journal has reviewed it, claimed a proof of the full conjecture. It was released in OpenAI's public mathematics repository together with a formal proof in the Lean proof assistant~\cite{oai173}. Its formalized statement uses the conventions of this section: second out-neighbors are the vertices at directed distance exactly two, and graphs with sinks are allowed. The proof draws on ideas well beyond this course. Section~\ref{ch16:sec:second} shows how the definition of $N_2^+(v)$ is written in Lean, and Section~\ref{ch17:sec:research} describes how to begin reading a formalized proof of this kind.

If a news report or an AI assistant tells you that ``Seymour's conjecture has been proved,'' first check \emph{which statement} was proved. Counting two-step routes instead of vertices, or letting first out-neighbors count as second out-neighbors, can only make the second count larger and the inequality easier to satisfy, so a proof of such a variant would prove a weaker statement.

We can already prove one special case completely.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch07:prop:acyclic-seymour}
Every nonempty finite acyclic oriented graph has a vertex $v$ with $|N_2^+(v)|\ge|N_1^+(v)|$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:sink}, the graph has a sink $v$. As we saw above, both out-neighborhoods of a sink are empty. Hence $|N_2^+(v)|=0\ge0=|N_1^+(v)|$.
\end{proof}

This proof says nothing about graphs with directed cycles, and that is where all the difficulty lies: a counterexample to the conjecture would have no sink, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:sink} it would contain a directed cycle. Keeping track of which graphs a proof covers is part of the statement contract of Habit~2 (Section~\ref{sec:magnitude}): removing the hypothesis ``acyclic'' from Proposition~\ref{ch07:prop:acyclic-seymour} turns it into the conjecture itself.
