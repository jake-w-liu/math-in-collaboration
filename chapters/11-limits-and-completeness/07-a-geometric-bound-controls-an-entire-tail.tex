\section{A geometric bound controls an entire tail}\label{ch11:sec:geometric}
The harmonic sums of Section~\ref{ch11:sec:cauchy} failed to be Cauchy because their steps, though small, added up to a lot. Steps that shrink by a fixed factor at every stage cannot add up to much, and the tool that shows this is the finite geometric sum.

\subsection*{The finite geometric sum}
Let $q$ be a real number with $q\ne1$, and let $r$ be a positive integer. Then
\[
1+q+q^2+\cdots+q^{r-1}=\frac{1-q^r}{1-q}.
\]
To see why, call the sum $S$. Multiplying by $q$ raises every power by one:
\begin{align*}
 S&=1+q+q^2+\cdots+q^{r-1},\\
 qS&=\phantom{1+{}}q+q^2+\cdots+q^{r-1}+q^r.
\end{align*}
When the second line is subtracted from the first, the terms $q$ through $q^{r-1}$ cancel, leaving $(1-q)S=1-q^r$. Since $1-q\ne0$, we may divide by it. For example, $q=1/2$ and $r=4$ give $1+\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18=\tfrac{15}8$, and the formula gives $\bigl(1-\tfrac1{16}\bigr)\big/\tfrac12=\tfrac{15}8$.

From now on let $0<q<1$. We need sums that start at a later power. For integers $m>n\ge0$, factor out $q^n$ and apply the formula with $r=m-n$:
\[
\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}q^j=q^n\bigl(1+q+\cdots+q^{m-n-1}\bigr)=q^n\,\frac{1-q^{m-n}}{1-q}\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}.
\]
The final inequality holds because $q^{m-n}>0$, so $1-q^{m-n}<1$, and $q^n/(1-q)>0$. The bound does not depend on $m$: however many terms we add, a sum of consecutive powers starting at $q^n$ never exceeds $q^n/(1-q)$. We call this the \emph{geometric tail bound}.

\subsection*{Powers of a number between zero and one tend to zero}
The bound $q^n/(1-q)$ is useful only if $q^n\to0$. Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence} handled $q=1/2$ by proving $2^n\ge n+1$; for general $0<q<1$, we imitate that proof with $1/q$ in place of $2$. Put $c=1/q-1$. Since $1/q>1$, we have $c>0$, and $1/q=1+c$. We prove by induction that
\[
(1+c)^n\ge1+nc\qquad\text{for every }n\in\NN.
\]
This is known as \emph{Bernoulli's inequality}. At $n=0$ both sides equal $1$. If $(1+c)^n\ge1+nc$ for some $n$, multiply both sides by the positive number $1+c$:
\[
 (1+c)^{n+1}\ge(1+nc)(1+c)
 =1+c+nc+nc^2=1+(n+1)c+nc^2\ge1+(n+1)c,
\]
because $nc^2\ge0$. This is the inequality for $n+1$. Since $q^n=1/(1+c)^n$, taking reciprocals of positive numbers gives
\[
 0<q^n\le\frac1{1+nc}.
\]
Now let $\eps>0$ be given, and use the Archimedean property to choose a positive integer $N>1/(c\eps)$. If $n\ge N$, then $1+nc>nc\ge Nc>1/\eps$, where the last step multiplies $N>1/(c\eps)$ by $c>0$. Taking reciprocals once more gives $q^n\le1/(1+nc)<\eps$. This proves $q^n\to0$. The closer $q$ is to $1$, the smaller $c$ is, and the larger the threshold that this recipe gives.

\subsection*{Geometrically shrinking steps give a Cauchy sequence}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch11:prop:geometric-cauchy}
Let $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$ be a sequence in a metric space $(X,d)$, and suppose there are numbers $D\ge0$ and $0<q<1$ such that
\[
d(x_{j+1},x_j)\le Dq^j\qquad\text{for every }j\in\NN.
\]
Then $d(x_m,x_n)\le Dq^n/(1-q)$ whenever $m>n$, and the sequence is a Cauchy sequence.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

For example, the sequence $x_n=1-2^{-n}$ of Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence} has steps $x_{j+1}-x_j=2^{-j}-2^{-(j+1)}=\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)^j$; these are positive, so they equal the distances $d(x_{j+1},x_j)$, and the hypothesis holds with $D=q=1/2$. The proposition promises $|x_m-x_n|\le\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)^n\big/\tfrac12=2^{-n}$ for all $m>n$; directly, $|x_m-x_n|=2^{-n}-2^{-m}$, which is indeed less than $2^{-n}$.

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: from single steps to many steps.} Fix $n$. We show by induction on $m$ that the distance from $x_n$ to $x_m$ is at most the total length of the single steps between them: for every $m>n$,
\[
d(x_m,x_n)\le\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j+1},x_j).
\]
When $m=n+1$, the sum has the single term $d(x_{n+1},x_n)$, and the claim is an equality. Suppose the claim holds for some $m>n$. The triangle inequality with $x_m$ as the stopping point gives
\[
d(x_{m+1},x_n)\le d(x_{m+1},x_m)+d(x_m,x_n)\le d(x_{m+1},x_m)+\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j+1},x_j)=\sum_{j=n}^{m}d(x_{j+1},x_j),
\]
which is the claim for $m+1$.

\emph{Step 2: a bound for the whole tail.} For $m>n$, Step~1, the assumed bounds on the steps and the geometric tail bound give
\[
d(x_m,x_n)\le\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j+1},x_j)\le\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}Dq^j=D\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}q^j\le\frac{Dq^n}{1-q}.
\]
The last step multiplies the geometric tail bound by $D$, which keeps the inequality in the same direction because $D\ge0$.

\emph{Step 3: the Cauchy condition.} Let $\eps>0$. If $D=0$, Step~2 gives $d(x_m,x_n)\le0$, hence $d(x_m,x_n)=0$, for all $m>n$; so all the terms are equal, and the threshold $N=0$ works. Now let $D>0$. Since $q^n\to0$ and $\eps(1-q)/D>0$, there is a threshold $N$ such that $q^n<\eps(1-q)/D$, that is, $Dq^n/(1-q)<\eps$, for every $n\ge N$. Take any two indices $m,n\ge N$. If $m=n$, their distance is $0<\eps$. Otherwise, by symmetry of $d$, we may name them so that $m>n$, and Step~2 gives $d(x_m,x_n)\le Dq^n/(1-q)<\eps$. So the sequence is Cauchy.
\end{proof}

The bound $Dq^n/(1-q)$ in Step~2 involves $n$ but not $m$, so a single choice of $n$ controls the whole tail from $x_n$ onward. If the space is complete, as the real line and every closed interval are, the sequence also has a limit in the space. This combination of a geometric bound on the steps with completeness is the heart of the contraction theorem in Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction}.
