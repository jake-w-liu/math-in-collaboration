\section{Completeness excludes missing limits}\label{ch11:sec:complete}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Complete metric space]\label{ch11:def:complete}
A metric space $(X,d)$ is \emph{complete} if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point of $X$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Informally, a complete space has no holes: whenever the terms of a sequence crowd closer and closer together, there is a point of the space for them to crowd around.

Completeness is a property of a whole space, not of a single sequence; it is a promise about every Cauchy sequence in the space at once. To use it, we prove that one particular sequence is Cauchy, and the definition then hands us a limit that belongs to the space. To show that a space is \emph{not} complete, it is enough to find one Cauchy sequence in it that does not converge to any point of the space.

\subsection*{The real line}
The real line, with distance $|x-y|$, is complete. We accept this as a starting fact about the real numbers, just as we accepted the existence of square roots in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation} and the Archimedean property in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers}. It is the last of the four starting facts listed in \emph{To the reader} at the front of the book. A proof would require constructing the real numbers from simpler objects, which we do not do. Every other completeness result we use, such as the completeness of the interval $[0,1]$ proved below, is derived from this one fact, so whenever a later argument says that a limit exists, you can trace that claim back to this assumption.

The rational numbers show what the assumption rules out. Cutting off the decimal expansion of $\sqrt2=1.41421\ldots$ after more and more digits gives the rational numbers $1$, $1.4$, $1.41$, $1.414$, $1.4142$, and so on. These numbers crowd together, and the point they crowd around is $\sqrt2$, which is not a fraction of integers (Theorem~\ref{thm:sqrt2}). So the set $\QQ$ of rational numbers, with distance $|x-y|$, has a hole at $\sqrt2$, and it is not complete. The completeness of $\RR$ says that the number line has no such holes.

\subsection*{An interval with a missing endpoint}
The interval $(0,1)$, with distance $|x-y|$, is not complete. Consider the sequence $x_n=2^{-(n+1)}$, that is, $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\ldots$. Every term lies in $(0,1)$.

First, the sequence is Cauchy. On the real line it converges to $0$. Indeed, Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence} showed that $2^k\ge k+1$ for every $k\in\NN$, so $|x_n-0|=2^{-(n+1)}\le1/(n+2)<1/(n+1)$, and the argument given there for $1/(n+1)$ shows that these distances become smaller than any $\eps>0$ from some threshold on. By Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:convergent-cauchy}, the sequence is Cauchy on the real line. The Cauchy condition involves only distances between terms, and those distances are the same whether we regard the terms as points of $(0,1)$ or of $\RR$. So the sequence is Cauchy in $(0,1)$ as well.

Second, the sequence has no limit in $(0,1)$. Suppose it converged to some point $L$ of $(0,1)$. Distances in $(0,1)$ are distances in $\RR$, so it would also converge to $L$ on the real line. It already converges to $0$ there, so Proposition~\ref{thm:limitunique} gives $L=0$. But $0$ is not in $(0,1)$, a contradiction. The only possible destination of the sequence has been removed from the space.

\subsection*{Closed subsets of a complete space}
Completeness passes from a space to many of its subsets. Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space, let $C\subseteq X$, and give $C$ the same distance $d$, as in Section~\ref{ch11:sec:metric}.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Closed subset]\label{ch11:def:closed}
A subset $C$ of a metric space $(X,d)$ is \emph{closed} in $X$ if, whenever a sequence of points of $C$ converges to a point of $X$, that limit lies in $C$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

In words, you cannot leave $C$ by taking limits.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch11:prop:closed-complete}
If $(X,d)$ is complete and $C$ is closed in $X$, then $C$, with the same distance $d$, is complete.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Take a Cauchy sequence of points of $C$. The Cauchy condition involves only distances between terms, and those distances are the same whether we regard the terms as points of $C$ or of $X$. So the sequence is also Cauchy in $X$. Since $X$ is complete, the sequence converges to some point of $X$. Since $C$ is closed, that point lies in $C$, and the distances from the terms to it are again the same in $C$ as in $X$. So the sequence converges to a point of $C$. Hence every Cauchy sequence in $C$ converges in $C$.
\end{proof}

The interval $(0,1]$ shows what goes wrong without closedness. The points $x_n=1/(n+1)$ all lie in $(0,1]$, and on the real line they converge to $0$, which is not in $(0,1]$. So $(0,1]$ is not closed in $\RR$. It is not complete either. The sequence converges in $\RR$, so it is Cauchy there by Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:convergent-cauchy}, and therefore Cauchy in $(0,1]$, where the distances are the same. By Proposition~\ref{thm:limitunique}, its only possible limit is $0$, which is missing from $(0,1]$.

The closed interval $[0,1]$, on the other hand, is closed in $\RR$, and therefore complete. To prove that it is closed, suppose a sequence of points $x_n\in[0,1]$ converges on the real line to a number $L$. We show that $L<0$ and $L>1$ are both impossible. We use the fact that, for $\eps>0$, the inequality $|x_n-L|<\eps$ means $L-\eps<x_n<L+\eps$. This is the rule of Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} applied to the number $x_n-L$, with strict inequalities in place of non-strict ones; the proof is the same.

If $L<0$, the number $\eps=-L/2$ is a positive tolerance. Every sufficiently late term satisfies $|x_n-L|<-L/2$, so
\[
x_n<L+\left(-\frac L2\right)=\frac L2<0.
\]
This contradicts $x_n\in[0,1]$. If $L>1$, use instead the positive tolerance $\eps=(L-1)/2$. Every sufficiently late term then satisfies
\[
 x_n>L-\frac{L-1}2=\frac{L+1}2>1,
\]
where the last step holds because $L>1$ gives $L+1>2$. This again contradicts $x_n\in[0,1]$. Both cases are impossible, so $0\le L\le1$, that is, $L\in[0,1]$. Hence $[0,1]$ is closed in the complete real line, and by Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:closed-complete} it is complete.

The same argument, with $a$ and $b$ in place of $0$ and $1$ and the tolerances $(a-L)/2$ and $(L-b)/2$, shows that every closed interval $[a,b]$ with $a\le b$ is complete.

\pauseq{Is the set $[0,\infty)$ of nonnegative real numbers, with distance $|x-y|$, complete?}{Yes. Suppose a sequence of nonnegative numbers converges on the real line to $L$. If $L<0$, the first case of the argument for $[0,1]$ applies word for word and produces a negative term, which is impossible. So $L\ge0$, the set $[0,\infty)$ is closed in $\RR$, and Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:closed-complete} shows that it is complete. A complete space need not have a right-hand end; what matters is that no limit point is missing.}
