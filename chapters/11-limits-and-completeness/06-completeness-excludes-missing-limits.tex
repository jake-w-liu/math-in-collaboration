\section{Completeness excludes missing limits}\label{ch11:sec:complete}
Section~\ref{ch11:sec:cauchy} showed that every convergent sequence is Cauchy and noted that the converse can fail. The spaces in which it never fails have a name.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Complete metric space]\label{ch11:def:complete}
A metric space $(X,d)$ is \emph{complete} if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point of $X$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Informally, a complete space has no holes: whenever the terms of a sequence eventually all lie as close to one another as we like, there is a point of the space that they approach. Notice that this asks for more than consecutive terms getting closer together; the harmonic sums of Section~\ref{ch11:sec:cauchy} take smaller and smaller steps and still fail to be Cauchy.

Completeness is a property of a whole space, not of a single sequence; it is a promise about every Cauchy sequence in the space at once. To use it, we prove that one particular sequence is Cauchy, and the definition then hands us a limit that belongs to the space. To show that a space is \emph{not} complete, it is enough to find one Cauchy sequence in it that does not converge to any point of the space.

\subsection*{An interval with a missing endpoint}
The interval $(0,1)$, with distance $|x-y|$, is not complete. Consider the sequence $x_n=2^{-(n+1)}$, that is, $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\ldots$. Every term lies in $(0,1)$, because $0<2^{-(n+1)}\le\tfrac12$.

First, the sequence is Cauchy. On the real line it converges to $0$. Indeed, Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence} showed that $2^k\ge k+1$ for every $k\in\NN$; with $k=n+1$ this gives $2^{n+1}\ge n+2$. Taking reciprocals, $|x_n-0|=2^{-(n+1)}\le1/(n+2)<1/(n+1)$, and the argument given there for $1/(n+1)$ shows that these distances become smaller than any $\eps>0$ from some threshold on. By Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:convergent-cauchy}, the sequence is Cauchy on the real line. The Cauchy condition involves only distances between terms, and those distances are the same whether we regard the terms as points of $(0,1)$ or of $\RR$. So the sequence is Cauchy in $(0,1)$ as well.

Second, the sequence has no limit in $(0,1)$. Suppose it converged to some point $L$ of $(0,1)$. Distances in $(0,1)$ are distances in $\RR$, so it would also converge to $L$ on the real line. It already converges to $0$ there, so Proposition~\ref{thm:limitunique} gives $L=0$. But $0$ is not in $(0,1)$, a contradiction. The only possible destination of the sequence has been removed from the space.

\subsection*{The real line}
The real line, with distance $|x-y|$, is complete. We accept this as a starting fact about the real numbers, just as we accepted the existence of square roots in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation} and the Archimedean property in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers}. It is the last of the four starting facts listed in \emph{To the reader} at the front of the book. A proof would require constructing the real numbers from simpler objects, which we do not do. The closed intervals that we prove complete later in this section owe their completeness to this one fact, and so, in the end, does every later argument that needs a limit to exist without being able to name it in advance.

The rational numbers show what the assumption rules out. Cutting off the decimal expansion of $\sqrt2=1.41421\ldots$ after more and more digits gives the rational numbers $t_0=1$, $t_1=1.4$, $t_2=1.41$, $t_3=1.414$, $t_4=1.4142$, and so on. These numbers crowd together, and the point they crowd around is $\sqrt2$, which is not a fraction of integers (Theorem~\ref{thm:sqrt2}). So the set $\QQ$ of rational numbers, with distance $|x-y|$, has a hole at $\sqrt2$: it is not complete. The completeness of $\RR$ says that the number line has no such holes.

To make this precise, we follow the two steps used above for $(0,1)$. Both need the fact that $t_k\to\sqrt2$ on the real line, so we check that first. The difference $\sqrt2-t_k$ consists of the digits of $\sqrt2$ after the $k$th decimal place; for instance, $\sqrt2-t_2=1.41421\ldots-1.41=0.00421\ldots$, which is less than $0.01$. In general the first $k$ digits of $\sqrt2-t_k$ after the decimal point are zeros, so $0\le\sqrt2-t_k\le10^{-k}$. Moreover $10^{-k}\le2^{-k}\le1/(k+1)$, because $10^k\ge2^k$ and, by Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence}, $2^k\ge k+1$. So the argument given there for $1/(k+1)$ shows that $t_k\to\sqrt2$ on the real line.

With this in hand, the two steps go through. First, by Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:convergent-cauchy}, the sequence $t_0,t_1,t_2,\ldots$ is Cauchy on the real line. The distances $|t_m-t_n|$ are the same whether we regard the $t_k$ as rational or as real numbers, so it is also a Cauchy sequence in $\QQ$. Second, suppose it converged in $\QQ$ to some rational number $L$. Distances in $\QQ$ are distances in $\RR$, so it would converge to $L$ on the real line too, and Proposition~\ref{thm:limitunique} would give $L=\sqrt2$. That is impossible, because $\sqrt2$ is not rational. So this Cauchy sequence has no limit in $\QQ$.

\subsection*{Closed subsets of a complete space}
Completeness passes from a space to many of its subsets. Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space, let $C\subseteq X$, and give $C$ the same distance $d$, as in Section~\ref{ch11:sec:metric}.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Closed subset]\label{ch11:def:closed}
A subset $C$ of a metric space $(X,d)$ is \emph{closed} in $X$ if, whenever a sequence of points of $C$ converges to a point of $X$, that limit lies in $C$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

In words, you cannot leave $C$ by taking limits.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{ch11:prop:closed-complete}
If $(X,d)$ is complete and $C$ is closed in $X$, then $C$, with the same distance $d$, is complete.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Take a Cauchy sequence of points of $C$. The Cauchy condition involves only distances between terms, and those distances are the same whether we regard the terms as points of $C$ or of $X$. So the sequence is also Cauchy in $X$. Since $X$ is complete, the sequence converges to some point of $X$. Since $C$ is closed, that point lies in $C$, and the distances from the terms to it are again the same in $C$ as in $X$. So the sequence converges to a point of $C$. Hence every Cauchy sequence in $C$ converges in $C$.
\end{proof}

The interval $(0,1]$ shows what goes wrong without closedness. The points $x_n=1/(n+1)$ all lie in $(0,1]$, and on the real line they converge to $0$, which is not in $(0,1]$. So $(0,1]$ is not closed in $\RR$. It is not complete either: exactly as for $(0,1)$, the sequence is Cauchy in $(0,1]$, and its only possible limit, $0$, is missing from $(0,1]$.

The closed interval $[0,1]$, on the other hand, is closed in $\RR$, and therefore complete. To prove that it is closed, suppose a sequence of points $x_n\in[0,1]$ converges on the real line to a number $L$. We show that $L<0$ and $L>1$ are both impossible. We use a rule from Section~\ref{sec:magnitude}: for every positive number $\eps$, the inequality $|x-c|<\eps$ holds exactly when $c-\eps<x<c+\eps$. With $x_n$ in place of $x$ and $L$ in place of $c$, it says that $|x_n-L|<\eps$ means $L-\eps<x_n<L+\eps$.

If $L<0$, then $L$ lies a distance $-L$ to the left of $0$, the nearest point of the interval. We take half of this distance as the tolerance: $\eps=-L/2$, which is positive. The idea is that every number less than $\eps$ away from $L$ is still negative, while the late terms of the sequence must lie that close to $L$. In detail, convergence supplies a threshold $N$ such that $|x_n-L|<-L/2$ for every $n\ge N$. In particular the term $x_N$ satisfies
\[
x_N<L+\left(-\frac L2\right)=\frac L2<0.
\]
This contradicts $x_N\in[0,1]$. If $L>1$, then $L$ lies a distance $L-1$ to the right of $1$, and half of this distance gives the positive tolerance $\eps=(L-1)/2$. Now convergence supplies a threshold $N$ such that $|x_n-L|<(L-1)/2$ for every $n\ge N$, and the term $x_N$ satisfies
\[
 x_N>L-\frac{L-1}2=\frac{L+1}2>1,
\]
where the last step holds because $L>1$ gives $L+1>2$. This again contradicts $x_N\in[0,1]$. Both cases are impossible, so $0\le L\le1$, that is, $L\in[0,1]$. Hence $[0,1]$ is closed in the complete real line, and by Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:closed-complete} it is complete.

The same argument, with $a$ and $b$ in place of $0$ and $1$ and the tolerances $(a-L)/2$ and $(L-b)/2$, shows that every closed interval $[a,b]$ with $a<b$ is complete. For instance, if $L<a$, a late term $x_N$ satisfies $x_N<L+(a-L)/2=(L+a)/2<a$, where the last step holds because $L<a$ gives $L+a<2a$. This is impossible for a point of $[a,b]$.

\pauseq{Is the set $[0,\infty)$ of nonnegative real numbers, with distance $|x-y|$, complete?}{Yes. Suppose a sequence of nonnegative numbers converges on the real line to $L$. If $L<0$, the first case of the argument for $[0,1]$ applies word for word and produces a negative term, which is impossible. So $L\ge0$, the set $[0,\infty)$ is closed in $\RR$, and Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:closed-complete} shows that it is complete. A complete space need not have a right-hand end; what matters is that no limit is missing.}
