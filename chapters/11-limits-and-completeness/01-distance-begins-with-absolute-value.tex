\section{Distance begins with absolute value}\label{ch11:sec:distance}
Recall from Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} that the absolute value $|x|$ of a real number $x$ is $x$ when $x\ge0$ and $-x$ when $x<0$. The \emph{distance} between real numbers $x$ and $y$ is $|x-y|$, the length of the stretch of the number line between them; for $x=-2$ and $y=3$ it is $|-5|=5$. The order does not matter: $y-x=-(x-y)$, and changing the sign of a number does not change its absolute value, so $|y-x|=|x-y|$.

This chapter asks which properties of $|x-y|$ our arguments actually use. An argument that relies only on them then works for any objects with a distance that has the same properties, such as the vectors of Chapter~\ref{ch:linear}.

The most important of these properties is the \emph{triangle inequality}: for all real numbers $x$ and $y$,
\[
|x+y|\le|x|+|y|.
\]
For example, $x=5$ and $y=-2$ give $|x+y|=3<7=|x|+|y|$. When $x$ and $y$ are both nonnegative or both nonpositive, the two sides are equal: $x=-5$ and $y=-2$ give $|x+y|=7=|x|+|y|$.

To prove it, recall from Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} that, for $b\ge0$, the statement $|x|\le b$ holds exactly when $-b\le x\le b$. With $b=|x|$, the true statement $|x|\le|x|$ becomes $-|x|\le x\le|x|$. In the same way, $-|y|\le y\le|y|$. Adding the two left-hand inequalities, and then the two right-hand ones, gives
\[
-(|x|+|y|)\le x+y\le|x|+|y|.
\]
The same rule, now used in the other direction with $x+y$ in place of $x$ and the nonnegative number $b=|x|+|y|$, turns this into $|x+y|\le|x|+|y|$.

The triangle inequality is most often used in a second form. Given real numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$, take $x=a-b$ and $y=b-c$. Then $x+y=a-c$, and the triangle inequality becomes
\[
|a-c|\le|a-b|+|b-c|.
\]
In words: going from $a$ to $c$ by way of a stopping point $b$ is never shorter than going directly. For $a=0$, $b=5$ and $c=2$, the direct distance is $2$, while the route through $b$ has length $5+3=8$; when $b$ lies between $a$ and $c$, the two sides are equal. This form appears in almost every error estimate in this chapter: to show that $a$ and $c$ are close, find a third quantity $b$ close to each of them and add the two small distances.

Here is a first error estimate. Suppose $x$ approximates $a$ and $y$ approximates $b$, with errors $|x-a|\le\eps$ and $|y-b|\le\delta$, where $\delta$ (the Greek letter delta) is a second tolerance. Regrouping $(x+y)-(a+b)=(x-a)+(y-b)$ and applying the triangle inequality gives
\[
|(x+y)-(a+b)|\le |x-a|+|y-b|\le\eps+\delta.
\]
So when approximations are added, their error bounds add. With $a=2$, $b=5$, $\eps=0.01$ and $\delta=0.02$, the bound $0.03$ is reached by $x=2.01$ and $y=5.02$, whose errors point the same way, while errors in opposite directions partly cancel ($x=2.01$ and $y=4.98$ give $6.99$). Since we know only the sizes of the errors, not their directions, $\eps+\delta$ is the best guarantee available.

\pauseq{Suppose $|x-a|\le\eps$ and $|y-b|\le\delta$. Does it follow that $|(x-y)-(a-b)|\le\eps-\delta$? If not, what bound is correct?}{No. Take $a=b=0$, $x=\eps$ and $y=-\delta$. Both hypotheses hold, and $(x-y)-(a-b)=\eps+\delta$, which is larger than $\eps-\delta$ because $\delta>0$. The correct bound is $\eps+\delta$: write $(x-y)-(a-b)=(x-a)+\bigl(-(y-b)\bigr)$ and apply the triangle inequality, using $|-(y-b)|=|y-b|$. Subtracting two approximations does not subtract their errors; in the worst case the errors still add.}
