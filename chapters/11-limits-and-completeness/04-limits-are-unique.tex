\section{Limits are unique}\label{ch11:sec:unique}
Can a sequence converge to two different points at once? On the real line the answer seems obvious, but the proof below uses only the three requirements of a metric, so it settles the question in every metric space.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{proposition}\label{thm:limitunique}
A sequence in a metric space has at most one limit: if $x_n\to a$ and $x_n\to b$, then $a=b$.
\end{proposition}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Suppose $x_n\to a$ and $x_n\to b$, and assume, for contradiction, that $a\ne b$. By the first requirement of a metric, the number $D=d(a,b)$ is then positive, and so is the tolerance $D/3$. Convergence to $a$ supplies a threshold $N_1$ such that $d(x_n,a)<D/3$ for every $n\ge N_1$. Convergence to $b$ supplies a threshold $N_2$ such that $d(x_n,b)<D/3$ for every $n\ge N_2$. Now take a single index $n$ that is at least as large as both thresholds, for instance the larger of $N_1$ and $N_2$. The term $x_n$ is then close to both points. The triangle inequality with $x_n$ as the stopping point, together with the symmetry $d(a,x_n)=d(x_n,a)$, gives
\[
D=d(a,b)\le d(a,x_n)+d(x_n,b)<\frac D3+\frac D3=\frac{2D}3.
\]
So $D<2D/3$; subtracting $2D/3$ from both sides gives $D/3<0$, which contradicts $D>0$. Therefore $a=b$.
\end{proof}

The tolerance $D/3$ is not the only one that works. Any positive tolerance $\eps$ with $2\eps\le D$ leads in the same way to $D<2\eps\le D$, which is again impossible.

The key step is the choice of one index beyond both thresholds. The triangle inequality needs a single point $x_n$ that is close to $a$ and close to $b$ at the same time, because that point is the stop on the route from $a$ to $b$. A term close to $a$ and a \emph{different} term close to $b$ would give two short pieces that do not join up into a route from $a$ to $b$. The phrase ``for every $n\ge N$'' in the definition of convergence is what allows us to choose a common index.

Because of the proposition, we may speak of \emph{the} limit of a convergent sequence. For example, the sequence $1/(n+1)$ of Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence} converges to $0$ and to no other real number.
