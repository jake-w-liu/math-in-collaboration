\section{Euclid's algorithm: why it is right and why it stops}\label{ch05:sec:euclid}
Recall from Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular} that a positive integer $d$ is a divisor of an integer $a$ when $a=dk$ for some integer $k$. We also say that $d$ \emph{divides} $a$, and we write $d\mid a$. Do not confuse the statement $d\mid a$, which is true or false, with the number $d/a$: for example, $6\mid84$ is true, because $84=6\cdot14$, while $6/84=1/14$. Every positive integer $d$ divides $0$, because $0=d\cdot0$.

Let $a$ and $b$ be nonnegative integers that are not both zero. A \emph{common divisor} of $a$ and $b$ is a positive integer that divides both of them. Their \emph{greatest common divisor}, written $\gcd(a,b)$, is the largest common divisor. For example, the positive divisors of $12$ are $1,2,3,4,6,12$ and those of $18$ are $1,2,3,6,9,18$, so the common divisors of $12$ and $18$ are $1,2,3,6$, and $\gcd(12,18)=6$. The definition has to exclude $a=b=0$: every positive integer divides $0$, so $0$ and $0$ have no largest common divisor. Every other pair has one. Its common divisors include $1$, and they all lie among $1,2,\ldots,c$, where $c$ is the larger of $a$ and $b$, which is positive: as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{ch05:prop:prime-product}, a positive divisor $d$ of $c$ satisfies $c=dk$ with $k=c/d$ a positive integer, so $k\ge1$ and $d\le c$. A nonempty finite set of integers has a largest member.

\subsection*{Division with remainder}
Let $a$ be any integer and $b$ a positive integer. Then there are unique integers $q$ and $r$ with
\[
a=qb+r,\qquad 0\le r<b.
\]
The integer $q$ is the \emph{quotient}; when $a\ge0$, it counts how many whole copies of $b$ fit into $a$. The integer $r$ is the \emph{remainder}, what is left over, also written $a\bmod b$.

For example, the largest multiple of $5$ that is at most $17$ is $15=3\cdot5$, so $17=3\cdot5+2$ and $17\bmod5=2$. The equation $17=2\cdot5+7$ is also true, but it is not division with remainder, because $7$ is not less than $5$. For a negative number, the largest multiple of $5$ that is at most $-7$ is $-10=(-2)\cdot5$, so $-7=(-2)\cdot5+3$ and $(-7)\bmod5=3$. The remainder is never negative.

The well-ordering property shows where $q$ and $r$ come from. Let $S$ be the set of all numbers of the form $a-qb$, with $q$ an integer, that are at least $0$; for $a=17$ and $b=5$, for example, $S=\{2,7,12,17,22,\ldots\}$. The set $S$ is never empty: if $a\ge0$, then $q=0$ puts $a$ itself in $S$, and if $a<0$, then $q=a$ puts $a-ab=(-a)(b-1)$ in $S$, because $-a$ is positive and $b-1\ge0$. So $S$ is a nonempty set of natural numbers, and by the well-ordering property (in the form of Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular}, which allows $0$) it has a smallest member. Call it $r$, and let $q$ be an integer with $r=a-qb$. Then $a=qb+r$ and $r\ge0$. Also $r<b$: if $r\ge b$, then $r-b=a-(q+1)b$ would also belong to $S$ and be smaller than $r$, which is impossible.

No other pair of integers works. Suppose also that $a=q'b+r'$ with $0\le r'<b$. Subtracting $a=qb+r$ gives $0=(q'-q)b+(r'-r)$, so $r'-r=(q-q')b$. Since $r$ and $r'$ both lie between $0$ and $b-1$, their difference satisfies $-b<r'-r<b$, so $-b<(q-q')b<b$. Dividing by the positive number $b$ shows that the integer $q-q'$ lies strictly between $-1$ and $1$, so $q-q'=0$. Hence $q'=q$, and then $r'=a-qb=r$.

\subsection*{One step of Euclid's algorithm}
An \emph{algorithm} is a list of precise instructions that can be followed mechanically, one step at a time, like the school method for long division. Euclid's algorithm, from Euclid's \emph{Elements} (around 300 BC), computes greatest common divisors by repeating a single step: replace the pair $(a,b)$ by $(b,r)$, where $r=a\bmod b$. For example, $(84,30)$ is replaced by $(30,24)$, because $84=2\cdot30+24$. The common divisors of $84$ and $30$ are $1,2,3,6$, and so are those of $30$ and $24$. This is no coincidence.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lemma}\label{ch05:lem:euclid-step}
Let $a\ge0$ and $b>0$ be integers, and write $a=qb+r$ with integers $q,r$ and $0\le r<b$. Then the common divisors of $a$ and $b$ are exactly the common divisors of $b$ and $r$. Consequently $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)$.
\end{lemma}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Let $d$ be a positive integer. First suppose that $d$ divides both $a$ and $b$, say $a=ds$ and $b=dt$ with integers $s$ and $t$. Then
\[
 r=a-qb=ds-qdt=d(s-qt).
\]
Since $s-qt$ is an integer, $d$ divides $r$. So every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ is a common divisor of $b$ and $r$.

Conversely, suppose that $d$ divides both $b$ and $r$, say $b=dt$ and $r=du$ with integers $t$ and $u$. Then
\[
 a=qb+r=qdt+du=d(qt+u),
\]
so $d$ divides $a$ as well. So every common divisor of $b$ and $r$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$.

The two directions together show that the pairs $(a,b)$ and $(b,r)$ have exactly the same set of common divisors. Both greatest common divisors are defined, because $b>0$. Each is the largest member of the same set, so they are equal.
\end{proof}

Both directions are needed. With only the first, the pair $(b,r)$ might have an extra common divisor, larger than every common divisor of $a$ and $b$, and then $\gcd(b,r)$ would be bigger than $\gcd(a,b)$.

\subsection*{The algorithm, and why its answer is right}
Euclid's algorithm starts with a pair $(a,b)$ of nonnegative integers, not both zero. To keep the starting pair in view, we call the current pair $(u,v)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start with $(u,v)=(a,b)$.
\item If $v=0$, stop and output $u$.
\item Otherwise replace $(u,v)$ by $(v,\,u\bmod v)$ and go back to step~2.
\end{enumerate}

For $(a,b)=(84,30)$ the divisions are
\[
84=2\cdot30+24,\qquad30=1\cdot24+6,\qquad24=4\cdot6+0.
\]
The successive pairs are $(84,30)$, $(30,24)$, $(24,6)$, $(6,0)$, and the algorithm outputs $6$. If the first number is smaller than the second, the first step simply swaps them: for $(30,84)$, we have $30=0\cdot84+30$, so the next pair is $(84,30)$.

Why is the output the greatest common divisor? Every number in the pairs is nonnegative, because $a$ and $b$ are and a remainder is never negative. A step is taken only when $v$ is positive, so Lemma~\ref{ch05:lem:euclid-step}, with $u$ and $v$ in place of $a$ and $b$, applies to every step: no step changes the greatest common divisor of the current pair. By induction on the number of steps, $\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(a,b)$ for every pair that the algorithm produces. This is the invariant of the algorithm.

When the algorithm stops, the current pair has the form $(g,0)$ with $g>0$. Indeed, if no step was taken, then $b=0$ and $g=a$, which is positive because $a$ and $b$ are not both zero; otherwise $g$ was the second number of the previous pair, which was positive. Since every positive integer divides $0$, the common divisors of $g$ and $0$ are exactly the positive divisors of $g$, the largest of which is $g$. So $\gcd(g,0)=g$, and by the invariant, $g=\gcd(a,b)$.

\subsection*{Why the algorithm stops}
The invariant shows that \emph{if} the algorithm stops, its answer is correct; to show that it stops, we need a measure of progress. Watch the second number $v$ of the current pair. Each step replaces it by $u\bmod v$, which is less than $v$. So the second number is a nonnegative integer that strictly decreases at every step.

An integer that decreases goes down by at least $1$: if $w$ and $v$ are integers with $w<v$, then $w\le v-1$. The second number starts at $b$, so by induction on $s$, after $s$ steps it is at most $b-s$. It is never negative, so $0\le b-s$, that is, $s\le b$. Hence at most $b$ steps can occur, so the algorithm stops within $b$ steps. For $(84,30)$ the second numbers were $30,24,6,0$: the bound allows at most $30$ steps, and in fact $3$ were enough. The same reasoning shows that a strictly decreasing list of nonnegative integers cannot go on forever: if it starts at $c$, it has at most $c+1$ entries.

\pauseq{Does a strictly decreasing nonnegative real quantity prove that a process stops?}{No. The gaps $1-x_n=2^{-n}$ of Section~\ref{ch05:sec:recurrence}, namely $1,\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\ldots$, decrease forever and remain positive. A nonnegative \emph{integer} must drop by at least $1$ each time it decreases, but a real number can decrease by smaller and smaller amounts and never run out of room.}

\begin{principle}{Habit 5: separate preservation from progress}
For a process that repeats one step, ask two separate questions. What stays true after every step? And what quantity measures progress: a nonnegative integer that must decrease, or an error that must shrink? A preserved property can prove that an answer is correct without proving that the process ever stops. An error that keeps shrinking shows that the process approaches the answer, not that it stops.
\end{principle}
