\section{An invariant shows what cannot change}\label{ch05:sec:invariant}
Many problems describe a process: there is a collection of possible \emph{states}, and there are \emph{legal moves}, each of which turns one state into another. An \emph{invariant} of such a process is a quantity or property that every legal move leaves unchanged.

Here is an example. The states are rows of $n$ signs, each a plus or a minus, where $n\ge2$. A legal move picks two different positions in the row and flips the sign in each: a plus becomes a minus, and a minus becomes a plus. Can we start from a row of all plus signs and reach a row with exactly one minus sign?

To find an invariant, replace each plus sign by the number $1$ and each minus sign by $-1$, and multiply all the numbers in the row together. Call the result the \emph{product of the row}. Flipping one sign multiplies the product by $-1$, so a legal move, which flips two signs, multiplies it by $(-1)(-1)=1$: the product never changes. For instance, the row $+\,-\,+\,+$ has product $1\cdot(-1)\cdot1\cdot1=-1$, and flipping the first two signs gives $-\,+\,+\,+$, whose product is still $-1$.

A row of all plus signs has product $1$. Call a row \emph{reachable} if some sequence of legal moves turns the all-plus row into it. Induction on the number of moves shows that every reachable row also has product $1$. Let $P(k)$ be the statement ``every row obtained from the all-plus row by $k$ legal moves has product $1$.'' The base case $P(0)$ holds, because after $0$ moves the row is still all plus signs. For the induction step, assume $P(k)$. A row obtained by $k+1$ moves comes from a row obtained by $k$ moves, whose product is $1$ by the induction hypothesis, followed by one more move, which does not change the product; so $P(k+1)$ holds. A row with exactly one minus sign has product $-1$, so it can never be reached, however many moves we make. Notice that the proof never examined sequences of moves one by one, which would be hopeless, since moves can be repeated and there are infinitely many sequences.

A row with $m$ minus signs has product $(-1)^m$, so the invariant says that the number of minus signs stays even. Here the invariant is the only obstacle: every row with an even number of minus signs \emph{can} be reached from the all-plus row, by pairing up the positions that should hold minus signs and flipping one pair at a time. In general, though, an invariant can only show that a state is unreachable; to show that a state can be reached, we must exhibit moves that get there.

An invariant also does not show that a process comes to an end. Nothing in the sign-flipping rules forces us to stop: we can flip the first two signs, flip them back, and repeat forever, and every row along the way has product $1$. For a process that is meant to finish, we need a separate argument that it makes \emph{progress}. Euclid's algorithm, in the next section, needs both kinds of argument.
