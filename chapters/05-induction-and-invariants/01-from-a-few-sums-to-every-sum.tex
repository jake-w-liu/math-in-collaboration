\section{From a few sums to every sum}\label{ch05:sec:first-induction}
Add up the odd positive integers, one at a time:
\[
1=1,\qquad 1+3=4,\qquad 1+3+5=9,\qquad 1+3+5+7=16.
\]
The totals $1,4,9,16$ are the squares $1^2,2^2,3^2,4^2$. The $n$th odd positive integer is $2n-1$ (for $n=1,2,3$ this gives $1,3,5$), so the pattern suggests that
\[
1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2\qquad\text{for every } n\ge1.
\]
As Section~\ref{ch01:sec:pattern} explained, a few examples do not prove a pattern. Even a computer check of the first thousand values of $n$ would tell us only about those thousand values. What we need is a reason why the pattern can never break.

Here is the reason. Compare the statement for $n$ with the statement for $n+1$. The left side gains one new term, the next odd number, which is $2(n+1)-1=2n+1$. The right side changes from $n^2$ to $(n+1)^2$, an increase of
\[
(n+1)^2-n^2=n^2+2n+1-n^2=2n+1.
\]
So both sides increase by exactly the same amount, $2n+1$. If the two sides are equal for some value of $n$, they are still equal for the next value. They are equal for $n=1$, so they are equal for $n=2$, hence for $n=3$, and so on forever. This way of reasoning is called \emph{induction}.

\subsection*{The principle of induction}
Let $P(n)$ be a statement about a natural number $n$ that becomes true or false once a value of $n$ is chosen, as in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:negation}. For instance, $P(n)$ might be the statement ``$1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2$.'' To prove that $P(n)$ is true for every natural number $n$, it is enough to prove two things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the \emph{base case}: $P(0)$ is true;
\item the \emph{induction step}: for every natural number $k$, if $P(k)$ is true, then $P(k+1)$ is true.
\end{enumerate}
In the induction step we fix a natural number $k$, without choosing any particular value for it, and assume that $P(k)$ is true. This temporary assumption is called the \emph{induction hypothesis}. From it we must deduce $P(k+1)$. Because $k$ was arbitrary, the one argument works for every value of $k$ at once.

Why do these two facts give $P(n)$ for every $n$? The base case gives $P(0)$. The induction step with $k=0$ turns $P(0)$ into $P(1)$; with $k=1$ it turns $P(1)$ into $P(2)$; and so on. To reach $P(5)$, for example, we use the induction step five times, with $k=0,1,2,3,4$. In the same way, every natural number $n$ is reached after $n$ uses of the step. Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular} gives a more careful version of this argument and explains why assuming $P(k)$ is not the same as assuming what we want to prove.

Induction does not have to start at $0$. If a statement is claimed only for $n\ge1$, prove $P(1)$ as the base case and prove the induction step for every $k\ge1$. The same chain then reaches $1,2,3,\ldots$.

\subsection*{Watching one induction step}
Before writing the general step, watch it happen at $k=3$. The formula at $3$ says $1+3+5=3^2=9$. The next sum is the old sum plus the next odd number, $7$:
\[
1+3+5+7=(1+3+5)+7=9+7=16=4^2.
\]
The general step does exactly the same thing with $k$ in place of $3$. The sum of the first $k+1$ odd numbers is the sum of the first $k$ odd numbers plus the next one, $2k+1$. The induction hypothesis replaces the first part by $k^2$, and $k^2+2k+1=(k+1)^2$.

\subsection*{Starting at zero}
In this book zero is a natural number, so we would like the formula to start at $n=0$. In sigma notation the left side is $\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2j-1)$. For $n=0$ this is $\sum_{j=1}^{0}(2j-1)$, where the index $j$ is supposed to run from $1$ up to $0$. There are no such values of $j$, so this sum has no terms at all. A sum with no terms is called an \emph{empty sum}, and its value is defined to be $0$. This is the only sensible choice: adding no numbers to a running total should leave the total unchanged, and the only number whose addition changes nothing is $0$. With this agreement the formula holds at $n=0$, since both sides are $0$: we add no odd numbers, and $0^2=0$. The agreement also keeps the rule ``the sum of the first $k+1$ terms is the sum of the first $k$ terms plus the last term'' true when $k=0$: the one-term sum $\sum_{j=1}^{1}(2j-1)$ equals $0+1=1$. The proof below uses this rule in its induction step, including the step from $0$ to $1$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:odds}
For every $n\in\NN$,
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2j-1)=n^2.
\]
For $n=0$ the left side is the empty sum, which is zero.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
We use induction on $n$.

\emph{Base case.} For $n=0$ the left side is the empty sum, which is $0$, and the right side is $0^2=0$.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $k$ be a natural number, and assume the induction hypothesis
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{k}(2j-1)=k^2.
\]
We must show that $\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(2j-1)=(k+1)^2$. Split the sum for $k+1$ into its first $k$ terms and its last term, the term with $j=k+1$:
\begin{align*}
\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(2j-1)
&=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(2j-1)+\bigl(2(k+1)-1\bigr)\\
&=k^2+\bigl(2(k+1)-1\bigr) &&\text{by the induction hypothesis}\\
&=k^2+2k+1\\
&=(k+1)^2.
\end{align*}
This is the formula with $k+1$ in place of $n$. By induction, the formula holds for every natural number $n$.
\end{proof}

Throughout the induction step, $k$ names one fixed natural number. Its meaning does not change partway through, and the proof uses nothing about $k$ except the induction hypothesis. If an induction argument puzzles you, rewrite its induction step with a specific number, say $k=2$:
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{3}(2j-1)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}(2j-1)+5=2^2+5=9=3^2.
\]
Then return to the general $k$ and check that each line you wrote would work just as well for any other value.
