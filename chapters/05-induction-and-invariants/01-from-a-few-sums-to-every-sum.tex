\section{From a few sums to every sum}\label{ch05:sec:first-induction}
Add up the odd positive integers, one at a time:
\[
1=1,\qquad 1+3=4,\qquad 1+3+5=9,\qquad 1+3+5+7=16.
\]
The totals are the squares $1^2,2^2,3^2,4^2$. The $n$th odd positive integer is $2n-1$, so the pattern suggests that
\[
1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2\qquad\text{for every } n\ge1.
\]
Section~\ref{ch01:sec:notation} asked whether this always holds, and as Section~\ref{ch01:sec:pattern} explained, examples cannot settle it: even a computer check of the first thousand values of $n$ tells us only about those values. We need a reason why the pattern can never break. The idea is to show that whenever the formula holds for one value of $n$, it also holds for the next; since it holds for $n=1$, it then holds for $n=2$, hence for $n=3$, and so on forever. This way of reasoning is called \emph{induction}.

\subsection*{The principle of induction}
Let $P(n)$ be a statement about a natural number $n$ that becomes true or false once a value of $n$ is chosen, as in Section~\ref{ch02:sec:negation}; for instance, ``$1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2$,'' whose meaning at $n=0$ is explained below. To prove that $P(n)$ is true for every natural number $n$, it is enough to prove two things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the \emph{base case}: $P(0)$ is true;
\item the \emph{induction step}: for every natural number $k$, if $P(k)$ is true, then $P(k+1)$ is true.
\end{enumerate}
In the induction step, $k$ stands for a natural number, but we do not say which one, and we assume that $P(k)$ is true. This temporary assumption is the \emph{induction hypothesis}, and from it we must deduce $P(k+1)$. Because the argument never uses a particular value of $k$, it works for every $k$ at once.

These two facts give $P(n)$ for every $n$: the base case gives $P(0)$, and $n$ uses of the induction step, with $k=0,1,\ldots,n-1$, carry it on to $P(1),P(2),\ldots,P(n)$. Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular} makes this argument more careful.

Induction does not have to start at $0$. To prove $P(n)$ for every integer $n\ge1$, prove $P(1)$ as the base case and the induction step for every $k\ge1$; the chain then reaches $1,2,3,\ldots$. Any other starting point works the same way.

\subsection*{Starting at zero}
Our formula also makes sense at $n=0$, which is a natural number in this book. In sigma notation its left side is $\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2j-1)$, and for $n=0$ the index $j$ would run from $1$ up to $0$: there are no such $j$, so the sum has no terms. A sum with no terms is called an \emph{empty sum}, and its value is defined to be $0$, since adding no numbers to a running total leaves it unchanged. The book uses this agreement throughout. It keeps the rule ``the sum of the first $k+1$ terms is the sum of the first $k$ terms plus the last term'' true when $k=0$: the one-term sum $\sum_{j=1}^{1}(2j-1)$ equals $0+1=1$.

\subsection*{Watching one induction step}
Before writing the general step, watch it happen at $k=3$. The formula at $3$ says $1+3+5=3^2=9$. The next sum is the old sum plus the next odd number, $7$:
\[
1+3+5+7=(1+3+5)+7=9+7=16=4^2.
\]
The induction step below does exactly the same thing with $k$ in place of $3$. When an induction argument puzzles you, rewrite its step like this with a specific number.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:odds}
For every $n\in\NN$,
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2j-1)=n^2.
\]
For $n=0$ the left side is the empty sum, which is zero.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
We use induction on $n$.

\emph{Base case.} For $n=0$ the left side is the empty sum, which is $0$, and the right side is $0^2=0$.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $k$ be a natural number, and assume the induction hypothesis
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{k}(2j-1)=k^2.
\]
We must show that $\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(2j-1)=(k+1)^2$. Split the sum for $k+1$ into its first $k$ terms and its last term, the term with $j=k+1$:
\begin{align*}
\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(2j-1)
&=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(2j-1)+\bigl(2(k+1)-1\bigr)\\
&=k^2+\bigl(2(k+1)-1\bigr) &&\text{by the induction hypothesis}\\
&=k^2+2k+1\\
&=(k+1)^2.
\end{align*}
This is the formula with $k+1$ in place of $n$. By induction, the formula holds for every natural number $n$.
\end{proof}
