\section{Euclid's algorithm: meaning and termination}\label{ch05:sec:euclid}
Recall from Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular} that a positive integer $d$ is a divisor of an integer $a$ when $a=dk$ for some integer $k$. We also say that $d$ \emph{divides} $a$, and we write $d\mid a$. For example, $6\mid84$ because $84=6\cdot14$, but $5$ does not divide $84$. Every positive integer divides $0$, because $0=d\cdot0$.

Let $a$ and $b$ be nonnegative integers that are not both zero. A \emph{common divisor} of $a$ and $b$ is a positive integer that divides both of them. Their \emph{greatest common divisor}, written $\gcd(a,b)$, is the largest common divisor. For example, the positive divisors of $12$ are $1,2,3,4,6,12$ and those of $18$ are $1,2,3,6,9,18$, so the common divisors of $12$ and $18$ are $1,2,3,6$, and $\gcd(12,18)=6$.

Why is there always a largest common divisor? The set of common divisors is not empty, because $1$ divides every integer. It is also finite. At least one of $a$ and $b$ is positive, say $a>0$. If $d$ divides $a$, then $a=dk$ where $k=a/d$ is positive, because $a$ and $d$ are. Since $k$ is a positive integer, $k\ge1$, and so $a=dk\ge d$. Thus every common divisor lies among $1,2,\ldots,a$, and a nonempty finite set of integers has a largest member. The definition has to exclude $a=b=0$: every positive integer divides $0$, so $0$ and $0$ have no largest common divisor.

\subsection*{Division with remainder}
Let $a$ be any integer and $b$ a positive integer. Then there are unique integers $q$ and $r$ with
\[
a=qb+r,\qquad 0\le r<b.
\]
The integer $q$ is the \emph{quotient}; it counts how many whole copies of $b$ fit into $a$. The integer $r$ is the \emph{remainder}, what is left over. The remainder is also written $a\bmod b$. This is a familiar fact of integer arithmetic, and we use it without a formal proof. To see where $q$ and $r$ come from, take $q$ to be the largest integer with $qb\le a$, and put $r=a-qb$. Then $r\ge0$ because $qb\le a$, and $r<b$ because $(q+1)b>a$ by the choice of $q$. For $b=2$ the remainder is $0$ or $1$, so the fact says that every integer can be written as $2q$ or $2q+1$, that is, every integer is even or odd, as Chapter~\ref{ch:proof} showed.

For example, $17=3\cdot5+2$, so the quotient is $3$ and the remainder is $17\bmod5=2$. The equation $17=2\cdot5+7$ is also true, but it is not division with remainder, because $7$ is not less than $5$. For a negative number, $-7=(-2)\cdot5+3$, so $(-7)\bmod5=3$. The remainder is never negative.

\subsection*{One step of Euclid's algorithm}
Euclid's algorithm is built on one idea: replace the pair $(a,b)$ by the pair $(b,r)$, where $r=a\bmod b$. For example, $(84,30)$ is replaced by $(30,24)$, because $84=2\cdot30+24$. The common divisors of $84$ and $30$ are $1,2,3,6$, and so are the common divisors of $30$ and $24$. This is no coincidence.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lemma}\label{ch05:lem:euclid-step}
Let $a\ge0$ and $b>0$ be integers, and write $a=qb+r$ with integers $q,r$ and $0\le r<b$. Then the common divisors of $a$ and $b$ are exactly the common divisors of $b$ and $r$. Consequently $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)$.
\end{lemma}
\end{minipage}\par

\begin{proof}
Let $d$ be a positive integer. First suppose that $d$ divides both $a$ and $b$, say $a=ds$ and $b=dt$ with integers $s$ and $t$. Then
\[
 r=a-qb=ds-qdt=d(s-qt).
\]
Since $s-qt$ is an integer, $d$ divides $r$. So every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ is a common divisor of $b$ and $r$.

Conversely, suppose that $d$ divides both $b$ and $r$, say $b=dt$ and $r=du$ with integers $t$ and $u$. Then
\[
 a=qb+r=qdt+du=d(qt+u),
\]
so $d$ divides $a$ as well. So every common divisor of $b$ and $r$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$.

The two directions together show that the pairs $(a,b)$ and $(b,r)$ have exactly the same set of common divisors. Both greatest common divisors are defined, because $b>0$. Each is the largest member of the same set, so they are equal.
\end{proof}

Both directions are needed. The first alone shows only that every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ is also a common divisor of $b$ and $r$. Without the second, the pair $(b,r)$ might have an extra, larger common divisor, and then its greatest common divisor would be bigger.

\subsection*{The algorithm, and why its answer is right}
Euclid's algorithm starts with a pair $(a,b)$ of nonnegative integers, not both zero, and repeats one step. To keep the starting pair in view, call the current pair $(u,v)$; at the start, $(u,v)=(a,b)$. While $v$ is positive, replace the pair $(u,v)$ by $(v,u\bmod v)$. When $v$ is $0$, stop and output $u$.

For $(a,b)=(84,30)$ the divisions are
\[
84=2\cdot30+24,\qquad30=1\cdot24+6,\qquad24=4\cdot6+0.
\]
The successive pairs are $(84,30)$, $(30,24)$, $(24,6)$, $(6,0)$, and the algorithm outputs $6$. If the first number is smaller than the second, the first step simply swaps them. For $(30,84)$, for example, $30=0\cdot84+30$, so the next pair is $(84,30)$.

Why is the output the greatest common divisor? Every number that appears in the pairs is nonnegative, and a step is taken only when $v$ is positive, so Lemma~\ref{ch05:lem:euclid-step}, applied with $u$ and $v$ in place of $a$ and $b$, covers every step. By the lemma, each step leaves the greatest common divisor of the current pair unchanged. By induction on the number of steps, $\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(a,b)$ holds for every pair that the algorithm produces. This is the invariant of the algorithm.

When the algorithm stops, the current pair has the form $(g,0)$, and $g>0$. Indeed, if no step was taken, then $b=0$ and $g=a$, which is positive because $a$ and $b$ are not both zero. If at least one step was taken, then $g$ was the second number of the previous pair, which was positive. Since every positive integer divides $0$, the common divisors of $g$ and $0$ are exactly the positive divisors of $g$, and the largest of these is $g$ itself. So $\gcd(g,0)=g$, and by the invariant, $g=\gcd(a,b)$.

\subsection*{Why the algorithm stops}
The invariant shows that \emph{if} the algorithm stops, its answer is correct. It does not show that the algorithm ever stops. For that we need a measure of progress. Watch the second number $v$ of the current pair. Each step replaces it by $u\bmod v$, a remainder on division by $v$, and such a remainder is less than $v$. So $v$ is a nonnegative integer that strictly decreases at every step. An integer that strictly decreases goes down by at least $1$ each time. The second number starts at $b$, so after $s$ steps it is at most $b-s$. It is never negative, so $b-s\ge0$, that is, $s\le b$: at most $b$ steps can occur. The same reasoning shows that there is no endless strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers. Once the second number reaches $0$, the algorithm stops and outputs the first number. For $(84,30)$ this bound allows at most $30$ steps; in fact $3$ were enough.

The two halves of the argument do different jobs. The invariant proves that the answer is right, and the decreasing integer proves that an answer is eventually reached.

\begin{principle}{Habit 5: separate preservation from progress}
For a process that repeats one step, ask two separate questions. What stays true after every step? And what quantity measures progress: a nonnegative integer that must decrease, or an error that must shrink? A preserved property can prove that an answer is correct without proving that the process ever stops. A quantity that keeps shrinking, like an error that halves at every step, can come as close to zero as we like without ever reaching it.
\end{principle}

\pauseq{Does a strictly decreasing nonnegative real quantity prove that a process stops?}{No. The gaps $1-x_n=2^{-n}$ in Section~\ref{ch05:sec:recurrence}, namely $1,\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\ldots$, decrease forever and remain positive. The termination proof for Euclid's algorithm depends on the decreasing quantity being a nonnegative \emph{integer}. An integer that decreases must drop by at least $1$ each time, so it cannot keep decreasing forever without becoming negative. A real number can decrease by smaller and smaller amounts and never run out of room.}
