\section{A sequence defined by a recurrence}\label{ch05:sec:recurrence}
A \emph{sequence} is an endless list of numbers $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$, one for each natural number. Each $x_n$ is called a \emph{term} of the sequence. The subscript $n$ gives its position in the list, counting from $0$: $x_0$ is the first term, $x_1$ the second, and in general $x_n$ is the term in position $n$. A \emph{recurrence} defines a sequence step by step: it gives the first term and a rule that computes each later term from the earlier ones. Let
\[
x_0=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad x_{n+1}=\frac{x_n+1}{2}\quad\text{for every } n\in\NN.
\]
Read the notation carefully: $x_{n+1}$ is the name of the term after $x_n$, whereas $x_n+1$ means one added to the current term. In words, the rule says: to get the next term, add one to the current term and halve the result. Starting from $x_0=0$,
\[
x_1=\frac{0+1}{2}=\frac12,\qquad x_2=\frac{\frac12+1}{2}=\frac34,\qquad x_3=\frac{\frac34+1}{2}=\frac78.
\]
The terms seem to approach $1$. They fall short of $1$ by $1$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac18$, each gap half the one before. This suggests the formula
\[
x_n=1-2^{-n},
\]
where $2^{-n}=1/2^n$ as in Section~\ref{ch01:sec:algebra}. We prove it by induction on $n$.

For the base case, take $n=0$. The formula gives $1-2^{-0}=1-2^{0}=1-1=0$, which is $x_0$. For the induction step, let $n$ be a natural number and assume that $x_n=1-2^{-n}$. (Here the letter $n$ plays the role that $k$ played in Section~\ref{ch05:sec:first-induction}; the name of the letter does not matter.) Substitute the induction hypothesis into the rule for the next term:
\begin{align*}
 x_{n+1}
 &=\frac{x_n+1}{2} &&\text{the rule for the next term}\\
 &=\frac{(1-2^{-n})+1}{2} &&\text{induction hypothesis}\\
 &=\frac{2-2^{-n}}2\\
 &=1-\frac{2^{-n}}2\\
 &=1-2^{-(n+1)}.
\end{align*}
The last equality uses $2^{-n}/2=1/(2^n\cdot2)=1/2^{n+1}=2^{-(n+1)}$. This is the formula at $n+1$, so by induction it holds for every natural number $n$.

The formula tells us more than the first few terms did. The gap between $x_n$ and $1$ is exactly $1-x_n=2^{-n}$. This gap halves at every step, so the terms come as close to $1$ as we like. For instance, $x_{10}=1-1/1024$, which is within $0.001$ of $1$, and $x_{20}=1-1/2^{20}$ is within one millionth of $1$, since $2^{20}=1048576$. Yet the gap is never zero, so no term ever equals $1$.

\subsection*{Tracking the error instead}
Why is $1$ the right number to compare with? If some term were exactly $1$, the next term would be $(1+1)/2=1$ again: the rule leaves $1$ unchanged. A number that an update rule leaves unchanged is called a \emph{fixed point} of the rule. Section~\ref{ch04:sec:unique} showed that $1$ is the only real number $x$ with $x=(x+1)/2$, so $1$ is the only fixed point of this rule.

This suggests a more revealing way to organize the calculation. Instead of tracking $x_n$ itself, track its \emph{error} $x_n-1$, the difference between the current term and the fixed point. The error is the gap $1-x_n$ studied above, with its sign reversed: it is negative when $x_n<1$, and its absolute value $\abs{x_n-1}$ is the distance from $x_n$ to $1$. Subtract $1$ from both sides of the rule:
\[
x_{n+1}-1=\frac{x_n+1}{2}-1=\frac{x_n+1-2}{2}=\frac{x_n-1}{2}.
\]
In words: each update exactly halves the error. Nothing in this calculation used the starting value $x_0=0$. So if we keep the same rule but allow \emph{any} real starting value $x_0$, induction gives
\[
x_n-1=2^{-n}(x_0-1)\qquad\text{for every } n\in\NN.
\]
At $n=0$ both sides equal $x_0-1$, because $2^0=1$. If the formula holds at $n$, halving both sides and using the rule $x_{n+1}-1=(x_n-1)/2$ gives it at $n+1$. Exercise~\ref{ex:5.5} asks you to write out this induction in full. With $x_0=0$ the formula becomes $x_n-1=-2^{-n}$, which is the formula $x_n=1-2^{-n}$ proved above.

This more general statement covers every starting value at once, yet its induction step is shorter than the one for $x_n=1-2^{-n}$. It records only what each step does, namely halve the error, while the first formula mixed that fact with the particular starting value $0$. So when an induction proof gets tangled, try proving a more general statement instead; its induction step can be simpler. We will meet the same idea in the contraction theorem of Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction}, where the distance to a fixed point shrinks at every step by at least a fixed factor.
