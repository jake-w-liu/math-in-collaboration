\section{Why induction is not circular}
A first reaction to induction is often suspicion: the induction step \emph{assumes} the formula at $k$, so are we assuming what we want to prove? We are not. The induction step proves a conditional statement, ``\emph{if} the formula holds at $k$, \emph{then} it holds at $k+1$,'' and proves it for every $k$ at once. It never asserts that the formula holds at any particular $k$. The base case supplies the first actual truth, and the conditional statement then carries that truth forward one step at a time, like a row of dominoes in which the first one is pushed and each one knocks over the next.

Here is a more careful way to see that nothing can slip through. Suppose we have proved the base case $P(0)$ and the induction step, and imagine that $P$ nevertheless failed for some natural number. Among all natural numbers where $P$ fails, choose the smallest one and call it $m$. (Every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest member; this is the same well-ordering property used in Chapter~4.) Now look at what we know about $m$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $m\ne0$, because $P(0)$ was proved directly. So $m-1$ is a natural number.
\item $P(m-1)$ is true, because $m-1$ is smaller than $m$ and $m$ was the \emph{smallest} failure.
\item The induction step, applied with $k=m-1$, turns $P(m-1)$ into $P(m)$.
\end{itemize}
So $P(m)$ is both false (by the choice of $m$) and true (by the last bullet). This contradiction shows that no failure can exist. This argument is called the \emph{minimal-counterexample} view of induction, and it will reappear whenever a proof begins ``choose a smallest counterexample.''

Sometimes the step to $k+1$ needs more than the case immediately before it. \emph{Strong induction} allows us to assume all of $P(0),P(1),\ldots,P(k)$ when proving $P(k+1)$. The same smallest-counterexample argument justifies it: if $m$ were the smallest failure, then \emph{every} smaller case would be true, which is exactly what the strong induction step needs in order to prove $P(m)$.

A classic example shows why the extra strength helps. Call an integer $p\ge2$ \emph{prime} if its only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. We claim that every integer $n\ge2$ is a prime or a product of primes. If $n$ is prime, there is nothing to do. Otherwise $n=ab$ for integers $a,b$ with $2\le a,b<n$. Knowing the claim for $n-1$ would be useless here, because $a$ and $b$ are usually far from $n-1$; for $n=12=3\cdot4$ we need the cases $3$ and $4$, not $11$. Strong induction gives the claim for every number from $2$ up to $n-1$, so in particular for $a$ and for $b$. Writing each as a product of primes and multiplying the two products expresses $n$ as a product of primes. In Chapter~9 a graph will split into two smaller parts whose sizes are not known in advance, and we will need strong induction for the same reason.
