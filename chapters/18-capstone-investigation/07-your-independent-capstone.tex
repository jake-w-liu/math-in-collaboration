\section{Your independent capstone}\label{ch18:sec:independent}
Now it is your turn. Choose one of the three problems that run through the book (assigning tasks, iterating a map, or sign sequences) and start with a question narrow enough that you can check every claim you make. Here are three possible starting points; none is expected to lead to a new theorem, but each leaves room to go further.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Assignments with capacities.} Allow a task to be given to several students, up to a fixed capacity, and find the right version of Hall's condition; Exercise~\ref{ex:18.5} takes the first step. Then ask how the search procedure of Chapter~\ref{ch:matching} changes.
\item \emph{Iteration with a specified pattern of errors.} Pick a pattern, such as $\eps_n=1/(n+1)$ or $\eps_n=s^n$ with $0<s<1$ and $s\ne q$, and find out as precisely as you can how fast $e_n$ goes to zero; Exercise~\ref{ex:18.4} treats the case $\eps_n\le aq^n$.
\item \emph{Sign sequences at a new length.} Extend the table of Section~\ref{ch14:sec:experiment} by one more length, with a program that anyone can run to reproduce your counts. Then find out which operations, such as negating every sign, reversing the order, or rotating, preserve each of the two properties the table counts.
\end{itemize}
Your write-up should contain:
\begin{itemize}
\item a precise statement of your question and your result, and, if the question changed while you worked on it, both versions, with the difference explained;
\item a map of the background your argument needs, like the prerequisite map of Section~\ref{ch17:sec:map};
\item a proof, or a counterexample;
\item computer checks where relevant, with the program, the software it runs on, and the range of cases it covers;
\item a record of the assumptions you used and of the evidence for each claim: proved, cited, checked by computer for finitely many cases, or not established, as in ``Keeping track of evidence'' in \emph{To the reader};
\item credit to every source you used, an honest account of how you used an AI assistant, and a list of what remains unsettled;
\item a short explanation of what is new compared with the published literature, and what is new only to you.
\end{itemize}
The investigation ends with a short oral discussion in which you explain one delicate step of your argument, change one of your assumptions, and predict what fails; your answers matter more than how polished your saved conversation with an assistant looks. Section~\ref{appE:sec:rubric} of Appendix~\ref{app:guide} suggests a grading rubric.

Before you hand in your work, ask an assistant, one request at a time, to restate your argument in its own words (a step it gets wrong is often one you explained badly), to find counterexamples to stronger versions of your statement and say whether each hypothesis is needed, and to check that your prose, formulas, code and any Lean statements agree. All three answers come from the same assistant and may share its blind spots, so they do not replace a careful human reader such as a classmate or an instructor; check each point they raise yourself.
