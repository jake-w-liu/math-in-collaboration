\section{The one-step estimate}\label{ch18:sec:onestep}
Write
\[
 e_n=d(y_n,x_*)
\]
for the distance from the $n$th point to the fixed point, and call it the \emph{error} at step~$n$. Keep the two kinds of error apart: $e_n$ measures how far $y_n$ is from $x_*$, while the update error $\eps_n$ measures how badly a single update went wrong.

To compare $e_{n+1}$ with $e_n$, insert the exact update $T(y_n)$ as a stop between $y_{n+1}$ and $x_*$:
\begin{align*}
 e_{n+1}
 &=d(y_{n+1},x_*)\\
 &\le d(y_{n+1},T(y_n))+d(T(y_n),x_*)
 &&\text{triangle inequality}\\
 &=d(y_{n+1},T(y_n))+d(T(y_n),T(x_*))
 &&\text{since $T(x_*)=x_*$}\\
 &\le\eps_n+q\,d(y_n,x_*)
 &&\text{update error and contraction inequality}\\
 &=q e_n+\eps_n.
\end{align*}
So for every $n\in\NN$ we have the \emph{one-step estimate}
\[
 e_{n+1}\le qe_n+\eps_n.
\]
In the spirit of Habit~3 (Section~\ref{ch03:sec:images}), notice how little the argument uses: the triangle inequality, the fixed-point equation, the bound on the update error and the contraction inequality; completeness enters only through the existence of $x_*$. So $X$ need not be a set of numbers, and the update errors need not follow any pattern.

\subsection*{Unfolding the estimate three times}
To see how an early update error affects later steps, substitute each bound into the next. Every substitution multiplies an inequality by $q$, which keeps its direction because $q>0$:
\begin{align*}
 e_1&\le qe_0+\eps_0,\\
 e_2&\le qe_1+\eps_1
       \le q(qe_0+\eps_0)+\eps_1
       =q^2e_0+q\eps_0+\eps_1,\\
 e_3&\le qe_2+\eps_2
       \le q(q^2e_0+q\eps_0+\eps_1)+\eps_2
       =q^3e_0+q^2\eps_0+q\eps_1+\eps_2.
\end{align*}
Each term has been multiplied by $q$ once for every update that came after it: $e_0$ three times, $\eps_0$ twice, and the latest error $\eps_2$ not at all. The same count works at every step: $\eps_j$ is made while producing $y_{j+1}$, and after it come the $n-(j+1)=n-1-j$ updates that produce $y_{j+2},\ldots,y_n$. So in the bound for $e_n$, $\eps_j$ carries the weight $q^{n-1-j}$, from $q^{n-1}$ for $\eps_0$ down to $q^0=1$ for $\eps_{n-1}$.

\subsection*{Two assumptions about the update errors}
Parts~2 and~3 of the theorem below make different assumptions. Part~2 assumes that the update errors are \emph{bounded}: $\eps_j\le\eta$ for every $j$, for a fixed number $\eta\ge0$, as for the constant bias. Part~3 assumes that they \emph{tend to zero} in the sense of Definition~\ref{ch11:def:convergence}: for every tolerance $\delta>0$, all the update errors from some index onward are smaller than $\delta$. (We write $\delta$ because $\eps$ already names the update errors.) This is the stronger assumption, because errors that tend to zero are bounded: with the tolerance $1$, every update error from some index $J$ on is below $1$, so the largest of $1,\eps_0,\ldots,\eps_{J-1}$ bounds them all. Yet such errors can add up to a lot: $\eps_j=1/(j+1)$ tends to zero (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence}), but $\eps_0+\cdots+\eps_{n-1}=1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1n$ is a harmonic sum, which does not converge (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:cauchy}). We will see that $y_n\to x_*$ all the same.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}[Iteration with update errors]\label{thm:inexact}
Let $(X,d)$ be a nonempty complete metric space, let $T:X\to X$ be a contraction with factor $q$, where $0<q<1$, and let $x_*$ be its fixed point. Let $y_0,y_1,y_2,\ldots$ be points of $X$ and let $\eps_0,\eps_1,\eps_2,\ldots$ be nonnegative numbers such that $d(y_{n+1},T(y_n))\le\eps_n$ for every $n\in\NN$. Write $e_n=d(y_n,x_*)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every integer $n\ge1$,
\[
 e_n\le q^n e_0+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-j}\eps_j.
\]
\item If there is a number $\eta\ge0$ with $\eps_j\le\eta$ for every $j\in\NN$, then for every integer $n\ge1$,
\[
 e_n\le q^n e_0+\eta\,\frac{1-q^n}{1-q}.
\]
\item If $\eps_n\to0$, then $y_n\to x_*$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

In part~2, the term $q^ne_0$, what remains of the starting error, tends to zero, while the second term, the combined effect of the update errors, never exceeds $\eta/(1-q)$ and approaches it as $n$ grows. So after many steps the error is at most a little more than $\eta/(1-q)$.

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Part 1.} We use induction on $n$. For $n=1$, the sum has the single term $j=0$, with weight $q^0=1$, so the claim is the one-step estimate $e_1\le qe_0+\eps_0$. Now suppose the claim holds for some $n\ge1$. Apply the one-step estimate, and then the induction hypothesis multiplied by $q>0$:
\begin{align*}
 e_{n+1}
 &\le qe_n+\eps_n\\
 &\le q\left(q^n e_0+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-j}\eps_j\right)+\eps_n\\
 &=q^{n+1}e_0+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^{n-j}\eps_j+q^0\eps_n\\
 &=q^{n+1}e_0+\sum_{j=0}^{n}q^{n-j}\eps_j.
\end{align*}
In the third line, $q\cdot q^{n-1-j}=q^{n-j}$, and $\eps_n$ is written as $q^0\eps_n$ so that it becomes the term $j=n$ of the new sum. Since $n-j=(n+1)-1-j$, the last line is the claim for $n+1$, which completes the induction.

\textbf{Part 2.} Suppose $\eps_j\le\eta$ for every $j$. Every weight is positive, so replacing each $\eps_j$ by the larger number $\eta$ cannot make the sum smaller. Then put $k=n-1-j$; as $j$ runs from $0$ up to $n-1$, the exponent $k$ runs from $n-1$ down to $0$:
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-j}\eps_j
 \le\eta\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-j}
 =\eta\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^k
 =\eta\,\frac{1-q^n}{1-q},
\]
by the finite geometric sum of Section~\ref{ch11:sec:geometric}. Together with part~1, this proves part~2.

\textbf{Part 3: the plan.} Now suppose $\eps_n\to0$. We must find, for every $\delta>0$, a threshold $N$ with $e_n<\delta$ for every $n\ge N$. The early update errors in the sum of part~1 may be large, but they are finitely many, and each later update multiplies their effect by another factor $q$. The late ones may be many, but each is small, and their weights add up to less than $1/(1-q)$. So we split the sum at an index $J$. First $\delta$ is given, then $J$ is chosen depending on $\delta$, and only then $N$. The index $J$ must not change with $n$, so that the early part is one fixed collection of finitely many terms, which a single $N$ can make small for every $n\ge N$ at once.

\textbf{Part 3: choosing $J$.} Let $\delta>0$. The number $(1-q)\delta/2$ is positive, and $\eps_j\to0$, so the definition of convergence, with this tolerance and the distance $|\eps_j-0|=\eps_j$, gives an index $J$ such that
\[
 \eps_j<\frac{(1-q)\delta}{2}\qquad\text{for every }j\ge J.
\]
A larger index works as well, so we may take $J\ge1$; then the early part below contains at least $\eps_0$. For every $n>J$, split the sum of part~1 into the terms with $j<J$ and those with $j\ge J$:
\[
 e_n\le\left(q^ne_0+\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}q^{n-1-j}\eps_j\right)+\sum_{j=J}^{n-1}q^{n-1-j}\eps_j.
\]

\textbf{Part 3: the early part.} Written out, the expression in parentheses is
\[
 q^ne_0+q^{n-1}\eps_0+q^{n-2}\eps_1+\cdots+q^{n-J}\eps_{J-1}.
\]
The smallest exponent is $n-J$, and factoring out $q^{n-J}$ leaves
\[
 C_J=q^Je_0+q^{J-1}\eps_0+q^{J-2}\eps_1+\cdots+\eps_{J-1},
\]
so the early part equals $q^{n-J}C_J$, where $C_J\ge0$ depends on $J$ but not on $n$.

\textbf{Part 3: the late part.} Every $\eps_j$ with $j\ge J$ is less than $(1-q)\delta/2$, the weights are positive, and the sum has at least the term $j=n-1$, because $n>J$. Substituting $k=n-1-j$ again,
\[
 \sum_{j=J}^{n-1}q^{n-1-j}\eps_j
 <\frac{(1-q)\delta}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1-J}q^k
 =\frac{(1-q)\delta}{2}\cdot\frac{1-q^{n-J}}{1-q}
 <\frac{(1-q)\delta}{2}\cdot\frac1{1-q}=\frac\delta2.
\]
This is why we asked for the tolerance $(1-q)\delta/2$: its factor $1-q$ cancels the $1/(1-q)$ that comes from adding up the weights, leaving half of $\delta$. The other half is kept for the early part.

\textbf{Part 3: choosing $N$.} So far, for every $n>J$,
\[
 e_n<q^{n-J}C_J+\frac\delta2.
\]
If $C_J=0$, this gives $e_n<\delta$ for every $n>J$, and $N=J+1$ will do. If $C_J>0$, then $q^m\to0$ (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:geometric}) gives a threshold $M\ge1$ such that $q^m<\delta/(2C_J)$ for every $m\ge M$. Put $N=J+M$. For every $n\ge N$ we have $n>J$ and $n-J\ge M$, so $q^{n-J}C_J<\delta/2$, and therefore
\[
 e_n<\frac\delta2+\frac\delta2=\delta.
\]
In both cases $d(y_n,x_*)=e_n<\delta$ for every $n\ge N$. Since $\delta>0$ was arbitrary, $y_n\to x_*$.
\end{proof}

Splitting a sum into finitely many early terms, damped by the powers of $q$, and late terms that are all small is a technique worth remembering; it combines the split of a tolerance into halves (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:cauchy}) with a split of the sum. In particular, the proof covers $\eps_j=1/(j+1)$, whose total is unbounded: it controls only the weighted sum, in which older errors carry smaller powers of $q$.
