\section{The complete proof}\label{ch17:sec:proof}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subsequence}
Let $(X,d)$ be a complete, totally bounded metric space. Then every sequence $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$ in $X$ has a subsequence that converges to a point of $X$.
\end{theorem}
\end{minipage}\par

Unlike the contraction theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:banach}), this theorem does not assume that $X$ is nonempty: if $X$ has no points, there is no sequence in $X$, and the statement is vacuously true (Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive}).

\begin{proof}
Let $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$ be a sequence in $X$.

\emph{Step 1: nested infinite sets of indices.} We construct infinite sets of indices $S_0\supseteq S_1\supseteq S_2\supseteq\cdots$ such that, for every $k\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{ch17:eq:stage}
 d(x_i,x_j)<2^{-k}\qquad\text{for all }i,j\in S_k.
\end{equation}
Start with $S_0=\NN$. Now let $k\ge1$, and suppose that an infinite set $S_{k-1}$ has been constructed. Since $X$ is totally bounded, finitely many balls of radius $2^{-(k+1)}$ cover $X$. For each of these balls, take the set of indices $n$ with $x_n$ in that ball; since every $x_n$ lies in some ball, every index lies in one of these finitely many sets. By Lemma~\ref{ch17:lem:pigeonhole}, applied to the infinite set $S_{k-1}$, there is a ball $B$ among them such that the set
\[
 S_k=\set{n\in S_{k-1}:x_n\in B}
\]
is infinite. By its definition, $S_k\subseteq S_{k-1}$. If $i,j\in S_k$, then $x_i$ and $x_j$ both lie in $B$, so the diameter bound gives $d(x_i,x_j)<2\cdot2^{-(k+1)}=2^{-k}$, which is~\eqref{ch17:eq:stage}. Since $S_k$ is again infinite, the construction continues for every $k$.

\emph{Step 2: increasing indices.} Choose any $n_1\in S_1$. For $k\ge2$, once $n_{k-1}$ has been chosen, choose $n_k\in S_k$ with $n_k>n_{k-1}$. Such an index exists because $S_k$ is infinite, and an infinite set of natural numbers contains a number larger than any given one (Section~\ref{ch17:sec:subsequence}). Then $n_1<n_2<n_3<\cdots$, so $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},x_{n_3},\ldots$ is a subsequence, and $n_k\in S_k$ for every $k\ge1$.

\emph{Step 3: later terms keep the earlier bounds.} Let $1\le k\le\ell$. The sets are nested, so $n_\ell\in S_\ell\subseteq S_k$: every term chosen at stage~$k$ or later has its index in $S_k$. So if $\ell\ge k$ and $m\ge k$, then $n_\ell,n_m\in S_k$, and~\eqref{ch17:eq:stage} gives
\[
 d(x_{n_\ell},x_{n_m})<2^{-k}.
\]

\emph{Step 4: the subsequence is a Cauchy sequence.} Let $\eps>0$. By the Archimedean property (Section~\ref{ch02:sec:quantifiers}), choose an integer $K>1/\eps$. It is positive, so $K\ge1$; this matters, because~\eqref{ch17:eq:stage} holds only from stage~$1$ on. Since $2^K\ge K+1$ (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence}),
\[
 2^{-K}\le\frac1{K+1}<\frac1K<\eps.
\]
For any $\ell,m\ge K$, Step~3 with $k=K$ gives $d(x_{n_\ell},x_{n_m})<2^{-K}<\eps$. Since $\eps>0$ was arbitrary, the subsequence is a Cauchy sequence.

\emph{Step 5: completeness supplies the limit.} The space $X$ is complete, so the Cauchy sequence $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},x_{n_3},\ldots$ converges to some point $x_*$ of $X$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Watching the construction on an example}
The interval $[-1,2]$, with distance $|x-y|$, is complete, being a closed interval (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:complete}), and totally bounded by the argument used for $[0,1]$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:17.4} asks you to write it out for every interval $[a,b]$). In this space consider
\[
 x_n=(-1)^n+\frac1{n+1},
\]
whose first terms are $2,-\tfrac12,\tfrac43,-\tfrac34,\tfrac65$. The even-indexed terms crowd towards $1$ from above, and the odd-indexed terms crowd towards $-1$, never rising above $-\tfrac12$.

Stage~$k$ can cover $[-1,2]$, as we covered $[0,1]$, by balls of radius $2^{-(k+1)}$ whose centers run from $-1$ to $2$ in steps of $2^{-(k+1)}$; one of the centers is $1$. At stage~$1$ these are thirteen balls of radius $1/4$. The ball $B(1,1/4)$ contains $x_n$ exactly when $n$ is even and $\tfrac1{n+1}<\tfrac14$, that is, when $n$ is even and $n\ge4$, so the construction may take $S_1=\set{4,6,8,\ldots}$. Choosing the ball around $1$ at every stage gives the set $S_k$ of even numbers $n$ with $\tfrac1{n+1}<2^{-(k+1)}$, that is, with $n\ge2^{k+1}$: $S_2=\set{8,10,12,\ldots}$, $S_3=\set{16,18,20,\ldots}$, and so on.

Taking in Step~2 the smallest allowed index each time gives $n_1=4$, $n_2=8$, $n_3=16$, and in general $n_k=2^{k+1}$. The subsequence $x_4,x_8,x_{16},\ldots$, with terms $1+\tfrac1{2^{k+1}+1}$, converges to $1$. Stage~$1$ could instead have chosen $B(-1,1/4)$, which contains $x_n$ for every odd $n\ge5$; then every later index would have been odd, and the subsequence would converge to $-1$: the theorem promises \emph{some} convergent subsequence, not a particular one. In general the indices follow no simple formula; each $n_k$ need only lie in $S_k$ and exceed $n_{k-1}$.

\subsection*{Why the proof picks a new index at every stage}
Why not look for a single index $n$ in every set $S_k$, so that the one term $x_n$ lies in every chosen ball? In general there is none: nested infinite sets can have no common member. For example, the sets $\set{k,k+1,k+2,\ldots}$ for $k=1,2,3,\ldots$ are infinite and nested, but no natural number $n$ belongs to all of them, since $n\notin\set{n+1,n+2,\ldots}$. And even when such an index exists, as for a constant sequence, it gives one term, not a subsequence. So the proof picks a new, later index from each set, and Step~3 shows that any two sufficiently late picks lie together in one earlier set, which is exactly what the Cauchy condition asks for.

\pauseq{Where does the proof use each hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:subsequence}?}{Total boundedness is used at every stage of Step~1, to cover $X$ by finitely many balls of radius $2^{-(k+1)}$; the finiteness of that cover is what allows Lemma~\ref{ch17:lem:pigeonhole} to keep infinitely many indices. Completeness is used once, in Step~5, to give the Cauchy subsequence a limit in $X$. The diameter bound, the nesting of the index sets and the increasing choice of indices are not hypotheses but facts proved along the way.}
