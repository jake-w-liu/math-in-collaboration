\section{Reading a formalized research proof with the same method}\label{ch17:sec:research}
We now apply the same method to the two research claims met earlier in the book: Seymour's second-neighborhood conjecture (Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour}) and the circulant Hadamard conjecture (Section~\ref{ch14:sec:research}). Claimed proofs of both appear in OpenAI's public mathematics repository~\cite{oairepo}. The repository collects \emph{preprints}, which are research papers made public before a journal has reviewed them, together with formal proofs in Lean. For each problem it also describes exactly which statements were formalized. Being listed in a repository is not, by itself, a guarantee about every claim in a paper. The right unit of reading is one exact theorem, together with its definitions and the results it depends on, not a title or an impressive final line.

\subsection*{Decoding the statement}
Start with Seymour's conjecture. The repository's formal statement is about a nonempty finite oriented graph, and it asks for a vertex whose second out-neighborhood has at least as many vertices as its first~\cite{oai173}. Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour} supplies the mathematical vocabulary, and Chapter~\ref{ch:lean2} showed how the exclusions in the definition of a second out-neighbor are written in Lean. The theorem is called \code{exists\_goodVertex}. Reading its statement piece by piece, we find the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{V} is the type of vertices. Three assumptions written in square brackets, \code{[Fintype V]}, \code{[Nonempty V]} and \code{[DecidableEq V]}, say that there are finitely many vertices, that there is at least one vertex, and that Lean has a procedure for deciding whether two vertices are equal. The last of these is a technical requirement of Lean's library for finite sets. It does not exclude any graph, because classical reasoning, using the standard axioms discussed below, supplies such a procedure for every type.
\item The edge relation \code{r} has type \code{V -> V -> Prop}, so \code{r v w} is the proposition $v\to w$, exactly as in Chapter~\ref{ch:lean2}.
\item The explicit hypothesis \code{(hr : IsOriented r)} says that \code{r} has no loops and no pair of opposite edges. The definition of \code{IsOriented} has exactly these two parts, named \code{loopless} and \code{asymmetric}.
\item The conclusion says that some vertex \code{v} satisfies \code{GoodVertex r v}. This is defined to mean that the set \code{firstNeighbors r v} has at most as many members as the set \code{secondNeighbors r v}. The second of these sets is defined by exactly the three conditions of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour}: $w\ne v$, $v\to w$ fails, and $v\to u\to w$ for some vertex $u$.
\end{itemize}
Each piece of the formal statement matches a piece of the informal one, and no condition of the informal statement is missing. Reading the statement in this way needed nothing beyond Chapters~\ref{ch:graphs}, \ref{ch:lean1} and~\ref{ch:lean2}.

\subsection*{Locating the main steps}
The proof is in a different file~\cite{oaimain}. That file imports three further files, named \code{Reduction}, \code{Pruning} and \code{ExtremalContradiction}, and its own proof is only a few lines long. It argues by contradiction. It assumes that no vertex is good, so that the graph is a counterexample; it applies a theorem from the reduction file to turn the counterexample into a new oriented graph with additional properties; and it then applies results from the other two files to reach a contradiction.

The reduction theorem~\cite{oaireduction} says that from any counterexample one can build, for some positive integer $n$, an oriented graph whose vertices are the pairs $(i,j)$ of natural numbers with $i<n$ and $j\le n$, and which has two further properties, named \code{PositiveIndegree} and \code{StrictSubsetGrowth}. The reduction file in turn imports three more files, so the map keeps branching. The name \code{PositiveIndegree} suggests that every vertex has at least one edge coming in, but a name is not a definition: as Section~\ref{ch15:sec:symbols} showed, Lean accepts any name for any statement. To understand these properties, you have to open their definitions and read them.

\subsection*{Keeping a reading record}
A \emph{reading record} is a written account of a proof you are studying: what you have read, what you have understood, and what remains open. Appendix~\ref{app:guide} describes what a finished one contains. At this point, a reading record of the Seymour proof has entries of three different kinds.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Understood:} the final statement has been decoded, and its definitions match the informal conjecture.
\item \emph{Identified but borrowed:} the logical role of the reduction is clear, since it turns a counterexample into a graph with extra properties, but its proof has not been read.
\item \emph{Open:} the meaning of \code{PositiveIndegree} and \code{StrictSubsetGrowth}, and the mathematics of the pruning step and of the final contradiction, remain to be studied from their sources.
\end{itemize}
The proof as a whole is long and uses ideas well beyond this course. A dependency sketch like this one is a way into the proof, not a replacement for it.

\subsection*{What the formal check compares}
How does the repository connect the official statement to the proof? A configuration file~\cite{oaiconfig} lists what the formal check compares: the file containing the official statement, the file containing the proof, the name of the theorem, and the axioms the proof may use. The permitted axioms are \code{propext}, \code{Quot.sound} and \code{Classical.choice}, the three standard axioms discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:lean2}. The official statement file contains \code{sorry} in place of a proof, because its only job is to record what must be proved. The check is designed to confirm that the proof file proves exactly that statement, using no axioms beyond the permitted ones. Notice what does the work here: the two statements are compared, not merely their names. A theorem in the proof file that had the same name as the official one would, by itself, say nothing about whether the two statements agree.

\subsection*{The circulant Hadamard conjecture}
For the circulant Hadamard conjecture of Chapter~\ref{ch:signs}, you can decode the matrix and correlation conditions yourself, and you have proved a necessary condition, Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary}: a circulant Hadamard matrix of order greater than $4$ would need an order $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$, such as $16$, $36$ or $64$. The repository's description reports a complete classification, in which such a matrix of positive order $n$ exists exactly when $n=1$ or $n=4$. It also separates the formalized statement about Barker sequences of even length from the preprint's claims about odd lengths, which were not formalized~\cite{oai179}. You can therefore ask a precise next question: which lemma of the proof rules out the orders $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$ that our elementary argument still allows? That question is informed by mathematics you have done yourself, and it will get you much further than asking an assistant to ``explain the whole repository.''
