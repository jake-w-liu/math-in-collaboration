\section{Reading a formalized research proof with the same method}\label{ch17:sec:research}
Claimed proofs of Seymour's second-neighborhood conjecture (Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour}) and of the circulant Hadamard conjecture (Section~\ref{ch14:sec:research}) appear in OpenAI's public mathematics repository~\cite{oairepo}, which collects preprints with formal proofs in Lean and describes exactly which statements were formalized. A paper's presence there does not vouch for every claim in it. The right unit of reading is one exact theorem, with its definitions and the results it depends on, not a title or an impressive final line.

\subsection*{Decoding the statement}
The repository's statement of Seymour's conjecture asks, in a nonempty finite oriented graph, for a vertex whose second out-neighborhood has at least as many vertices as its first~\cite{oai173}. The official statement lives in a file of its own~\cite{oaistatement}, where the theorem is called \code{exists\_goodVertex}. Collecting its assumptions in one place, writing \code{->} and \code{Exists} for the file's $\to$ and $\exists$ as in Chapter~\ref{ch:lean2}, and putting \code{Type} where the file has \code{Type*} (a technical detail about the sizes of types that makes no difference here), we can display the statement as follows.
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem exists_goodVertex {V : Type}
    [Fintype V] [DecidableEq V] [Nonempty V]
    (r : V -> V -> Prop) (hr : IsOriented r) :
    Exists (fun v => GoodVertex r v) := by
  sorry
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\par
The repository builds on Mathlib (Chapter~\ref{ch:lean1}), which provides finite sets and ways of counting their members. Piece by piece:
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{V} is the type of vertices. \code{[Fintype V]} says that there are only finitely many vertices, and \code{[Nonempty V]} that there is at least one. \code{[DecidableEq V]}, that equality of vertices can be decided, is a technical requirement of Mathlib's finite sets that excludes no graph: with the standard axiom \code{Classical.choice} of Section~\ref{ch16:sec:axioms}, Lean can supply it for every type.
\item The edge relation \code{r} has type \code{V -> V -> Prop}, so \code{r v w} is the proposition $v\to w$, as in Section~\ref{ch16:sec:second}.
\item The hypothesis \code{(hr : IsOriented r)} says that \code{r} has no loops and no pair of opposite edges; the definition of \code{IsOriented} has exactly these two parts, named \code{loopless} and \code{asymmetric}.
\item The conclusion says that some vertex \code{v} satisfies \code{GoodVertex r v}, defined to mean that the set \code{firstNeighbors r v} has at most as many members as \code{secondNeighbors r v}. The first set consists of the out-neighbors of $v$, the vertices \code{w} with \code{r v w}. The second is defined by exactly the three conditions of Section~\ref{ch07:sec:seymour}: $w\ne v$, $v\to w$ fails, and $v\to u\to w$ for some vertex $u$.
\end{itemize}
No condition of the informal statement is missing, and decoding it needed nothing beyond Chapters~\ref{ch:graphs}, \ref{ch:lean1} and~\ref{ch:lean2}. The \code{sorry} at the end belongs there: this file only records what must be proved.

\subsection*{Locating the main steps}
The proof is in another file~\cite{oaimain}, which imports three further files, named \code{Reduction}, \code{Pruning} and \code{ExtremalContradiction}, and whose own proof is only a few lines long. It assumes that no vertex is good, so that the graph is a counterexample; uses a theorem from the reduction file to turn the counterexample into a new oriented graph with additional properties; and applies results from the other two files to reach a contradiction.

The reduction theorem~\cite{oaireduction} says that from any counterexample one can build, for some positive integer $n$, an oriented graph whose vertices are the pairs $(i,j)$ of natural numbers with $i<n$ and $j\le n$, and which has two further properties, named \code{PositiveIndegree} and \code{StrictSubsetGrowth}. The reduction file imports three more files, so the map keeps branching. The name \code{PositiveIndegree} suggests that every vertex has an incoming edge, but Lean accepts any name for any statement (Section~\ref{ch15:sec:symbols}): to understand these properties, you must read their definitions.

\subsection*{Keeping a reading record}
A \emph{reading record} is a written account of a proof you are studying: what you have read, what you have understood, and what remains open (Section~\ref{appE:sec:record} describes a finished one). So far, a reading record of the Seymour proof has entries of three kinds.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Understood:} the final statement has been decoded, and its definitions match the informal conjecture.
\item \emph{Borrowed:} the logical role of the reduction is clear, since it turns a counterexample into a graph with extra properties, but its proof has not been read.
\item \emph{Open:} the meaning of \code{PositiveIndegree} and \code{StrictSubsetGrowth}, and the mathematics of the pruning step and of the final contradiction.
\end{itemize}
A sketch like this one is a way into the proof, which uses ideas well beyond this course, not a replacement for it.

\subsection*{What the formal check compares}
A configuration file~\cite{oaiconfig} lists what the repository's formal check compares: the file containing the official statement, the file containing the proof, the name of the theorem, and the permitted axioms, which are \code{propext}, \code{Quot.sound} and \code{Classical.choice}, the three standard axioms of Section~\ref{ch16:sec:axioms}. The check is designed to confirm that the proof file proves exactly the official statement using no other axioms. It compares the statements, not just their names: a theorem in the proof file with the official name would, by itself, say nothing about whether the two statements agree.

The configuration file tells you what the check is designed to compare, not that it passed. Unless you run the check yourself, its passing is a claim made by the repository, and your reading record should attribute it: ``the repository reports that the check passes,'' not ``the check passes.'' Likewise, the record can describe the role of a file whose proofs you have not read, but cannot vouch for them; on your own authority it reports only what you decoded or reconstructed yourself.

\subsection*{The circulant Hadamard conjecture}
Section~\ref{ch14:sec:research} has already looked at which statements about circulant Hadamard matrices the repository formalized~\cite{oai179}, and you have proved a necessary condition yourself: by Proposition~\ref{thm:circulantnecessary}, a circulant Hadamard matrix of order greater than $4$ would need an order $4u^2$ with $u\ge2$, such as $16$, $36$ or $64$. That gives a precise next question: which lemma of the proof rules out these orders? A question informed by mathematics you have done yourself gets you much further than asking an assistant to ``explain the whole repository.''
