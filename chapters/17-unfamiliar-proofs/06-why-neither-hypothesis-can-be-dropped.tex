\section{Why neither hypothesis can be dropped}\label{ch17:sec:counterexamples}
As in Section~\ref{ch12:sec:hypotheses}, we remove one hypothesis at a time, keep the other, and look for an example in which the conclusion fails.

\subsection*{Without total boundedness}
Give $\NN$ the discrete metric of Section~\ref{ch17:sec:balls}. This space is not totally bounded, as we saw there, but it is complete. Indeed, for a Cauchy sequence in it, the tolerance $1/2$ gives a threshold $N$ such that any two terms from $x_N$ onward are less than $1/2$ apart. Distances here are only $0$ or $1$, so every term from $x_N$ onward equals $x_N$, and the sequence converges to $x_N$.

Yet the conclusion fails for the sequence $x_n=n$. In any subsequence the indices increase, so the terms are different numbers, any two of them at distance $1$. So no subsequence is a Cauchy sequence, since no threshold works for the tolerance $1/2$, and none converges (Proposition~\ref{ch11:prop:convergent-cauchy}). In the proof, Step~1 breaks down: no finite collection of balls of radius $1/4$ covers the space, so the selection cannot even begin.

\subsection*{Without completeness}
The interval $(0,1)$, with distance $|x-y|$, is totally bounded, although the centers $0$ and $1$ used for $[0,1]$ are not points of it; we use midpoints instead. Given $r>0$, the Archimedean property supplies a positive integer $N>1/(2r)$, so that $1/(2N)<r$. Split $[0,1]$ into the $N$ pieces $\bigl[\tfrac{j-1}N,\tfrac jN\bigr]$ for $j=1,\ldots,N$. Their midpoints $\tfrac{2j-1}{2N}$ lie in $(0,1)$. Every $x\in(0,1)$ lies in one of the pieces, and so is at distance at most half its length, $1/(2N)<r$, from that piece's midpoint.

But $(0,1)$ is not complete (Section~\ref{ch11:sec:complete}), and the conclusion fails for the sequence $x_n=1/(n+2)$, that is, $\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\tfrac14,\ldots$. On the real line it converges to $0$: given $\eps>0$, choose an integer $N>1/\eps$; then every $n\ge N$ gives $x_n=\tfrac1{n+2}<\tfrac1N<\eps$. Every subsequence also converges to $0$ on the real line: the indices increase by at least one at every step, starting from $n_1\ge0$, so induction gives $n_k\ge k-1$, and with $\eps$ and $N$ as before, every $k\ge N+1$ has $n_k\ge N$ and therefore $x_{n_k}<\eps$.

If some subsequence converged to a point $L$ of $(0,1)$, it would converge to $L$ on the real line too, since distances in $(0,1)$ are distances on the real line, and uniqueness of limits (Proposition~\ref{thm:limitunique}) would give $L=0\notin(0,1)$. So no subsequence converges in $(0,1)$. In the proof, Steps~1--4 go through, and Step~5 fails: the Cauchy subsequence exists, but the point it crowds around is missing from the space.

When you meet a new hypothesis, ask not only ``What does it mean?'' but also ``Which step fails without it?''
