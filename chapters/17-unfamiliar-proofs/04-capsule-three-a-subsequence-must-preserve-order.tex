\section{Capsule three: a subsequence must preserve order}\label{ch17:sec:subsequence}
\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Subsequence]\label{ch17:def:subsequence}
Let $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$ be a sequence. A \emph{subsequence} of it is a sequence of the form
\[
 x_{n_1},x_{n_2},x_{n_3},\ldots,
 \qquad\text{where }n_1<n_2<n_3<\cdots
\]
are natural numbers.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

In words: we may skip terms, but the terms we keep must stay in their original order, and no index may be used twice. For the sequence $x_n=(-1)^n$, which is $1,-1,1,-1,\ldots$, the indices $n_k=2k$ give the subsequence $x_2,x_4,x_6,\ldots$, which is the constant sequence $1,1,1,\ldots$. By contrast, $x_1,x_0,x_3,x_2,\ldots$ is not a subsequence, because the indices $1,0,3,2,\ldots$ do not increase. Neither is $x_0,x_0,x_0,\ldots$, because it uses the index $0$ again and again.

Two counters are involved, and it helps to keep them apart. The number $k$ counts positions in the subsequence, while $n_k$ is the position of the same term in the original sequence. In the example above, the third term of the subsequence, where $k=3$, is $x_6$. We number the terms of a subsequence from $k=1$, while the sequences of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} started at index $0$. This makes no difference: writing $y_k=x_{n_k}$, the definitions of convergence and of Cauchy sequences apply to $y_1,y_2,y_3,\ldots$ exactly as before, with thresholds that are at least $1$.

How can we make sure that the chosen indices increase? The following fact is all we need. \emph{An infinite set $S$ of natural numbers contains a number larger than any given natural number $M$.} Otherwise every member of $S$ would lie in the finite set $\set{0,1,\ldots,M}$, and $S$ would be finite. So if, at each stage of a construction, an infinite set of allowed indices is available, we can always choose an allowed index larger than the one chosen at the previous stage. This is what makes the chosen terms a subsequence, and not just an unordered collection of terms.

\subsection*{A plan with three kinds of objects}
The construction in the proof involves three different kinds of objects, and it is important not to mix them up: points of the space, such as the term $x_7$; balls, which are sets of points; and sets of indices, which are sets of natural numbers. A ball contains points. A set of indices contains numbers; in our construction it contains the number $7$ when the term $x_7$ lies in the chosen ball. If $x_7=x_9$, this is one point of the ball, but it contributes two indices, $7$ and $9$.

Here is the plan. Start with every index allowed: $S_0=\NN$. At stage~$1$, cover the space by finitely many balls of radius $1/4$, which total boundedness makes possible. By Lemma~\ref{ch17:lem:pigeonhole}, one of these balls contains $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n\in S_0$. Keep exactly those indices, and call the set of them $S_1$. By the diameter bound, any two terms $x_i$ and $x_j$ with $i,j\in S_1$ are less than $2\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac12$ apart. At stage~$2$, cover the space by finitely many balls of radius $1/8$, but now consider only the indices in $S_1$. One of the new balls contains $x_n$ for infinitely many $n\in S_1$; keep those indices, and call the set of them $S_2$. Any two terms with indices in $S_2$ are less than $2\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac14$ apart.

In general, stage~$k$ uses balls of radius $2^{-(k+1)}$, and any two terms with indices in $S_k$ are less than $2\cdot2^{-(k+1)}=2^{-k}$ apart. Why the radius $2^{-(k+1)}$ and not $2^{-k}$? The diameter bound doubles the radius, so starting from half of the desired distance gives the simple bound $2^{-k}$. Any radii that shrink towards zero would work; this choice keeps the arithmetic tidy.

Each set $S_k$ is a subset of $S_{k-1}$, because stage~$k$ only throws indices away and never adds new ones. So
\[
 S_0\supseteq S_1\supseteq S_2\supseteq\cdots,
\]
and we say that the index sets are \emph{nested}. The chosen balls, by contrast, need not be nested: the ball chosen at stage~$2$ may stick out of the ball chosen at stage~$1$. That does no harm. A term whose index lies in $S_2$ also has its index in $S_1$, so it lies in both chosen balls. In this way the nesting of the index sets carries the distance bound of each stage forward to all later stages. Section~\ref{ch17:sec:proof} makes this precise.
