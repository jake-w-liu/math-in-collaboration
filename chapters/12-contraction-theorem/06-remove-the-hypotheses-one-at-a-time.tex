\section{Remove the hypotheses one at a time}\label{ch12:sec:hypotheses}
Theorem~\ref{thm:banach} has four hypotheses: the space $X$ is nonempty, $X$ is complete, $T$ maps $X$ into itself, and $T$ satisfies the contraction inequality with a factor $q<1$. The proof used each of them at a definite place. A good way to understand why a hypothesis is there is to remove it, keep all the others, and look for an example in which the conclusion fails. This section does that for each hypothesis in turn.

\subsection*{Without completeness}
Let $T(x)=x/2$ on the open interval $X=(0,1)$. This $T$ is a self-map, because $0<x<1$ gives $0<x/2<1/2$, and it is a contraction with factor $1/2$, because $|x/2-y/2|=|x-y|/2$. But it has no fixed point in $X$: the equation $x/2=x$ forces $x=0$, and $0$ does not belong to $(0,1)$.

The proof breaks down at Step~3. Starting from $x_0=1/2$, the iterates are $x_n=2^{-(n+1)}$, that is, $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\ldots$. Steps~1 and~2 go through, and this is exactly the Cauchy sequence that Section~\ref{ch11:sec:complete} used to show that $(0,1)$ is not complete. Its only possible limit is $0$, which is missing from the space, so there is no point $x_*$ for Steps~4--6 to work with.

\subsection*{Without a factor below one}
Suppose we allow $q=1$, so that the inequality only says $d(Tx,Ty)\le d(x,y)$: the map never increases distances. On $\RR$, the identity map $T(x)=x$ satisfies this with equality, and every real number is a fixed point, so uniqueness fails. The map $T(x)=x+1$ also satisfies $|T(x)-T(y)|=|x-y|$, but the equation $x+1=x$ has no solution, so existence fails. So the value $q=1$ does not merely make convergence slower; it can destroy existence or uniqueness. In the proof, Steps~2, 5 and~6 all divide by $1-q$, which is now zero. In Step~5, for instance, the inequality $(1-q)\,d(x_*,y_*)\le0$ becomes $0\le0$ and says nothing.

Even a map that brings every pair of different points strictly closer together can fail, if the shrinking gets weaker and weaker. This is the example promised in Section~\ref{ch12:sec:iteration}. Let $X=[1,\infty)$, the set of real numbers $x\ge1$, and let
\[
T(x)=x+\frac1x.
\]
For $x\ge1$ we have $T(x)>x\ge1$, so $T$ maps $X$ into itself. The set $[1,\infty)$ is complete; Exercise~\ref{ex:12.4} asks you to check this by adapting the argument given for $[0,1]$ in Section~\ref{ch11:sec:complete}. To compare two outputs, use $\frac1x-\frac1y=\frac{y-x}{xy}=-\frac{x-y}{xy}$:
\[
T(x)-T(y)=(x-y)+\left(\frac1x-\frac1y\right)=(x-y)\left(1-\frac1{xy}\right).
\]
For $x,y\ge1$ we have $xy\ge1$, so the factor $1-1/(xy)$ lies between $0$ and $1$, with $1$ excluded. Hence $|T(x)-T(y)|<|x-y|$ whenever $x\ne y$. Yet no single factor $q<1$ works for all pairs. Given any $q$ with $0\le q<1$, choose an integer $k\ge1$ with $k>1/(1-q)$, which is possible by the Archimedean property. For $x=k+1$ and $y=k$ the ratio is $1-1/(k(k+1))$, and since $k(k+1)>k>1/(1-q)$, we get $1/(k(k+1))<1-q$, so the ratio is larger than $q$. And $T$ has no fixed point: $x+1/x=x$ would require $1/x=0$. Starting from $x_0=1$, the iterates $1,\,2,\,\tfrac52,\,\tfrac{29}{10},\,\ldots$ keep increasing. Their squares grow by more than $2$ at each step, because $(x+1/x)^2=x^2+2+1/x^2$, so $x_n^2\ge1+2n$ and the iterates grow without bound.

\subsection*{Without the self-map condition}
Let $T(x)=x/2+1$, used only for inputs in $[0,1]$. The interval $[0,1]$ is nonempty and complete, and the contraction inequality holds with factor $1/2$, since $|T(x)-T(y)|=|x-y|/2$. But $T(1)=3/2$ lies outside $[0,1]$, so $T$ is not a map from $[0,1]$ to itself. The only real solution of $x/2+1=x$ is $x=2$, which lies outside $[0,1]$, so $T$ has no fixed point in the interval. The proof breaks down already in Step~1: starting from $x_0=0$, we get $x_1=1$ and then $x_2=3/2$, which has left the space, and from then on the contraction inequality, which was only assumed for points of $[0,1]$, says nothing. A correct inequality cannot make up for outputs that leave the space.

\subsection*{Without nonemptiness}
The last hypothesis is needed for a simpler reason. If $X$ is the empty set, then there are no points at all. The requirements of completeness and of the contraction inequality then hold vacuously, in the sense of Section~\ref{ch02:sec:contrapositive}, because there is nothing to check. But there is no fixed point either, since there is no point. The proof uses nonemptiness exactly once, to choose a starting point.

\subsection*{Summary}
The table records where the proof uses each hypothesis and an example showing that it cannot be dropped.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}\toprule
Hypothesis & Used in the proof & Examples without it\\\midrule
$X$ is nonempty & Step~1, to choose $x_0$ & the empty set\\
$X$ is complete & Step~3 & $x/2$ on $(0,1)$\\
$T$ maps $X$ into $X$ & Step~1, to form the iterates & $x/2+1$ on $[0,1]$\\
factor $q<1$ & Steps~2, 5 and~6 & $x$ and $x+1$ on $\RR$\\
 & & $x+1/x$ on $[1,\infty)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
None of these examples contradicts the theorem, since each of them fails one of its hypotheses. Together they show that every hypothesis is doing real work.
