\section{From solving an equation to repeating a map}\label{ch12:sec:iteration}
Section~\ref{ch04:sec:unique} proved that the equation
\[
x=\frac{x+1}{2}
\]
has exactly one real solution, $x=1$. Section~\ref{ch05:sec:recurrence} reached the same number differently: starting from $x_0=0$ and applying the rule $x\mapsto(x+1)/2$ again and again gives
\[
0,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac78,\ \ldots,\qquad\text{in general }x_n=1-2^{-n},
\]
and Section~\ref{ch11:sec:convergence} proved that these numbers converge to $1$. So $1$ is both the solution of an equation and the limit of a repeated update. This chapter turns the second point of view into a general method for solving equations.

The general setting is a metric space $X$, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}, and a map $T:X\to X$ (a map is the same thing as a function; Section~\ref{ch03:sec:functions}). A \emph{fixed point} of $T$ is a point $x_*\in X$ with $T(x_*)=x_*$: a point that the map leaves where it is. Starting from any point $x_0\in X$, we can apply $T$ over and over: $x_1=T(x_0)$, $x_2=T(x_1)$, and in general $x_{n+1}=T(x_n)$ for every $n\in\NN$. The points $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$ are called the \emph{iterates} of $T$ starting from $x_0$. If they converge to a fixed point, then repeating the map solves the equation $T(x)=x$. Since in practice we stop after finitely many steps, we will also want to know how good the approximation is.

This simple method can fail in several ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item The iterates may never approach the fixed point. For $T(x)=1-x$ on $\RR$, the fixed point is $1/2$, but starting from $x_0=0$ the iterates are $0,1,0,1,\ldots$, each at distance $1/2$ from it.
\item There can be several fixed points, and then ``the solution'' has no clear meaning (Section~\ref{ch04:sec:unique}). For $T(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$, both $0$ and $1$ are fixed points.
\item The iterates can approach a point that is missing from the space. Take $T(x)=x/2$ on the interval $(0,1)$; an interval like this, which leaves out both of its endpoints, is called an \emph{open interval}. Starting from $x_0=1/2$, the iterates $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\ldots$ approach $0$, which does not belong to $(0,1)$, and in fact $T$ has no fixed point in $(0,1)$ at all.
\end{itemize}
We want conditions on $T$ and $X$ that rule out these failures in advance. The first condition says that $T$ brings points closer together. From now on we often write $Tx$ for $T(x)$ to save brackets.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Contraction]\label{ch12:def:contraction}
Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space, and let $q$ be a real number with $0\le q<1$. A map $T:X\to X$ is a \emph{contraction with factor $q$} if
\[
d(Tx,Ty)\le q\,d(x,y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in X.
\]
This inequality is called the \emph{contraction inequality}, and $q$ is called a \emph{contraction factor} of $T$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

In words: $Tx$ and $Ty$ are at most $q$ times as far apart as $x$ and $y$ were. A contraction with factor $q$ is also a contraction with any factor $q'$ satisfying $q\le q'<1$, since $q\,d(x,y)\le q'\,d(x,y)$ (distances are never negative). That is why we say ``a'' contraction factor rather than ``the.''

Two features of the definition matter. First, $x$ and $y$ are \emph{any} two points of $X$, not just consecutive iterates or points close to a fixed point. For $T(x)=(x+1)/2$ on $\RR$, with the usual distance $d(x,y)=|x-y|$,
\[
|T(x)-T(y)|=\left|\frac{x+1}{2}-\frac{y+1}{2}\right|
=\left|\frac{(x+1)-(y+1)}{2}\right|
=\frac12|x-y|,
\]
so this $T$ is a contraction with factor $1/2$. The constant terms cancel when two outputs are compared, as in the uniqueness proof of Section~\ref{ch04:sec:unique}. Second, one number $q$ must work for all pairs at once. It is not enough that every pair of different points moves closer together: Section~\ref{ch12:sec:hypotheses} gives a map that does this, with ratios $d(Tx,Ty)/d(x,y)$ coming closer and closer to $1$, and has no fixed point.

\subsection*{The outputs must be allowed inputs}
The second condition is built into the arrow of $T:X\to X$: every output $T(x)$ must again be a point of $X$. A map from a set to itself is called a \emph{self-map}. This is what allows us to apply $T$ to $x_1$, then to $x_2$, and so on. The condition is easy to overlook when a formula makes sense for more numbers than we intend to use; Section~\ref{ch12:sec:hypotheses} shows what goes wrong without it. For $T(x)=(x+1)/2$ on $[0,1]$ it holds: if $0\le x\le1$, then $1\le x+1\le2$, so $1/2\le T(x)\le1$. So before using a contraction, we check two separate things: the self-map condition, and the contraction inequality for all pairs of points of $X$.

\pauseq{Is $T(x)=x^2$ a contraction of the interval $[0,1]$? Is it a contraction of the interval $[0,1/3]$?}{On $[0,1]$ it is not: for $x=1$ and $y=1/2$ we get $|T(x)-T(y)|=\tfrac34>\tfrac12=|x-y|$, so no factor $q<1$ works for this pair. On $[0,1/3]$ it is. It is a self-map, since $0\le x\le1/3$ gives $0\le x^2\le1/9\le1/3$. Factoring the difference of two squares, $|x^2-y^2|=|x-y|\,|x+y|\le\tfrac23|x-y|$ for $x,y\in[0,1/3]$, because then $0\le x+y\le2/3$. So $q=2/3$ is a contraction factor.}
