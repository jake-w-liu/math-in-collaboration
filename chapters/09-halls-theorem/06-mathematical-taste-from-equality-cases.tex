\section{Mathematical taste from equality cases}\label{ch09:sec:taste}
The inductive proof uses the inequality $|N(S)|\ge|S|$ in two ways. When it holds with equality, the group $S$ is rigid: any full assignment must use up all of $N(S)$ on the students of $S$, and that told us where to split the problem. When it is strict, the sizes, being integers, differ by at least one, enough to absorb the loss of one task.

This rigidity is a general fact from Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}: an injective function between finite sets of the same size is also surjective. Here it is applied to the function from $S$ to $N(S)$ that sends each student of $S$ to the task the matching gives that student.

Whenever you meet an inequality, ask more than whether it can be improved: ask what happens when it holds with equality. If equality forces a recognizable structure, that structure may let you break the problem into smaller pieces. When the inequality is strict, ask how large the margin is and what it can pay for. You will meet a margin of this kind in Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction}: the contraction theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:banach}) requires a number $q$ with $0\le q<1$, and its error bounds divide by the gap $1-q$, so they make sense only because the gap is positive, and they are strong only when it is not too small.
