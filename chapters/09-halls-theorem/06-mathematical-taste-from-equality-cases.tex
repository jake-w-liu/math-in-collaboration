\section{Mathematical taste from equality cases}\label{ch09:sec:taste}
The inductive proof of Hall's theorem uses the inequality $|N(S)|\ge|S|$ in two different ways. When it holds with equality, the group $S$ is rigid: any full assignment must use up all of $N(S)$ on the students of $S$. That rigidity told us how to split the problem into two independent pieces. When the inequality is strict, the fact that sizes of sets are integers gives a margin of at least one, and that margin was enough to absorb the loss of one task. The proof comes from asking separately what each situation allows.

The rigidity in the equality case is a general fact from Section~\ref{ch03:sec:injsurj}: an injective function $f:A\to B$ between finite sets of the same size is also surjective, because its $|A|$ different outputs lie in $B$, which has only $|A|$ members, so they make up all of $B$. The tight-block argument is exactly this, with the students of $S$ as $A$, the tasks of $N(S)$ as $B$, and as $f$ the function that sends each student of $S$ to the task the matching gives that student.

Whenever you meet an inequality, ask more than whether it can be improved: ask what happens when it holds with equality. If equality forces a recognizable structure, that structure may let you break the problem into smaller pieces. And when the inequality is strict, ask how large the margin is and what it can pay for. You will meet a margin of this second kind in Chapter~\ref{ch:contraction}. The contraction theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:banach}) requires a number $q$ with $0\le q<1$. Its error bounds divide by the gap $1-q$, so they make sense only because the gap is positive, and they are strong only when the gap is not too small.
