\section{Why every part of the case split is needed}\label{ch09:sec:case-split}
Each condition in the case split of the inductive proof has a definite job. A good way to see the job is to drop the condition and watch the proof break.

\emph{Why must $S$ be nonempty?} Recall that the empty group is always tight. If Case~1 allowed $S=\varnothing$, then the first smaller problem would have no students, and the second would be the original problem all over again, because $L\setminus\varnothing=L$ and $R\setminus N(\varnothing)=R$. The induction hypothesis says nothing about a graph with $n$ students, so the proof would make no progress.

\emph{Why must $S$ be proper?} The whole class can be tight as well. In the four-student example of Section~\ref{ch09:sec:inductive}, the whole class accepts exactly the four tasks $1,2,3,4$. If Case~1 allowed $S=L$, the first of the two smaller problems would again contain all $n$ students. It would be the original problem with only the tasks that nobody accepts thrown away, and the induction hypothesis could not be applied to it.

\emph{Why must $S$ be tight?} Case~1 removes every task in $N(S)$ from the second problem. If $S$ had spare tasks, this could throw away tasks that other students need. Take $N(a)=\{1,2\}$ and $N(b)=\{2\}$, and let $S=\{a\}$. This group is nonempty and proper, but it is not tight, since $|N(S)|=2>1$. Removing $N(S)=\{1,2\}$ leaves $b$ with no acceptable task, although $a$--$1$, $b$--$2$ is a full assignment. In the proof, tightness is used at exactly one point: we substitute $|N(S)|=|S|$ and subtract $|S|$ from both sides.

\pauseq{In Case~1, why do we remove every task in $N(S)$, rather than only the tasks that the matching $M_1$ actually gives to the students in $S$?}{Because $S$ is tight, these are the same tasks: $M_1$ gives the $|S|$ students of $S$ different tasks inside $N(S)$, which has only $|S|$ members, so it uses them all. Describing the removed tasks as $N(S)$ has one advantage: we can name them before $M_1$ has been found, so the two smaller problems can be set up and solved independently.}

\emph{Why does Case~2 need the extra unit?} Giving $x$ the task $y$ can cost each remaining group one of its acceptable tasks. The inequality $|N(T)|\ge|T|+1$ is exactly enough to pay for that loss. Without it, an arbitrary first choice can be fatal. Recall the example from Section~\ref{ch08:sec:model} in which Ada accepts tasks $1$ and $2$ and Ben accepts only task~$1$. If we give Ada task~$1$, Ben is left with nothing, although Ada--$2$, Ben--$1$ is a full assignment. The proof never makes this mistake. The group $\{\text{Ben}\}$ is nonempty, proper and tight, so this graph belongs to Case~1, not Case~2. Case~1 first serves the tight block $\{\text{Ben}\}$ with task~$1$, and then Ada takes task~$2$.

\subsection*{Applying a theorem means checking all of its hypotheses}
Suppose an assistant proposes the proof ``split the students into a group and the rest, and apply induction to each part.'' That is not yet a proof of the theorem. The induction hypothesis is not the statement ``smaller graphs have matchings that saturate their left parts.'' That statement is false: the graph with $N(a)=N(b)=\{1\}$ has only two students and no such matching. What it actually says is that every finite bipartite graph with fewer than $n$ students \emph{that satisfies Hall's condition} has such a matching. So a proof must show that both smaller problems satisfy Hall's condition. This check is the heart of the proof, and the example under ``Why must $S$ be tight?'' shows that it fails for a badly chosen group.

Each time the proof applies the induction hypothesis, it must supply three things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a finite bipartite graph, with its left part and right part named;
\item a check that its left part has fewer than $n$ students;
\item a check that it satisfies Hall's condition, that is, an inequality for every subset of its left part.
\end{enumerate}
In Case~1 the proof applies the hypothesis to the two graphs $G_1$ and $G_2$, and in Case~2 to the graph $G'$. The first two items are easy. Deleting some vertices of a finite bipartite graph, together with the edges at them, leaves a finite bipartite graph, and each smaller graph has lost at least one student. The third item is where the work lies, because deleting tasks can destroy Hall's condition. The proof supplies it in two steps. For each group of students in a smaller graph, it first works out exactly which tasks are joined to that group there: $N(U)$ in $G_1$, $N'(T)=N(T)\setminus N(S)$ in $G_2$, and $N(T)\setminus\{y\}$ in $G'$. Then it shows that this set has at least as many members as the group.

Section~\ref{ch02:sec:implication} made the same point about a single implication: a result applies only after its hypothesis has been checked. When a proof says ``by the induction hypothesis'' or ``by the theorem,'' write out the hypotheses and check each one for the object at hand.
