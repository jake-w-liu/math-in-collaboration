\section{Why every part of the case split is needed}\label{ch09:sec:case-split}
Each condition in the case split of the inductive proof has a job, and the way to see it is to drop the condition and watch the proof break.

\emph{Why must $S$ be nonempty?} The empty group is always tight. If Case~1 allowed $S=\varnothing$, the first smaller problem would have no students, and the second would be the original problem again, because $L\setminus\varnothing=L$ and $R\setminus N(\varnothing)=R$. The induction hypothesis says nothing about a graph with $n$ students, so the proof would make no progress.

\emph{Why must $S$ be proper?} The whole class can be tight too: in the four-student example of Section~\ref{ch09:sec:inductive}, it accepts exactly the four tasks $1,2,3,4$. If Case~1 allowed $S=L$, the first smaller problem would again contain all $n$ students, and the induction hypothesis could not be applied to it.

\emph{Why must $S$ be tight?} Case~1 removes every task in $N(S)$ from the second problem. If $S$ had spare tasks, this could throw away tasks that other students need. Take $N(a)=\{1,2\}$ and $N(b)=\{2\}$, and let $S=\{a\}$, which is nonempty and proper but not tight, since $|N(S)|=2>1$. Removing $N(S)=\{1,2\}$ leaves $b$ with no acceptable task, although $a$--$1$, $b$--$2$ is a full assignment.

\pauseq{In Case~1, why do we remove every task in $N(S)$, rather than only the tasks that the matching $M_1$ actually gives to the students in $S$?}{Because $S$ is tight, these are the same tasks: $M_1$ gives the $|S|$ students of $S$ different tasks inside $N(S)$, which has only $|S|$ members, so it uses them all. Describing them as $N(S)$ lets us name them before $M_1$ has been found, so the two smaller problems can be set up and solved independently.}

\emph{Why does Case~2 need the extra unit?} Giving $x$ the task $y$ can cost each remaining group one of its acceptable tasks, and the inequality $|N(T)|\ge|T|+1$ is exactly enough to pay for that loss. Without it, an arbitrary first choice can be fatal. In the Ada--Ben example of Section~\ref{ch08:sec:model}, Ada accepts tasks $1$ and $2$ and Ben accepts only task~$1$. Giving Ada task~$1$ leaves Ben with nothing, although Ada--$2$, Ben--$1$ is a full assignment. The proof never makes this mistake: the group $\{\text{Ben}\}$ is nonempty, proper and tight, so this graph belongs to Case~1, which first serves the tight block $\{\text{Ben}\}$ with task~$1$ and then gives Ada task~$2$.

\subsection*{Applying a theorem means checking all of its hypotheses}
Suppose an assistant proposes the proof ``split the students into a group and the rest, and apply induction to each part.'' That is not yet a proof. The induction hypothesis does not say that smaller graphs have matchings that saturate their left parts, which is false: the graph with $N(a)=N(b)=\{1\}$ has only two students and no such matching. It says this only for graphs \emph{that satisfy Hall's condition}. So a proof must show that both smaller problems satisfy Hall's condition, and the example under ``Why must $S$ be tight?'' shows that this can fail for a badly chosen group.

Each time the proof applies the induction hypothesis, it must supply three things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a finite bipartite graph, with its left part and right part named;
\item a check that its left part has fewer than $n$ students;
\item a check that it satisfies Hall's condition, that is, an inequality for every subset of its left part.
\end{enumerate}
The proof supplies all three for $G_1$ and $G_2$ in Case~1 and for $G'$ in Case~2. The third item is where the work lies, because deleting tasks can destroy Hall's condition: for each group of students in a smaller graph, the proof first works out exactly which tasks are joined to it there ($N(U)$, $N'(T)$ or $N(T)\setminus\{y\}$), and then counts them. In general, when a proof says ``by the induction hypothesis'' or ``by the theorem,'' write out the hypotheses and check each one for the object at hand (compare Section~\ref{ch02:sec:implication}).
