\section{A second proof: equality tells us where to split}\label{ch09:sec:inductive}
A second proof, by strong induction, needs no search at all. It brings out an idea that is useful far beyond matchings: pay special attention to the cases in which an inequality holds with equality. Throughout this section, $G$ is a finite bipartite graph with parts $L$ and $R$.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{definition}[Tight group]\label{ch09:def:tight}
A set $S\subseteq L$ of students is \emph{tight} if $|N(S)|=|S|$.
\end{definition}
\end{minipage}\par

Under Hall's condition, a tight group is one with no spare tasks. The empty group is always tight, because $|N(\varnothing)|=0=|\varnothing|$. In the graph of Section~\ref{sec:matching-trace}, where $N(a)=\{1,2\}$, $N(b)=\{1\}$ and $N(c)=\{2,3\}$, the groups $\{b\}$, $\{a,b\}$ and $\{a,b,c\}$ are tight, since they accept exactly the tasks $\{1\}$, $\{1,2\}$ and $\{1,2,3\}$, as many as they have members. The group $\{c\}$ is not tight, because $c$ accepts two tasks.

Why do tight groups matter? Suppose that $S$ is tight and that a full assignment exists. The students of $S$ receive $|S|$ different tasks, all in $N(S)$, which has only $|S|$ members. So they use up every task in $N(S)$, and all the other students must be served by tasks outside $N(S)$. A tight group is therefore a self-contained block, and this suggests splitting the problem in two: the students of $S$ with the tasks in $N(S)$, and everyone else with the remaining tasks.

For an induction, both pieces must be smaller than the original problem. A subset $S$ of $L$ is called \emph{proper} if $S\ne L$, and we then write $S\subsetneq L$. If $S$ is nonempty and proper, then $|S|<|L|$, because some student of $L$ lies outside $S$, and $|L\setminus S|=|L|-|S|<|L|$, because $|S|>0$.

\noindent\textit{Proof plan.} Use strong induction on the number of students. If some nonempty proper group $S$ is tight, split the problem as just described. If no nonempty proper group is tight, every such group has at least one spare task. This spare capacity makes it safe to give any one student any task that student accepts, and we show that the smaller problem that remains still satisfies Hall's condition.

\subsection*{The two cases in small examples}
Here is the first case of the plan at work. Four students have the options
\[
N(a)=N(b)=\{1,2\},\qquad N(c)=\{2,3\},\qquad N(d)=\{3,4\}.
\]
In the drawing, the dashed box surrounds a tight block.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.15in,y=0.45in,every node/.style={font=\small}]
\foreach \y/\name in {0/a,1/b,2/c,3/d}
 {\node[circle,draw=ink,minimum size=6mm,inner sep=1pt] (s\name) at (0,-\y) {$\name$};}
\foreach \y/\name in {0/1,1/2,2/3,3/4}
 {\node[circle,draw=ink,minimum size=6mm,inner sep=1pt] (t\name) at (1,-\y) {$\name$};}
\draw[ink] (sa)--(t1) (sa)--(t2) (sb)--(t1) (sb)--(t2) (sc)--(t2) (sc)--(t3) (sd)--(t3) (sd)--(t4);
\draw[accent,dashed,thick,rounded corners] (-0.17,0.38) rectangle (1.17,-1.38);
\node[text=accent,anchor=west,align=left] at (1.25,-0.5) {tight block:\\$S=\{a,b\}$, $N(S)=\{1,2\}$};
\node at (0,-3.8) {students};
\node at (1,-3.8) {tasks};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
This graph satisfies Hall's condition, as you can confirm by checking all $2^4=16$ inequalities of \eqref{eq:hall}. The group $S=\{a,b\}$ is tight, so any full assignment must give $a$ and $b$ the tasks $1$ and $2$ in some order. So we set $a$ and $b$ aside, together with tasks $1$ and~$2$. Of the remaining tasks, $c$ accepts only task~$3$, and $d$ accepts tasks $3$ and~$4$, so $c$--$3$ and $d$--$4$, together with $a$--$1$ and $b$--$2$, give a full assignment.

For a group $T$ of remaining students, write
\[
N'(T)=N(T)\setminus N(S)
\]
for the set of tasks that someone in $T$ still accepts once the tasks of $N(S)$ are removed (the mark $'$, read ``prime'', shows that $N'(T)$ belongs to the smaller problem). For $T=\{c\}$, the set $N(T)=\{2,3\}$ loses task~$2$, and $N'(T)=\{3\}$. Why must some task of $c$ survive? Not because of Hall's condition for $T$ alone: $|N(c)|\ge1$ would still hold if $c$ accepted only task~$2$, leaving $c$ nothing. What helps is Hall's condition for the combined group $S\cup T=\{a,b,c\}$: it accepts at least three tasks, and only two of them can lie in $N(S)=\{1,2\}$, so it accepts some task outside $N(S)$. No student of $S$ accepts that task, so $c$ does. The general proof shows $|N'(T)|\ge|T|$ for every group $T$ of remaining students in the same way.

Here is the second case of the plan. Let $N(a)=\{1,2\}$ and $N(b)=\{2,3\}$. The nonempty proper groups $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ each accept one task more than they need, so neither is tight. The plan lets us give $a$ any task it accepts, even task~$2$, which $b$ also wants: then $b$ still has task~$3$, and $\{a\text{--}2,\ b\text{--}3\}$ is a full assignment. The spare task of the group $\{b\}$ absorbs the loss of task~$2$.

\subsection*{The inductive proof}
We prove the sufficient direction of Theorem~\ref{thm:hall} by strong induction on the number of students (Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular}). For each natural number $n$, let $P(n)$ be the statement:
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.88\linewidth}
\emph{Every finite bipartite graph whose left part has exactly $n$ vertices, and which satisfies Hall's condition, has a matching that saturates its left part.}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
The statement places no restriction on the right part, because the smaller graphs to which we apply the induction hypothesis lose tasks as well as students. In any graph, Hall's condition is computed with that graph's own edges: every set $U$ of vertices in its left part must be joined to at least $|U|$ vertices of its right part. In a smaller graph some edges are gone, so the tasks joined to $U$ there may be fewer than those in $N(U)$.

\begin{proof}[Inductive proof that Hall's condition is sufficient]
\emph{Base case, $n=0$.} If $L$ is empty, the empty matching saturates $L$, because there is no student to cover. So $P(0)$ holds.

\emph{Induction step.} Let $n\ge1$, and assume the induction hypothesis: $P(m)$ holds for every natural number $m<n$. (Here $n$ plays the role of $k+1$ in the strong induction step of Section~\ref{ch05:sec:not-circular}.) Let $G$ be a finite bipartite graph with parts $L$ and $R$, where $|L|=n$, and suppose that $G$ satisfies \eqref{eq:hall}. We must find a matching of $G$ that saturates $L$. There are two cases.

\textbf{Case 1: some nonempty proper subset of $L$ is tight.} Choose a set $S$ with $\varnothing\ne S\subsetneq L$ and $|N(S)|=|S|$.

\emph{The students in $S$.} Let $G_1$ be the graph with left part $S$, right part $N(S)$, and as edges all the edges of $G$ that join a student of $S$ to a task of $N(S)$. Every task that a student of $S$ accepts lies in $N(S)$, so $G_1$ keeps every edge of $G$ at the students of $S$. Hence, for every $U\subseteq S$, the set of tasks joined to $U$ in $G_1$ is exactly $N(U)$, and since $U\subseteq L$, \eqref{eq:hall} for $G$ gives $|N(U)|\ge|U|$. So $G_1$ satisfies Hall's condition. Since $0<|S|<n$, the induction hypothesis $P(|S|)$ gives a matching $M_1$ of $G_1$ that saturates $S$.

\emph{The other students.} Let $G_2$ be the graph with left part $L\setminus S$, right part $R\setminus N(S)$, and as edges all the edges of $G$ that join a student of $L\setminus S$ to a task of $R\setminus N(S)$. Let $T$ be any subset of $L\setminus S$. A task is joined to $T$ in $G_2$ exactly when someone in $T$ accepts it and it does not lie in $N(S)$. So the set of tasks joined to $T$ in $G_2$ is $N'(T)=N(T)\setminus N(S)$.

We show that $|N'(T)|\ge|T|$. The sets $S$ and $T$ are disjoint, because $T\subseteq L\setminus S$, so $|S\cup T|=|S|+|T|$. A task is acceptable to someone in $S\cup T$ exactly when it is acceptable to someone in $S$ or to someone in $T$, so $N(S\cup T)=N(S)\cup N(T)$. Applying \eqref{eq:hall} to $S\cup T$ therefore gives
\[
|N(S)\cup N(T)|\ge |S|+|T|.
\]
Every member of $N(S)\cup N(T)$ lies in exactly one of the two disjoint sets $N(S)$ and $N'(T)=N(T)\setminus N(S)$, so
\[
|N(S)\cup N(T)|=|N(S)|+|N'(T)|
\]
(Exercise~\ref{ex:9.3} asks you to check this carefully). Combining the last two displays,
\[
|N(S)|+|N'(T)|\ge |S|+|T|.
\]
Now we use tightness. Substitute $|N(S)|=|S|$ and subtract $|S|$ from both sides to obtain $|N'(T)|\ge|T|$. This holds for every $T\subseteq L\setminus S$, including $T=\varnothing$. So $G_2$ satisfies Hall's condition. Since $S$ is nonempty, $G_2$ has $n-|S|<n$ students, and the induction hypothesis $P(n-|S|)$ gives a matching $M_2$ of $G_2$ that saturates $L\setminus S$.

\emph{Combining the two matchings.} The edges of $G_1$ and of $G_2$ are edges of $G$. Every edge of $M_1$ joins a student of $S$ to a task of $N(S)$, and every edge of $M_2$ joins a student of $L\setminus S$ to a task of $R\setminus N(S)$, so no vertex lies both on an edge of $M_1$ and on an edge of $M_2$. Within each of the two matchings, no two edges share an endpoint either. Hence $M_1\cup M_2$ is a matching of $G$. Every student of $L$ lies in $S$ or in $L\setminus S$, so it is matched by $M_1$ or by $M_2$. Thus $M_1\cup M_2$ saturates $L$.

\textbf{Case 2: no nonempty proper subset of $L$ is tight.} Let $S$ be a nonempty proper subset of $L$. Condition~\eqref{eq:hall} gives $|N(S)|\ge|S|$, and $|N(S)|\ne|S|$ because $S$ is not tight, so $|N(S)|>|S|$. Both sides are integers, and an integer larger than the integer $|S|$ is at least $|S|+1$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:9.4}). Therefore
\[
|N(S)|\ge |S|+1\qquad\text{for every set $S$ with }\varnothing\ne S\subsetneq L.
\]
Since $n\ge1$, we can choose a student $x\in L$; any student will do. Condition~\eqref{eq:hall} for the group $\{x\}$ gives $|N(x)|\ge1$, so $x$ accepts at least one task $y$. Let $G'$ be the graph obtained from $G$ by deleting the student $x$, the task $y$, and every edge at $x$ or at $y$. Its left part $L\setminus\{x\}$ has $n-1$ students, and its right part is $R\setminus\{y\}$.

We check Hall's condition for $G'$. Let $T\subseteq L\setminus\{x\}$. If $T=\varnothing$, the condition $0\ge0$ holds. Otherwise, note that $G'$ has lost only the edges of $G$ at $x$ and at $y$, and the edges at $x$ do not touch $T$, because $x\notin T$. So a task is joined to $T$ in $G'$ exactly when it lies in $N(T)$ and is not $y$; the set of these tasks is $N(T)\setminus\{y\}$. The set $T$ is nonempty, and it is a proper subset of $L$ because it does not contain $x$. So $|N(T)|\ge|T|+1$. Removing the single task $y$ removes at most one member of $N(T)$, so
\[
|N(T)\setminus\{y\}|\ge |N(T)|-1\ge |T|.
\]
Thus $G'$ satisfies Hall's condition, and the induction hypothesis $P(n-1)$ gives a matching $M'$ of $G'$ that saturates $L\setminus\{x\}$.

The edges of $M'$ are edges of $G$, and so is $x$--$y$. This edge shares no endpoint with any edge of $M'$, because $x$ and $y$ are not vertices of $G'$. So $M'\cup\{x\text{--}y\}$ is a matching of $G$, and it matches $x$ and every student of $L\setminus\{x\}$, so it saturates $L$.

When $n=1$, the set $L=\{x\}$ has no nonempty proper subset, so Case~2 applies, $M'$ is the empty matching given by $P(0)$, and the single edge $x$--$y$ saturates $L$.

In both cases $G$ has a matching that saturates $L$, so $P(n)$ holds. By strong induction, $P(n)$ holds for every natural number $n$, and this proves the sufficient direction of Hall's theorem.
\end{proof}
